# World: The Well-Bond

A fen country of chalk wells, of water that is never free, and of a rule so old that nobody remembers agreeing to it: a bond spoken at a wellhead, witnessed, binds the water of that well to the speaker until the debt is paid, and a thing that lives inside a bond is a person. That is the whole of the frog in this version. He is not a curse and he is not a prince. He is a lien. Somebody in Coldwick-under-Fen said *I will owe you* at the third well one night in a bad summer, and the well took it, and what the well sends up afterwards to collect is a green thing with a bad cough and a very good memory. The kiss in the usual telling is the oldest transfer there is — it moves a bond without a word being spoken, and the whole trouble of this country is that a bond can be transferred silently, and a parish will not know until the water is gone.

## Setting

Coldwick-under-Fen is a scatter of nine villages on a chalk fen, raised four feet above the water table on every one of them, which is why the wells go down sixty feet and why nothing here is ever dry and never is fresh. The land is a milk-white chalk and green sedge cut for peat, under a sky with no horizon in it in any direction. Water is the only currency and the only tax and the only thing anybody argues about. Every well has a stone collar, a windlass or a pump, a bucket-rail, and a name; the names are the old ones — the Third, the Cold One, the Blind, the Bent, the Long — and the names are not decoration, because a well's name is its standing in the fen and a well cannot be renamed after it has been bonded.

The parish of Coldwick has nine wells and about four hundred people. The law about bonds is unwritten, enforced by about six people who are extremely dangerous, and administered by the Well Court, which meets in a tithe barn on the first Thursday and which anybody may attend. The fen trade is eel, fenland celery, cow parsley, thatch, and the hauling of other people's water. Money comes in at the sluice in August and goes out by March. Everyone can hear a pumped well from a quarter mile in still air, and the country is loud in a way that has nothing to do with voices: the sound of a country that is constantly drawing water out of itself.

## The Magic

1. **A bond is spoken once at a wellhead and witnessed, and it cannot be withdrawn.** It binds the speaker's draw from that well for as long as it is unpaid. A person may bond what they have, and only what they have, and the well is the judge of what they have.
2. **A bond transfers only by the bonded person, freely, to somebody who touches the collar with both hands.** No third party can move it, no court can move it, and a transfer made under fear is a transfer that does not hold.
3. **Whatever lives inside a bond is a person.** It will have a name, a temper, a cough, and an account. It cannot be drowned, trapped, or disposed of, and it will ask you, in the end, what you are going to do about it.
4. **The debt is paid in use, not in coin.** A bond is discharged by drawing and applying, not by paying. A farmer who bonds a well for a crop discharges it by growing the crop, and if the crop fails he has not paid and the well knows.
5. **A lien is visible.** The amount outstanding on a bonded person can be read off the surface of any water they have stood in: a dull ring, a flattening, a scum line, a colder patch. People in Coldwick can read a debt off a puddle, and children do it without being taught.
6. **Discarding the bonded thing is a breach, and the breach falls on the whole parish.** The well goes to salt. This is not a penalty anybody imposes. It is what a well does when a bond is thrown away, and no well has ever recovered.
7. **A well cannot be sealed, only bonded.** To close a well you must find a person willing to hold it, in their own name, forever, and almost nobody ever does.
8. **The kiss is a transfer.** It is the oldest and quietest of them, and it moves a bond without either party speaking. It cannot be refused by a person who has not already said no out loud and in advance, which is why nobody ever thinks to.
9. **A bond may be discharged by naming the thing owed, out loud, at the wellhead, and being wrong about it.** The well accepts a false discharge and takes the fine instead. A person who says "I owe you nothing" at the collar of a bonded well pays the whole amount twice over, in water, in one night.
10. **Water drawn under a disputed bond appears in the pails at the Thursday.** The well cannot be blamed and the defendant cannot be hidden, because the pails are the evidence and the pails come from nine villages.

## Cast

**Bess Brack**, 16, a water-carrier who takes four pails a day up the Fen Track for three households and is the only person in Coldwick who has been inside the Bent well. *Want:* to be let to carry water for the well itself. *Flaw:* she reads a lien off water better than anyone alive and cannot stop doing it, and has twice named a debt in front of the wrong person. *Detail:* hands permanently blue at the nails, and a habit of tilting her head to look at a surface before she looks at a face. *Speech habit:* describes everything she sees as a measurement — "that's a fortnight, that is" — and is right more often than she is comfortable with.

**Teb Furroway**, 44, the fen's only well-wright, a heavy, slow, courteous man who has dug and lined eleven wells and will not dig a twelfth until the eleventh is sound. *Want:* to be told that a thing he built has failed, by somebody who was there. *Flaw:* he has lined the Bent well twice and refuses to say why the first lining went, and the reason is that he was paid to make it go. *Detail:* a chalk-dust handshake he never washes off, and a habit of putting his thumb in a joint to test a seal. *Speech habit:* answers with a depth, always — "down to the marl, that is" — and will not speculate above ground.

**Hester Colm**, 38, a woman who was bonded to the Cold One in 1988 and has been paying in use ever since, which means she has spent thirty years keeping other people's fields. *Want:* to be discharged with her own name said out loud at the collar. *Flaw:* she has organised her entire life around not being discharged, and when the offer comes she cannot say no fast enough. *Detail:* a chalk-white line at the wrist where the water has been, and a habit of washing her hands even when they are clean. *Speech habit:* says "that is not mine to say" and then says it.

**Micken Vaunt**, 11, a boy who lives at the third well and has done since he was found there, and who is therefore believed by nobody to be a boy. *Want:* to be named in the parish book under a name of his own choosing. *Flaw:* he has had eleven names from eleven different households and has never once been asked which one he uses, and has begun giving them all. *Detail:* welts on both ankles, old and new, and a habit of sleeping in the water because he says the dry is too loud. *Speech habit:* answers to any name given, immediately, and cannot be talked out of it.

**Nell Drury**, 66, the clerk of the Well Court, forty years of Thursdays, holder of the only complete record of the fen's bonds since 1841. *Want:* to be asked to burn one page. *Flaw:* she has never burnt one, and she has three that ought to be burnt, and she knows which three, and she has never told anybody which. *Detail:* a ledger too heavy to lift one-handed, and a habit of writing with the book closed. *Speech habit:* reads the entry back exactly as written, including the spelling, and will not paraphrase a bond for anybody.

**Gideon Sparre**, 29, a bailiff's clerk from the sluice who has come to enforce a parish debt of eleven hundred years' accumulation and has discovered that the parish's bonds are held, in aggregate, by people who are all standing in the room. *Want:* to be told he may go home. *Flaw:* he has never once in his life been the person with the least right to be present, and he is about to be. *Detail:* good boots, wrong for the fen, and a habit of checking the latch on a door behind him. *Speech habit:* says "strictly" and "without prejudice" and has begun to hear how funny they are.

## Places

**The Third Well.** The oldest in the fen and the one the parish believes in: a chalk collar worn round, a windlass gone to rust, a bucket on a rope, and a name board so weathered it reads *The Thred*. Micken's well. It is the one the bonded thing was taken from.

**The Bent Well.** Nine hundred yards north, in ground that gives, a well that leans. It was lined twice. The first lining came out in a single night in 1994 and the reason has never been written down and Teb Furroway has never spoken of it. It is the coldest water in the fen and the best.

**The Tithe Barn.** Thatched, chalk-flagged, four hundred years old, the Well Court on the first Thursday. Two long tables, a stove in the middle, and forty to ninety people in coats. The stove is the only warm thing within two miles and it is the reason anybody comes.

**The Sluice House.** The August lock, the parish's money, a red-brick engine house by the drain with a sluice lever, a wage book, and a rate book going back to 1841. This is where the figures live and where the argument about the fen's total obligation is actually conducted, badly, on Thursdays.

**The Fen Track.** Four miles of raised causeway between Coldwick and Sallow, with a water-cut on either side and a drop of two feet into the drainage. It is the only dry walking in the parish, it is the whole parish's footpath, and the pails go up it four times a day.

**The Salt Pits.** The west end, where the breach happened in 1911: a spread of grey-white shallow water in old chalk workings, dead fish, reeds, and a sign. Nothing has grown there since and it is a hundred yards from the school and nobody can explain why the children are not allowed to go and play.

## Visual Style

A palette of chalk white, sedge green, and standing-water grey, lit by a huge flat northern sky at low angles so that every pail, rope, and wet surface throws a long cold highlight. The light is enormous and shadowless, the light of a country with nothing in it. Materials: wet chalk, tarred rope, cold iron, duckboard, eel traps, black silt, frost on a bucket rim, wool, oiled leather, and the inside of a well. The era reads as fenland, 1890s-1910s: windmill, sluice, hand pumps, a boy, a bicycle, no electricity, no armour, nothing shining but water. The recurring motif is the collar — the ring of dressed stone at the top of a well, worn round, with two hands' worth of it polished by everybody who has ever transferred anything.

## Motifs

1. **The collar** — dressed chalk blocks at the lip of a well, worn round and bright in a horseshoe where four hundred years of hands have gone down.
2. **The lien in the water** — a dull ring, a colder patch, a scum line on a standing surface, exactly readable, never ambiguous.
3. **Four pails on a shoulder-rail**, the shape everybody in Coldwick walks like.
4. **The name board on a well**, weathered past legibility, which is why a well cannot be renamed.
5. **The welt on an ankle** — the record of a thing that has been kept too long in too cold water.

## Thematic Spine

All nine stories are about a debt that cannot be argued with, only carried, and about what a community does when the carrying starts to fail. The accessible band treats it as water: a pail, a collar, a boy in a well. The intermediate band treats it as obligation: an audit, a discharge, a payment nobody can make. The advanced band treats it as structure: an evidence standard, a cross-holding, a settlement, and the price of a clean settlement.
