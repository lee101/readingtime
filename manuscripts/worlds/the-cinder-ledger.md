# World: The Cinder Ledger

Emberwyke is a river city of eleven thousand hearths in which fire is not a thing people have, it is a thing people are lent, and the price of it is ash. Every fire in the city is licensed at the Scale House, where the ash it makes is weighed on a beam and entered against the hearth that made it, and a hearth with more ash than it has spent is a hearth in credit, and a hearth with less is a hearth in deficit, and a hearth in deficit has its fire out-weighed at dusk and its name written into the Cinder Ledger with a mark beside it, and a marked name cannot testify at any hearing for seven years. The story everyone knows is the one about the girl and the slipper, and in Emberwyke it is remembered the way a fire is remembered afterwards: as the last warm thing, and slightly wrong. What actually happened is that a balance was struck, and a balance struck in this city is a legal instrument, and the slipper was not glass. It was a sand-cast proof, made at a specific weighing, for a specific purpose, and the girl did not lose it. She put it down, deliberately, in front of a witness, and then she did the hardest thing available to a person with nothing, which was to leave the room and let somebody else pick it up.

## Setting

Emberwyke stands where the River Corr descends out of the chalk downs, on a flat flood plain of black silt, brick and tar and standing water, with the Scale House on the only stone in the city and everything else built out of what the river brings. The streets are named for what burns in them: Cinder Row, the Rope Walk, Tanner's Gate, the Coalquay, Sallow Bridge, Ember Lane. Hearths are chimneys: every house is a chimney with rooms round the bottom, and every chimney carries a number, and the number is cast into the brick at the third course, and the number is the only thing the city knows a person by. In winter the plain is a field of low orange points under a brown sky, and at dusk the Scale House opens and every hearth in the city sends somebody to stand in the queue, because a hearth that does not send anybody is a hearth that has stopped.

The fire is coal from the downs, coke, oil, and — in the poorer quarters, and in the winter of a bad year — whatever can be burned. That last category has a name, and the name is the subject of the city's central legal fight: fuel-grade matter, weighed separately, at a discount, marked. The smell of Emberwyke in winter is coal smoke, wet silt, tallow, boiled cabbage, and the particular hot dry dust that comes off a hearth board when it is emptied. In summer the river smell wins and the city smells like a sewer that someone has been doing their best with.

The trade of the city is a trade in heat. Hearth-masters keep the boards; the ash-runners go up at dusk with their barrows; the Scale House weighs and the clerks write. The glass is made in Sallow Bridge, and the sand is dug at Cold Ash, three miles downriver, and the two businesses are on opposite banks of the water and do not speak. The one great public room is the Salt Hall, a long cold chamber off Sallow Bridge where the lighting-up of the winter is settled and where, by an arrangement nobody can date, no fire has ever been lit.

## The Magic

1. **Fire is licensed, and an unlicensed fire will not light.** This is not law; it is what coal does. A hearth whose number is not entered on the roll for the season will take a match, hold it, and go out. There is no point at which it can be hurried.
2. **Ash is weighed and never estimated.** Every hearth keeps a board: a slate ruled in nights, with a figure in the right-hand column for the ash each fire gave back. A board is a legal instrument. A figure on a board that was not weighed is a forgery, and a person who forges a board loses their hearing and their name for a year.
3. **A hearth's own ash may only pay that hearth's own debts.** Ash is not transferable. Given to another household's fire it is not a gift, it is a claim, and a claim is read aloud at the Scale House within the week with the giver named.
4. **A shortfall is entered as a short.** A hearth weighed short at dusk has a mark made against its number in the Cinder Ledger, and a marked name cannot testify, cannot hold a lease, and cannot be counted in a queue. Seven years.
5. **Glass remembers the balance it was made at.** A slipper cast in sand against a specific weighing will sit on one foot only, and that foot is not a lucky foot or a small foot. It is the foot that balances to the figure that was struck when the mould was closed.
6. **A godmother is an accountant.** A sponsor is a person who guarantees another's debt in writing and is repaid first out of everything, before any other creditor, before the parish, before the Crown. In Emberwyke this is an ordinary trade and about a fifth of the city is in some way sponsored.
7. **Burning a thing of a person's is a claim against them,** entered in the ledger the same night, at the value the board gives it, and a person whose things are burned may answer for the debt in kind.
8. **A slipper worn past midnight is a claim presented.** Whoever puts it on is honouring the claim and must answer it by the following dusk. Whoever picks it up and does not put it on has refused a claim, and refusal is entered against their own name, and refusal is worse than the debt.
9. **The Scale House cannot weigh a person twice in a day.** What the beam says at dusk is the whole of that person for that day. A hearthman who wants a different answer must wait until tomorrow, which is why, in Emberwyke, nobody wants an answer at all.
10. **A fire that is put out is dormant, not dead,** and it can be relit only by the person who put it out, and only at the same hour it went out, and only if that person's board is clean. A relit fire that is owed a night is counted twice.

## Cast

**Bess Tallis**, 14, a hearth-board keeper for the Rope Walk, one of about two hundred children paid a farthing a night to rule a slate. *Want:* to be allowed to carry a board to the Scale House herself and be believed. *Flaw:* she rules the figures in advance, in the evening, because the cold is worse in the morning, and has done so for two winters, and every figure has been right. *Detail:* a slate on a loop of tarred twine that she wears under her coat and checks in doorways like other children check a hand. *Speech habit:* states the night before the question — "that was a two-sack week, that was" — and treats every event as a week.

**Ivo Sarn**, 52, glass-founder at Sallow Bridge, fourth generation, the man who sands moulds and closes them. *Want:* to be asked to open the sand again after thirty-one years and be told no in a way that is not an insult. *Flaw:* he has never once in his life recorded the balance he struck, on the grounds that a number on a record is a promise and he does not make promises. *Detail:* the pale crescent of a burn across the heel of his right hand, from a mould that leaked at a temperature he had already decided was right, and a habit of testing hot glass with the back of that hand. *Speech habit:* gives temperatures, always — "eleven hundred and holding" — and answers every question with a number, including questions about people.

**Mared Holt**, 61, ash-runner, twenty-nine years with a barrow and a tarpaulin, holder of the longest clean board in the Rope Walk. *Want:* to die in a house that is not in deficit, which she has now arranged for, in writing, in a way she has not told anybody about. *Flaw:* she has never once missed a night and has never once been late, and she has built a life on a discipline she learned from a woman who cheated her in 1994 and she cannot say so. *Detail:* a barrow with a false bottom and a great deal of ballast, and a habit of checking the axle with her heel. *Speech habit:* apologises before every sentence, including "no".

**Ottoline Vray**, 9, a foundling on the Sallow Bridge steps who has been found twice and gone twice, and is currently under the protection of nobody and therefore of no account at all. *Want:* to be given something without it being a claim. *Flaw:* she has worked out how the board works and she is terrified of it, and has started refusing gifts, which in this city is a way of insulting people. *Detail:* a grey felt hat too large for her, turned up twice at the brim, and the habit of turning it up again whenever an adult looks at her. *Speech habit:* asks "who is it for" about every object, every meal, every kind word, and does not understand why the answer is always "you".

**Cobb Renn**, 39, a godmother by trade, a guarantor of eleven hearths on the Coalquay, the only person in the city who is liked and feared in the same breath. *Want:* to be repaid in full on a house she has already promised to buy. *Flaw:* she cannot refuse a sponsorship, which means she has guaranteed two hearths she knows will fail, and she did it anyway, and she knows the exact night both will fail. *Detail:* a green wax seal on a leather purse that is never closed, and a habit of counting a room as she enters it. *Speech habit:* says "I will see it through" and means it, always, and is therefore always late.

**Sim Uncle Prawd**, 74, retired Scale House beam-man, forty-four years on the balance, keeper of the tolerance of the beam. *Want:* to have the beam's tolerance written down before he dies, because he has been working to a figure in his own head for eleven years and it is not the figure on the plate. *Flaw:* he has adjusted the beam eleven times and written down the adjustment nine times and told the other two to nobody, and he is the reason the whole city is out by a hair. *Detail:* one eye gone and a green shade over the other, and a habit of testing the beam with his own shoe at the start of every day. *Speech habit:* argues for and against in the same sentence and will not be interrupted, and finishes every argument with "which is the point".

## Places

**The Scale House.** The only stone building in the city: a long hall with a plank floor stained in three generations of ash, a counter of oak, and at the far end the beam — a bronze yoke on a cast-iron column, twenty feet of throw, with a stow of duck-weights down one side and a catch-pan under it. Two clerks, a beam-man, and a queue. The whole city passes through it in the four hours after dusk, and the sound of it is one voice at a time saying a number.

**The Slag Bank.** The low ground south of the river, where every hearth in Emberwyke sends its residue and where the city gets its fill: a black, soft, smoking landscape of half-buried ash-pits, tin, and brick, worked by people who are not on any roll. Children go there for the copper. It is where a city's memory actually goes, and the Cinder Ledger has a column for it called *disposed*, and the column is never checked.

**Sallow Bridge Glasshouse.** A long shed on the river bank with a bottle furnace at one end and a cooling floor of sand the whole length of it. Sand is dug at Cold Ash, three miles downriver, and brought in wet and re-baked. The work is quiet, hot, and slower than it looks; a shoe mould takes two days to close and two to strike. Everything in the city that is transparent came from this shed.

**The Rope Walk.** A street of small terraced hearths, one room and a board, where the city's poorest live with the highest discipline. The boards are on the walls, slate, ruled, with the farthing a night. The children who rule them are called board-keepers and there are two hundred of them, and the largest of them is fourteen.

**The Salt Hall.** A long cold chamber off Sallow Bridge, lit by six tall windows and no fire at all: the one room in Emberwyke where a lighting-up cannot be settled, because the Hall's charter predates the licensing and nobody will light a fire in it. Renters use it for weddings, wakes, and the settling of disputes that must not be warm.

**The Cinder Ledger Room.** Two rooms over the Scale House: low, ledger-walled, one north window, a stove that is the only legal fire in the building and burns at a fixed figure recorded every hour. Nine clerks. The book itself is on a lectern under a brass rail, eleven volumes, one per year, and the eleventh is still filling.

## Visual Style

A palette of soot black, wet silt grey, and river green, lit almost entirely by one warm orange source — a hearth mouth, a furnace, a carried candle — against a cold blue dusk that comes in very fast and never quite finishes. The light is always low and always from below, because in this city the light comes off the floor, where the fire is. Materials: blackened brick with numbers cast in the third course, ash dust, wet tarred rope, duck-weights, worn ledger paper, coke smoke, silt, river ice, and the hard green-white glare of glass in the hand. The era reads as northern European river industrial, 1890s-1910s: no electric light, no fantasy armour, nothing shining that is not glass or bronze. The recurring motif is the beam, and the twenty feet of empty air on either side of the thing being weighed.

## Motifs

1. **The board** — a slate ruled in nights, the right-hand column in a child's careful figures, one small square for each day, and the square that is wrong.
2. **The number in the brick** — a hearth number cast into the third course of every chimney, in a casting hand, with the maker's tick under it.
3. **The slipper** — a sand-cast glass shoe, not delicate: heavy, flawed, with the seam of the mould still visible and one flat spot on the sole where it struck the lead when it was struck from the sand.
4. **The duck-weights** — a stow of small cast ducks on a board, each with its value struck into its breast, worn smooth and bright where they are handled.
5. **Ash on a sleeve** — the grey bloom that gets into the seams of a coat in one night in this city and is the surest sign of employment, and of debt.

## Thematic Spine

All nine stories are about a debt that is only a debt because somebody wrote it down, and about what a community does to itself when the writing is the only thing holding it together. The accessible band treats it as weight: a child ruling a figure, a shoe that will not go on, a queue in the rain. The intermediate band treats it as liability: a mark against a name, a guarantee called in, a fire relit at the wrong hour. The advanced band treats it as the ledger itself: an audit, a provenance, and a proposal to erase the record, and the discovery that the record was never what was in danger.
