# World: The Long Vow

The Long Vow is a mountain pilgrimage: eleven stone shrines strung along a high pass, each one with a roof of slate slabs and a keeper who writes down every vow spoken inside it, and between them four hundred miles of walking weather. People climb the pass for one reason — to say a promise out loud where it will be held — and the promise is held in the body. A vow of years sets the bones. A vow of a season sets the appetite. A vow of a lifetime sets the walk itself, so that the sworn can find their own shrine from anywhere on the mountain, in any weather, in the dark. Pilgrims come up carrying thin food and heavier intentions, and they go down changed and often a little thinner. What is wrong this year is the arithmetic. A great many vows are coming due at once, because the pass has been busy and prosperous for a century, and because the Register — the bound ledger the keepers copy into, which records the terms of every vow and not the dates they end — cannot answer the question the whole mountain is now asking: who is meant to be released, and when?

## Setting

The pass runs north to south over a shoulder of grey rock four thousand feet above the treeline, and the shrines sit at every place where a spring comes out of the stone. There is no road, only the old stair: eleven thousand stone steps cut and re-cut over six hundred years, worn into shallow bowls where six hundred years of feet have gone. The sun is fierce and the shade is cold. Hail comes in September, sideways, twice a day. Nothing grows above the fourth shrine but lichen, saxifrage, and the tough bent grass that holds the soil together with its roots. The wind comes over the ridge at night in one long unbroken push, and the pilgrims call it the Registrar, and they are only half joking.

Dwellings are drystone, slate-roofed, low, and thick-walled, built to keep heat. A keeper's house has one good room, a hearth, a stone trough, and a slate shelf of ledgers, which is the most valuable furniture in the building. Food is thin: hard sheep's cheese, smoked goat, oatcakes baked in the ash, boiled mountain lentils, a bitter root tea, and much water. Honey is a luxury and is used for wounds.

Transport is walking, and only walking. Four shaggy pack-mules serve the shrines, carrying ten stone-weight each; their names are part of the liturgy, because mules are among the few animals permitted through vow-bound ground. On pilgrim market day, the second week of the eighth month, the terrace above the Shrine of the Ninth Step fills: cheese wheels wrapped in rush, oatcake bundles, iron-shod staves, woollen wraps dyed in the four shrine colours, a seller of blank vow-slips nobody admits to needing, and a slow queue at the copy-desk where a travelling clerk will write out your vow for you, for a fee.

## The Magic

1. **A vow spoken aloud in a shrine room binds the body, not the will.** The speaker is not made loyal or good. They are made unable to do the one thing they swore not to do. A vow of silence means the tongue thickens and will not form the forbidden words; a vow of return means the legs turn north of their own accord.

2. **Every vow has a stated term, and the term is part of the vow.** "Until the first snow" is a term. "Until I am forty" is a term. "For life" is a term. A vow with no term is not read as a vow; it is read as noise, and the shrine is silent.

3. **The term ends at its own appointed time, without ceremony.** Nobody has to be forgiven, no rite is performed, no priest is required. On the day, the binding simply lifts. A person freed of a vow feels nothing dramatic — only that something they had been carrying is no longer there.

4. **The binding is physical and lives in the body.** A term of years sets the bone and the teeth and shows in the walking. A vow of silence sets the throat. A vow of fasting sets the stomach, which hurts. A vow of silence and a vow of fasting cannot be sworn by the same person in the same term, because the body will not hold both.

5. **Cost is proportional and non-negotiable.** The longer the term, the more the binding takes from the body during the term: sleep, appetite, colour, the ease of the breath on a hill. A life-vow holder in old age walks slowly and does not feel cold, and the mountain regards this as fair exchange, not as punishment.

6. **The limit: the binding can only be broken at a door a keeper opens.** A vow ends for its holder at any shrine door the keeper opens from the inside — any shrine, any keeper, any hour, no petition and no ceremony. This is the only mercy in the system, and it is entirely unrecorded, because it was never supposed to matter.

7. **A vow made under duress binds at full strength.** There is no way to tell. The shrine does not test for it. A keeper who suspects it may refuse to write — and a keeper who refuses to write is liable to the whole pass, which is why refusals are almost unheard of and almost always true.

8. **The binding reads only what is said, and only the plain sense.** Ambiguity is resolved by the keeper's written term, and if the keeper writes it wrong the body keeps the keeper's version. This is the entire history of every disaster in this world: not malice, but a bad pen.

9. **Breaking a term breaks the body, not the oath.** A person who does the forbidden thing does not go free. The cost is immediate and specific to the vow: the bone that held it resets wrong, the throat seals for days, the legs will not bear weight for a season. Recovery is possible and is not guaranteed, and the mountain sends no message about it either way.
10. **Nobody is a villain of this. The binding is arithmetic, and arithmetic can be wrong.** The Register records the *term* of every vow faithfully. It does not record the *date the term ends*, because a vow was never supposed to be about the end. It was supposed to be about the thing you said you would do.

## Cast

**Ilse Merrow**, 41, Keeper of the Shrine of the Ninth Step, twenty-two years in the chair. *Want:* to reach the end of her own term without ever once asking how much longer she has. *Flaw:* she has never once in her life asked the Register a question she already knew the answer to. *Detail:* a white streak in her hair that arrived in one night nineteen years ago and has not grown since, and a keeper's habit of turning her whole body to face whoever is speaking. *Speech habit:* repeats the last three words of whatever you just said, flatly, before answering it.

**Tovan Rell**, 68, warden-copier, the last man alive who can read the Register's older hand. *Want:* to be told he was right about the arithmetic in the year of the great thaw, and to say it out loud before he dies. *Flaw:* he has never told a living person how many years a term ran for in the older hand, because in his lifetime the number was considered a discourtesy. *Detail:* ink-stained cuffs to the elbow and a magnifying glass he no longer needs, carried anyway. *Speech habit:* numbers out loud, always, and "if I have it right" before any claim he cannot support.

**Ansel Dun**, 15, a cowherd from the valley under the pass who has never in his life been up the stairs. *Want:* to be let to swear a vow, so that the mountain will take him seriously. *Flaw:* he wants the binding more than he wants the thing he would be bound to. *Detail:* split, dirty fingernails and a burn on his hip from sleeping on the flat in sheep smoke, and a hand that is always already reaching for a wall. *Speech habit:* agrees too fast, with "yes, right, yes" before anyone has finished the sentence.

**Marda Osk**, 34, registrar's clerk, sent up from the low country by the Bureau of Vowed Obligations to bring the pass into compliance for the first time in a hundred and eighty years. *Want:* a complete, dated, correct inventory of every living vow on the mountain. *Flaw:* she believes that a thing recorded properly is a thing that has been dealt with. *Detail:* ink-callused right middle finger and a soft grey coat that is still city-clean at the top of the stairs, plus a smell of lamp oil in her sleeves that she cannot get out. *Speech habit:* reads her own questions back to you, word for word, before she answers anything.

**Brother Seat**, 52, a sworn man who gave nine years of labour to the pass and is in the last of them — the name on his tally-stick, chosen at his swearing, because names on the Register are the only ones that are not chosen. *Want:* to be alive on the far side of the date, which is now knowable. *Flaw:* he has arranged his entire life around not knowing and cannot tell whether the answer is a relief or an amputation. *Detail:* a badly set left shoulder that sits an inch higher than the right, and a full iron-grey beard he has grown for the first time in his life. *Speech habit:* finishes other people's sentences, and is always, always wrong.

**Het Kalder**, 9, Ilse's daughter, who lives in the upper room of the Ninth Step and has never been below the last step. *Want:* to know what her mother sounds like saying a vow. *Flaw:* she has read the Register in the cold hours and knows she should not have, and has told nobody. *Detail:* a knitted blue fingerless glove on the left hand only, and hair kept in a plait she does the whole length of it herself. *Speech habit:* narrates what she is doing out loud, softly, all the time, in the third person.

## Places

**The Terrace of the Ninth Step.** The flat stone shelf at the midpoint of the pass, the largest of the eleven shrine platforms, with the market held on it twice a year. It is paved with flat slabs worn into shallow dishes by six hundred years of standing still, and on a clear morning you can see nine of the shrines at once going up and two coming down, and every one of them with its own weather.

**The Copy-Desk.** The one warm, paper-filled room on the mountain: a slate shelf of ledgers, a lectern, three stools, and a smell of ink, tallow and old sheepskin. Vows come here to be written, checked against the Register, and initialled in four colours. It is the only room in the pass that is not made of mountain, and it was the first thing built and the last thing to be repaired.

**The Shutter-Stone.** The flat black slate outside the Ninth Step, three paces square, that no one may cross while a vow is being read. The stone is polished by six hundred years of feet going around it, in a wide worn arc, and looking at it in lamplight the arc looks exactly like the shape of a person standing aside.

**The High Ladder.** The last, steepest hundred and twenty steps between the Ninth Step and the Shrine of the Pass, where the wind comes over the edge in one long unbroken push. Pilgrims rest on the landing at the halfway bell and drink water there and do not talk much. Everything at the top is thin, bright, windless, and very cold in the shade.

**The Mule Yard.** The one flat piece of ground on the whole pass, at the Shrine of the Second Water, shored up with retaining wall and always in the lee of the wall. It smells of animals. It is where the four pack-mules are fed, where the last argument before a vow is usually had, and where the people going up to swear something generally sleep badly.

**The Reliquary of Set Terms.** A cold, dry, windowless cell cut into the rock under the top shrine, holding the carved wooden tally-sticks of every vow ever made on the pass. There are eleven thousand of them, stacked in bundles by year, each one cut with a notch for each year of its term, and the notches are the only record on this mountain of when anything ends.

## Visual Style

A high, thin, cold palette of wet grey granite and slate, bleached lichen, and one warm ochre accent of lamplight against juniper scrub and brown heather; a low winter sun that makes everything read as a silhouette against glare. Lighting is almost always a single small flame in a deep stone doorway, with a vast luminous sky behind it, and the second-light of a mountain afternoon, which is flat, blue and shadowless. Materials are drystone, slate slabs, linden-bark slips, lead and beeswax, oiled wool in four shrine colours — ochre, undyed grey, madder red, and undyed white — rope, chamois, and old paper. The era reads early-modern pilgrim and hand-built: felted hats, staff leather, no glass towers, no polished metal, nothing that has not been carried up a hill. The single recurring motif, reused across every cover in this world, is one human hand held out flat and open in cold air, wearing or releasing a thin band of hammered iron.

## Motifs

1. **The tally-stick bundle** — a bundle of notched linden wood tied with waxed twine, eleven thousand of them, the only record of an ending.
2. **The four shrine colours** in wool: ochre, undyed grey, madder red, undyed white, worn at the cuff, the sash, the hatband, the boot-lace.
3. **The worn arc** on the Shutter-Stone, the shape of six hundred years of people stepping politely aside.
4. **A stoup of cold water and a dry crust of bread** on a windowsill, left for whoever is coming down the stairs.
5. **Hands** — knuckles, chalk, split nails, a hand held out to be released, a hand held up to stop an argument.

## Thematic Spine

All nine stories are about promises as a physical fact, and about the difference between a vow's meaning and a vow's term — between what you said you would do and the date on which the mountain stops making you do it. The accessible band treats the vow as a living thing to be approached carefully: a stair, a word repeated back, a night before, a child in a warm room. The intermediate band treats it as a matter of record and consequence — what a term is, who wrote it, what happens when the record and the memory disagree. The advanced band treats it as a matter of administration and consent: who may date a vow, who benefits from an undated one, and what it means to be released when you had quietly built a life on not being.
