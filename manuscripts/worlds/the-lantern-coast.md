# World: The Lantern Coast

Brinehold is a steep little fishing town glued to a shelf of black shale above a cold green bay, and its people are ordinary in the way that good lighthouse families are ordinary: up before the sky, down after the boats, hands always smelling of rope, eel oil, and woodsmoke. Along the cliff top runs the Ridge Line, a walk of nine iron lamp posts where the lamplighters carry brass lanterns with name plaques and light them at dusk to pay a debt the sea has held over the town since the Winter of Lost Boats. The wonderful thing about the coast is that everyone knows the ritual, sings the walking songs, and sleeps soundly because of it. The wrong thing is that the lights are going out from the inside: the innermost lantern, the First Lantern in the harbour, is guttering in its own glass, and the lamps nearer home are dying before the ones out on the head.

## Setting

Brinehold stands on a switchback of black shale three hundred feet above the bay, so that every house is reached by stone stairs and every child learns to carry a light uphill before they learn to swim. The rock is fissile and soft, stacked in flat black plates that flake into shale gravel and give the whole bay a smell of struck flint and cold iron; the water itself is a pale jade in sunlight and near-black in the lanes, with kelp coming up in slow green ropes. Summers are warm and long, all salt wind, hot stone and flies; winters are wet and vertical, with sleet coming sideways off the water that turns the Ridge Line into a sheet of moving gold. The town is one street: Brinehold Row, stepped, with slate roofs, red-painted doors bleached pink, drying nets on lines strung between chimneys, and a stone breakwater down at Tidetown Landing where the auction bell is rung by hand. Food is whatever came in that morning: smoked hake, mussel bread baked in the kiln, limpets pried off the rocks, eiderdown oil in stoppered jars that smells faintly of vanilla, and hot bowls of milk broth carried up the steps at two in the morning. Market day is Thursday and happens on the landing: canvas booths, a weigh shed, crates of ice going white, boat crews arguing about the price of bait, the lamp-trimmer passing through with an armful of trimmed wicks and a plug of his own tallow for anyone who will take it. People move by foot, by hand-cart, or by dory; the only wheeled vehicle in town is the Harbour Council tally wagon. Beyond the town lie the Outer Stacks, a scatter of black reefs that give the bay its teeth, and out on the point the Crown lighthouse, which no one has kept lit in years because, as the harbourmaster says, the sea can see well enough on its own.

## The Magic

1. **The Bargain.** In the Winter of Lost Boats the sea took thirty-one boats and their crews off Brinehold. The town, unable to bury or even find them, agreed to a debt: every dusk the coast pays the water in light, and in exchange the drowned sleep warm at the bottom of the bay instead of rising. The debt is owed to the sea, not to the drowned, and it has no end date.
2. **Named lanterns.** Each post of the Ridge Line holds one lantern, and each lantern bears a brass plaque with a name. The name is the lantern's legal identity: it names the sleeper the light keeps, and it is the only thing the sea accepts as a signature on a payment. A lantern with no name is not a lantern; it is only a fire in a glass box, and the sea counts it as zero.
3. **Light is the only currency, and it must be given freely.** A lamplighter lights a lantern by giving a piece of their own light, taken from the hours they will not live. It costs hours, not years: an ordinary dusk relighting costs about an hour of life, and no one feels it at the time. The light must be offered willingly, by the bearer, with a named intention in mind. Light taken from a sleeping person, a child, or anyone who did not consent is not currency at all; it is theft, and theft cannot be spent.
4. **The cost accumulates and is never refunded.** Hours given cannot be given back, borrowed, or repaid by earning more. A lamplighter who has spent heavily ages visibly and quickly; the most spent lighters on the coast are the youngest-looking women and the most tired-looking men. This is why nobody on the Ridge Line envies the job.
5. **The limit is nine.** The Ridge Line has nine lamps and has always had nine. A tenth lantern cannot be raised anywhere on this coast, and a seventh name cannot be added to a lamp. The shape of the ritual is fixed; only the currency is negotiable, which is precisely where the town went wrong.
6. **The inward order.** Lights go out from the inside outward, always. The First Lantern, at the harbour, holds the whole chain; if it dies, the next fails within a night, then the next. The town has learned this the hard way, twice, and it is chalked on the tide board in the Harbour Council house.
7. **What sleeps does not dream, and what is spoken to wakes.** A sleeper in the bay is inert: warm, unaware, safe. But a sleeper can be spoken to. One who answers a single word, or who is given a name by someone who is not the lamplighter for that lamp, begins to surface, and a surfacing sleeper takes the warmth of the bay with it, which means the whole bay gets colder and one lamp fewer can stay lit. This is the mechanism by which the wrong currency kills: not violence, arithmetic.
8. **Quiet Tallow, the wrong currency.** Thirty years ago the Harbour Council, unable to keep paying in hours, began rendering "Quiet Tallow" from the rendered fat and memory-scrapings of sleeping townsfolk. It burns beautifully, smells sweet, and costs no one anything, so it was adopted coast-wide. It does not pay the debt. It defers it, by shortening each sleeper's rest, so the debt comes due again sooner and larger, and each payment with it buys less. A lantern fed Quiet Tallow is not lit; it is merely warm, and the glass corrodes from within, which is why the failures start inside.
9. **Breaking a rule has a physical cost in the bay.** Take a name off a plaque, speak a sleeper's true name outside the ritual, or spend light that was not freely given, and the corresponding stretch of coast loses its name first: the street sign, then the road, then the family names of everyone living there, in that order, over three nights. This is the only genuinely frightening thing in the world's magic, and it reverses only by paying correctly.

## Cast

**Ansel Quance**, 13, lamplighter's apprentice three years into carrying a pole. *Wants:* to walk the Ridge Line alone, all nine lamps, one dusk, without Hadren behind him. *Flaw:* he hoards light instead of spending it, and has a stoppered jar of spare flame that he is ashamed of and will not throw away. *Physical detail:* a single red mitten with no mate, its thumb-end darned with a strip of sailcloth. *Speech habit:* narrates everything as a numbered plan out loud, counting his fingers, and says "One, two, three, and" at the end of lists.

**Neel Ferrin**, 11, wick-trimmer and junior lamp-runner, the fastest child on Brinehold Row. *Wants:* to be grown enough to be trusted with a real lantern. *Flaw:* she is frightened of the water and of the dark under the Ridge Line, and will admit neither to anyone. *Physical detail:* a bright yellow oilskin rain-slicker with a hood she never takes off, not indoors, not in August. *Speech habit:* begins every honest sentence with "Listen -" and then, half the time, changes it into something safer.

**Hadren Vole**, 61, Chief Lamplighter of the Ridge Line, holder of the First Lantern key. *Wants:* to die with all nine lamps burning and a coast that knows how to carry on without him. *Flaw:* he signed the Quiet Tallow compact thirty years ago and has never told anyone, including his own family. *Physical detail:* white stubble on his jaw gone the pale green of sea foam, and a thumbprint of lamp-black in the crease of his right thumb that will not wash out. *Speech habit:* answers any question he does not like with the single word "cheaper."

**Wren Adry**, 15, ledger-keeper to the Harbour Council, third-generation keeper of the Great Oweing Book. *Wants:* to prove on paper that the debt was fully paid sixty years ago and the coast is being dunned for nothing. *Flaw:* she trusts arithmetic more than people, and will follow a correct sum into a room where it does not belong. *Physical detail:* ink-stained blue fingertips and a ledger bound in tarblue cloth that she carries under one arm the way other children carry a doll. *Speech habit:* finishes other people's arithmetic aloud before they say the number.

**Sten Ollivant**, 41, water-diver and salvage rigger working the Outer Stacks, the only man in town the sea has not yet collected. *Wants:* his sister Maren back, warm, whole, and out of the water. *Flaw:* he takes light without asking, from places and people that have not consented, and has done it so long he no longer notices the cold in his hands. *Physical detail:* a red scarf stiff with dried salt that he has never washed. *Speech habit:* a single low word, "Still," delivered at the end of a sentence like a verdict.

**Pim Sabiston**, 8, the youngest lamp-runner on the coast, whose whole job is to carry a lantern key from the Lamp House to whoever needs it. *Wants:* a pet, and she has decided a lit lantern counts. *Flaw:* she gives away her own light so freely that the lamplighters have had to invent a rule to stop her. *Physical detail:* a brass lantern-shaped key worn on a cord at her throat, always, even in bed. *Speech habit:* rhymes the last two words of everything she says, cheerfully, whether or not the rhyme makes sense.

## Places

**Brinehold Row.** The town's single stepped street, slate roofs and red doors bleached pink, drying nets strung between chimneys, a tide board nailed by every door where the Chief Lamplighter chalks the evening's nine lamps. At dusk the whole street turns amber from the Ridge Line above it, and the shadows of the drying nets stripe the wall like handwriting.

**The Ridge Line.** The cliff-top walk of nine cast-iron lamp posts, four miles of salt grass and low gorse between the harbour and Cormorant Head, with the sea a hundred and forty feet below and the whole bay laid out like a lit map. A lamplighter walking it at dusk is a small orange figure in a great deal of moving weather; from the water it looks like nine sparks in a line, which is exactly the shape of a debt being paid off.

**The Lamp House.** A squat stone hut at the harbour end of the Ridge Line with a hundred and one lanterns hung on cedar pegs, sorted by name, each brass plaque polished and each chimney trimmed. The Chief Lamplighter's key board hangs inside the door, and the room smells of hot brass, kerosene, and trimmed wick ends.

**Tar Kiln Steps.** The black zigzag stair down to the old pitch works, where a hundred years of rendered oil and Quiet Tallow was boiled in an open iron pot, and the steps themselves are glossy and slightly soft. Kelp and rag hang on the lines overhead to catch the drip, so the whole staircase is one flat black shine from top to bottom and walking it feels like walking down a wet roof.

**Tidetown Landing.** Brinehold's harbour: a stone breakwater, a timber weigh shed, canvas auction booths, a hundred crates of ice going white in the sun. On Thursday morning it is the loudest place in the world, and at slack water it goes so quiet you can hear the pilot boat knocking in the Outer Stacks.

**The Drowned Bell on Black Spit.** A bronze bell hung from a timber frame on a black shingle spit, rung by no living hand: it swings in still weather, three slow notes, and the bay goes quiet enough to hear the eiderdown on the water. Low benches of flat rock face it, and the one closest to the water has worn smooth from a century of people sitting down to wait.

## Visual Style

A wet, black-shale palette of slate grey, ink blue, and jade water, lit almost entirely by warm ochre and amber flame from brass lanterns, so every image reads as cold stone with one orange pool in it. Materials are oiled canvas, tarred rope, salt-crusted brass, verdigris copper, and shuttered slate; the era feel is late-nineteenth-century working coast - wooden cranes, no steam, no electric light, everything hand-hauled. Light behaves like a held breath: fog and sea-smoke give every flame a soft-edged halo and a long amber path on wet stone. The recurring motif is the line of nine small flames against a vast dark bay, with a brass name plaque in the foreground catching the light the story is about.

## Motifs

1. **The brass name plaque**, screwed to every lantern body and polished with a chamois, its engraved letters filling with light when the lamp is properly lit and staying flat and cold when it is not.
2. **The nine-lamps-at-dusk walk**, the walking song that counts the posts out loud; the most repeated image in the world's stories.
3. **Eiderdown oil**, pale goose-grease in stoppered jars, sweet-smelling, the honest fuel; the coast's measure of what a gift costs.
4. **The red mitten with the sailcloth darn**, passed between characters as a small lamp of its own.
5. **The Blue Tally**, a line of chalked strokes on the Harbour Council tally wagon or on the tide board recording what the coast has spent this month, and the chalk hand that keeps adding to it wrongly.

## Thematic Spine

Underneath all nine stories is one quiet question: what is owed, who agreed to it, and what happens when a community discovers it has been paying the wrong way because the right way hurt. There are no villains here, only tired, decent people who let a small cheat harden into a system, and every story turns on the moment somebody admits that in front of neighbours. The accessible band takes the moral as honesty with small things - finish the job, put the name back, walk all nine lamps - and pays for it in effort. The intermediate band takes it as arithmetic with feelings inside it, asking what a fair price is when one side wrote the contract. The advanced band takes it as complicity: the debt was real, the substitution was chosen, and every generation since has made it easier by not asking.

## Canon Rulings (binding on all nine stories)

These settle questions raised after the bible was first circulated. Where a ruling and the story text conflict, the ruling wins.

**The arithmetic.** Ridge Line raised 1846. Lit Michaelmas to Candlemas, 150 dusks a season, 9 lamps a dusk = 1,350 lamplights a season; 55 seasons = 74,250 entered by October 1901. Delivered 70,701. Arrears 3,549, and only 3,549. Split: 3,000 tallow (4,500 tallow-dusks rendered from 1871, each entered as a full lamplight while delivering a third, so two thirds of 4,500 was never shown to anybody) plus 549 carried for the twenty-two unposted sleepers at the rate of the nine. Derivations that travel with the figure: 394 nights and one hour at nine a dusk; 25 hours and 10 minutes across 141 doors; the remainder hour is the whole difference between a rota and a rate. A lamplight is one hour of life given to one post. There is no coin in this world and no coin-shaped word.

**The season.** The Ridge Line is dark between Candlemas and Michaelmas, so no dusk walk can happen outside the lit season. Stories set on the Ridge Line run late October to February. The Winter of Lost Boats is an event, not a month, and may be named in any season.

**The 1871 act was one decision, not two.** Hadren Vole cut "until the owing is paid" out of the recited compact in the same afternoon the tallow was first rendered. He was 31. No four-signatory compact, no 1869 version, no 1839 lamps.

**The nine plaques name nine of the thirty-one.** Twenty-two sleepers never had a post; a tenth lantern cannot be raised and a seventh name cannot go on a lamp.

**Ruling on the 549 and the sea's silence (binds 101, 105, 108).** Some of the twenty-two the sea has simply never charged for. The 549 is a Council bookkeeping line, not a sea demand: the Council carries the unposted at the rate of the nine because it has always assumed the debt is owed on their behalf, when for several of them it never was. So 101's shingle lantern is correct as written - its sleeper was never charged, and her lantern has nothing to burn for - and the 549 is simultaneously a real deficit and a false one. It is the smaller line of the same lie as the 3,000 tallow, written by the same hand, and must not be treated as a separate grievance. 105 should find the 549 unsourceable; 108 should find that the Council entered men against themselves.

**Ruling on the First Lantern (binds 109).** It is the arrears, not a candle count. The First has been fed tallow for thirty years and is failing from within because of what that fuel is; the 3,549 is the debt-level reason the honest light has run out. So 109 is about a town discovering it has been short-paid, not one discovering it has been dunned - the sea sends no bill and no letter. The inward order stays load-bearing: the First dies first, and a town that pays in a fuel the sea does not count still loses the chain, because theft cannot be spent. The chain may survive, but only if the ninth lamp is paid for honestly and last. A town that gathers light by daylight collection has not paid, and the ninth lamp goes dark at Cormorant Head.

**Reserved nouns.** Maren Ollivant, 21, lost in the gale of 1889, number twenty-three on the 1841 list, no post: the mechanism of the naming breach and of Sten's arc, and the one person he can name with no lamp to sign it against. Halla Skerry, 34, plaque-keeper and the Council's hand with the Great Oweing Book, holds it as the widow of the last Adry, who died of his lungs in March; Wren Adry, 15, is her apprentice and heir. Osa Kilbride, 66, chairs the Harbour Council. Jory Trask, 26, lamplighter, no relation to the Adry line. Teo Ivers, 19, lamp-runner. Ilsa Trew, shingle lantern, never charged by the sea (101).
