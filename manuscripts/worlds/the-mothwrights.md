# World: The Mothwrights

Candleford is a low, warm, gaslit city built on a peat fen, and out beyond its lamp-lit bridges stand the Pale Hundreds: ninety plantations of a forest that has never put out a leaf in living memory. The city keeps that forest alive by flying clockwork moths over it at dusk, nine thousand of them a night, wound and built in guild workshops and released from lofts by the thousand. Every night is written down. The city is honest, well fed, and loud with a soft sound like rain on paper — the wings. The one thing wrong is a number nobody is asking: the trees set seed every autumn in a record harvest, and the ledger says the forest is thriving, because the ledger records what was sent, and nobody has counted what came back.

## Setting

Candleford sits where the River Sallow turns and spreads, a fen city of black timber, cream stucco and green copper roofs, always a little damp at the ankles. The great gas lamps go on at the first grey of evening and burn amber to dawn; by day the streets are lit instead by lime-white walls, and the fen mist comes up the drains around noon. The Pale Hundreds ring the city to the east and north — ninety numbered plantations, divided into Rows, planted in hard lines so a ledger clerk can walk them. Between them stand the feeder-groves, a different, sappier species that is cut to keep the guild supplied. Two hundred years ago the Hundreds had leaves; old prints in the Fen Museum show a canopy like weather, and nobody alive has seen one.

Food is bog onion, peat-smoked eel, hard brown fen bread, and a great deal of milk, because the fen keeps cows in summer houses. Pear and damson come up the river barges in autumn and are pressed into a black, sticky, faintly lamp-tasting syrup. Transport is barges, foot, and the Lamp Service — a guild-funded fleet of small hand-lamps carried by children and errand-runners so that anyone walking a Row after dusk can see the ground. Market day is Aldersday: from first light the barges nose in, the eel stalls smoke, the lamp-oil stalls do brisk trade, and at noon the Releasing Loft bells ring, and the whole city stops for a minute and a half to listen to nine thousand wings go out over the water.

## The Magic

1. **Moths here are built, not born.** Every moth is a guild instrument: brass wing-plates, a pin joint, a mainspring, and a bobbin. It is built to a document called **the Pattern**, and the Pattern is nine coil-holes and a number.
2. **A standard moth carries a living filament.** Woven into the lampblack silk on its bobbin is a spore coat that is alive only while the moth flies. The instant the moth comes to rest, the coat dies. Nothing can be stored, saved, or re-flown.
3. **A branch fertilized by a standard moth sets seed and does not leaf.** Every autumn it fruits; it never makes a leaf. The two facts sit in different rooms and are never added together, so the tree reads as generous and is in fact dying by degrees.
4. **A moth is wound for exactly one night.** The mainspring unwinds in flight and cannot be re-wound. A spent moth is a paperweight, then a firelighter, then a fist of ash — and the ash is where new filament silk comes from.
5. **The Pattern may not be varied.** A moth that differs in any measurable part is not a moth, it is a fault. Faulted moths are collected, rendered, and their workshops struck from the Roll of certified houses. A varied moth that works is still void.
6. **The cost is sap, and the sap is a tree.** Every coil of filament is spun from moth-ash warmed with sap bled from the feeder-groves. So each night that the forest is fertilized, living trees are cut to feed it. This is recorded in a second column, and the second column is never compared with the harvest column.
7. **The limit: moths will only work in warm dark.** A swarm must be released between the first lamp and the first grey, in still air. In daylight, wind or rain, every moth in the swarm comes down dead. Weather is therefore a ledger line of its own: *NIGHT: NO FLY, RAIN*.
8. **No one can see the work.** A moth neither stings, bites, nor lights. Its only proof that it flew is the sound, and sound is gone by morning. A failure is invisible for years, and a success is invisible forever.
9. **Breaking the Pattern has a price written in advance.** Any tree touched by a void moth must be struck from the records within one season, along with every other tree in the same Row, and the workshop that built the moth is struck from the Roll. The guild does not fine anybody. It forgets you, in writing.
10. **The release ledger cannot lie and cannot be asked a new question.** One line per swarm per night: plantation, number released, hour, name of the releasing party, weather mark. It is exact, complete, unalterable, and forty thousand lines deep. The question on a line is fixed when the line is written and can never be widened afterward — so a ledger that records only release will record release faithfully forever.

## Cast

All six recur across the world's nine stories. Individual tales may add one or two local figures — a lamp-runner, a Row clerk, a bargeman — but these six carry the voice of the world and must be present or referenced in every tale.

1. **Ansel Reyne**, 58, Assay of the Concord and the only man in Candleford who may certify a moth against the Pattern, working alone in the Comparator Vault. *Want:* to sign a line he can defend out loud, in person, to a person. *Flaw:* he has never once read a line past the release column, and he has signed eleven thousand of them. *Physical detail:* a pale crescent burn under the left jaw, and a collar worn buttoned in midsummer to hide it. *Verbal habit:* "That is to Pattern," delivered as if it were a report of the weather.
2. **Nell Ashgrove**, 15, junior in the Releasing Loft and the youngest person in Candleford who can read a Row of trees by eye. *Want:* to be allowed to touch the Pattern and be shown what each of the nine coils does. *Flaw:* she trusts a number more than a tree, which is the guild's whole disease in a body. *Physical detail:* a scorched brass thumbnail guard on her right thumb, worn bright at the tip. *Verbal habit:* says "on the count of three" before doing anything, and counts wrong.
3. **Fennim Sallowe**, 44, sap-tapper and cutter of the feeder-groves, twenty-two years in Row Eleven and the only man the city admits is killing anything. *Want:* the grove to have been worth the felling. *Flaw:* he has built a life on being the necessary man, and cannot refuse a second cutting. *Physical detail:* a black beard re-braided every week of his life, and stiff green boots. *Verbal habit:* apologises to the tree out loud before he touches it.
4. **Orla Vessey**, 39, ledger-clerk of the release ledger, the woman who writes the line every night in a hand that has not changed since she was twenty-two. *Want:* for the ledger to be right, which is the one thing in Candleford nobody is arguing about. *Flaw:* she has never written a line she doubted, and has begun to suspect that a book nobody can doubt is not a book. *Physical detail:* spectacles with the right lens ground from oiled horn. *Verbal habit:* corrects the other person's number before she answers anything else.
5. **Halvard Tace**, 52, factor of the Concord, keeper of the Roll of certified workshops and of the release rights sold by the plantation. *Want:* the Roll small enough to be true. *Flaw:* he audits everyone but himself, and he made the standard unamendable by making amendment ruinously expensive. *Physical detail:* black kid gloves, the right one cut from a different, browner leather. *Verbal habit:* greets people with "Who paid for that?"
6. **Teasel Sillet**, 12, lamp-service runner and the youngest person on the Releasing Loft stairs. *Want:* to be told that the numbers he finds are real numbers. *Flaw:* he counts too well and cannot stop showing people. *Physical detail:* a striped wool scarf he never takes off, and one permanently bare foot in the winter. *Verbal habit:* whispers every count he makes to the iron lamp-rail as he passes it, so the numbers are somewhere.

## Places

1. **Ledger Hall, Bridge Nine.** A long cream-plastered room over the canal, every window shuttered, forty thousand ruled sheets in glazed presses and the smell of hot brass, sealing wax and paper warmed by forty thousand little radiators. Two clerks work at a time and the second desk has been used as a pigeon perch since 1902. At the front hangs the Roll, framed, a hundred and forty years of certified workshops with the struck ones left visible, not erased.
2. **The Comparator Vault.** Six floors below Ledger Hall, a windowless room kept at ninety-four degrees because the comparator drifts in the cold. Ansel Reyne sits at a brass bench with a machine the size of a bread slicer and nine steel pins, and a tray of moths comes down from the lofts in oiled tin each night to be put in and taken out. The walls are black with lampblack and the floor is scored by twenty years of boots.
3. **The Releasing Loft, Crib Tower.** A shuttered wooden room at the top of the tallest tower in the city, whose doors open only at dusk. Inside, nine wooden cradles on turntables, one per plantation district, and a lamp-bell on a wire that rings over the water. A swarm goes out and the room is suddenly, absurdly quiet, and you can hear the wings for two full minutes after the doors close.
4. **The Feeder-Grove, Row Eleven.** Twenty-two acres of black sappy trees four miles north, grown in straight lines on peat, with the cut faces of the last winter still white and wet and slowly ambering. The sap line runs in buried lead pipe to the spinning house, and at felling time the whole grove smells like a cut apple and a hot lamp.
5. **The Kilnfield and the Rendering Yard.** An open field of six squat brick ovens with iron chimneys, where every spent moth in Candleford is brought in sacks and burned, and the ash is sifted for silk and re-spun into new filament. It is the warmest place in the city in the worst month, it is a beautiful and slightly obscene thing, and the smoke at night lies along the ground like a floor.
6. **The Sounding Bridge.** A plain iron footbridge over the canal where the swarms cross between the lofts and the eastern Rows, and every evening for years people came to stand on it and listen. The canal goes black and the wings go over it as a soft continuous hiss, two hundred thousand small wings, and the water keeps the sound a half-second longer than the air does.

## Visual Style

The visual identity of The Mothwrights is **brass and lampblack warmed by gaslight against a bare, fog-grey forest**: amber, brass-gold, bone-white lime plaster, soft lampblack grey, and one hard accent of sap-green. Lighting is always warm and low and practical — desk lamps, loft lamps, oven glow — against a cold blue-grey outdoors, so every cover has a hot pool of light in a large cool dark. Materials are riveted brass, oiled tin, cream ledger paper, blackened wood, bone-pale bark, and soft black silk thread. The era feel is late gaslight and guild industry: high buttoned coats, waxed capes, no motors and no plastic, fog on every window. The recurring motif is **a bobbin of black silk** — the moth spool, the Lamp Service reel, the ash-sifter's coil — so a single dark thread runs through the picture wherever the story is.

## Motifs

1. **A bobbin of lampblack silk**, unspooling in a loose soft curve across a warm wooden table.
2. **One grey feather-down** from a moth, caught in a latch, on a collar, in a book.
3. **The Pattern plate**: a small brass rectangle with nine drilled coil-holes in a precise arc, held up to a lamp.
4. **A ruled ledger line with a tick in the second column**, seen close, the ink still wet.
5. **A green-sap-stained cuff or thumb**, the one living colour on a grey person in a warm room.

## Thematic Spine

All nine stories are quietly about the difference between a record of effort and a record of result, and about the ordinary human courtesy of never asking a ledger a second question. The three levels each approach that differently: the accessible stories find the gap by accident, through a stray moth, a too-loved tree and a stubborn dusk, and resolve it with people acting together; the intermediate stories find it on paper, in a tray and a column, and resolve it by the slow courage of arithmetic and refusal; the advanced stories find it in the institution itself, where the guilt lives in the format, and resolve it by making the guild spend something it has never spent — the willingness to be recorded as having tried and failed.
