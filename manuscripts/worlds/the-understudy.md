# World: The Understudy

Beneath a soot-black river city called Verrow stands the Thistlewire, a gaslit playhouse of four hundred and twelve seats whose season is sold eleven months in advance as a set of named performers. The names are real on paper and absent from the stage. What plays the season instead is the understudy corps: eight salaried, numbered, unnamed people who go on in the places of the advertised leads every single night, and who know their characters more intimately than any of the advertised leads ever will, because they have watched every rehearsal, mended every costume, and learned every line from the prompt corner. The theatre is warm, loud, and full, and the house is watching a person be a character six nights out of seven, and nobody in the building — management, subscribers, or the printer of the programmes — is permitted to correct this. Someone has begun refusing to go on.

## Setting

Verrow is a cold, wet, industrial river city: grey stone, tarred timber, low cloud that never quite breaks from October to April, and gaslight that makes every wet pavement the colour of weak tea. The Thistlewire stands on Ardle Street, its brick front black with soot, its gas jets burning a hard blue-white, its doors hung with a board of gilt lettering that names performers who are not inside. Inside: an auditorium in the shape of a horseshoe, four hundred and twelve seats in red threadbare velvet worn pale at the front row, a plaster ceiling stained the colour of tea, and a stage framed by heavy black drapes gone brown at the hems. Under the stage, the scene-lamp machinery lives in a low iron gallery, hot enough to fry an egg in August; the lamps themselves are gas jets behind coloured glass, and their spill through the footlights is a green-teal that lies on the players' hands like water. Backstage is the real country: a plank corridor the width of a coffin, a green room smelling of greasepaint, sweat, hot iron and cold tea, an understudy bench in the wings on which eight people sit in the dark through every performance, in order, wearing their coats. A market day on Ardle Street means hot chestnuts, wet wool, a horse outside the stage door, and a queue at the box office window where Ilse Karrow sells the season from a book bound in teal cloth. The building is always warm, always late, and always, every night, full.

## The Magic

1. **A Position is a term, not a person.** A Position is a written description held in the theatre's books: a clause in a contract, a salary, a rank, an unpaid debt, an idea of a mother. It has a name and a description and it does not have a body. The description is what is on stage, and it is always printed in the programme.
2. **A Position may only be filled by a person the theatre employs.** An understudy is a salaried member of the corps, on the roll, on call, on a wage. Their own name is never printed; the Position's name is. A body may hold one Position at a time and no more, and may not hold the same Position two nights running without a rest day, because the terms of a Position may not be exceeded.
3. **The terms of the Position are the whole requirement.** If the body does what the description requires and neither more nor less, the theatre accepts the performance as correct, and correctness is not measured by feeling. Nobody upstairs can tell a good one from a faithful one.
4. **The Position must be finished by the curtain.** A Position not completed before the curtain falls is unpaid, and the strike is the whole season's wage, taken out of the book on the Friday. This is the cost, and it is the reason nobody has refused for twenty years.
5. **The house may not be told.** A subscriber who learns the truth and speaks it aloud may not purchase another seat that season, and the book will refuse the entry in the clerk's own hand. The ban is commercial, but the strike on wages is real and lands regardless of whether the truth was spoken.
6. **The Figure takes the memory, not the life.** A body that has played a Position for a long season loses the edges of its own life and gains the Figure's, including lines never spoken to it, childhoods that are not its own, and a way of standing in a room that no longer fits the body it is standing in.
7. **The book outranks the stage.** Where a name in the book and a body on stage disagree, the book is correct and the body is wrong. An entry cannot be struck by argument, by the management, or by the theatre's own board. It can be struck one way only: every member of the understudy corps must refuse in the same house, on the same night, in the same season, and the refusal must be given before the curtain.
8. **A Position may not be permanently vacated.** If no body is on stage for a Position, the house lights rise on an empty chair, the performance is a failure, the season closes that night, and every subscription is refunded in full. A failure is not a scandal. A failure is an ending.
9. **Breaking the terms empties the stage.** A body that tries to be the person rather than the Position — speaks a line that is not in the text, or answers as themselves, or stops mid-scene — is not punished. The Position simply finishes the scene without them. They wake on the understudy bench in the wings, uncredited, and the wage strike is applied on the Friday as usual.
10. **Refusal is the only act with no clause against it.** Nobody may be fined, dismissed, blacklisted or remembered for refusing to go on. The machinery of the theatre has no procedure for it, because it was never supposed to happen. What refusal does is Rule 8, and Rule 8 is the whole story.

## Cast

1. **Ilse Karrow** — 52, head of subscriptions, the officer who writes the season into the book and signs it with her own name at the head of the list. Wants: to reach the end of her final season without one unaccounted seat. Flaw: she has never once sat in the house, has never seen the play, and does not know that the person on stage is not the person in the gilt letters. Detail: a green lorgnette on a black cord she never once looks through, and a right hand in a black kid-leather glove whose index finger has gone shiny with handling the pen. Speech habit: apologises to the furniture when she passes it — "Sorry. Chair is mine" — and calls every subscriber "the book's people" and the book "the book", never "our theatre".
2. **Bram Sillet** — 41, senior understudy, the Alternate of Record for the Position of the Younger Brother, played nineteen hundred and six consecutive times. Wants: to be released from the theatre on the terms already written into his own contract. Flaw: he is the best actor in the building and he has no idea the theatre has a front door; he has not been outdoors in daylight in nineteen years and believes the river is a rumour. Detail: a permanent greasepaint tan, and two small strips of flesh-coloured tape on his cheekbones where the paint of the Younger Brother has been worn away and re-laid so many times the skin underneath gave up. Speech habit: speaks other people's business aloud in the third person, flat and precise, as rehearsal notes — "He takes the tray left of the door, and he is late, and he means to be late."
3. **Corvin Ashby** — 34, stage manager, prompter, and keeper of the prompt book; he stands in the dark left of the stage and speaks the lines of any Figure whose body has gone missing. Wants: to get through one night's performance without inventing a single word of it. Flaw: he has been supplying lines for years and does not know where his own invention stops and the theatre's memory begins, and forty of the lines in his book are marked only with the word "supplied". Detail: a hard blue bruise-shadow under his right eye, permanently, from the lamp in the prompter's box, and a thin black notebook he consults by feel. Speech habit: gives everything as distance and time — "Eleven seconds. Two paces. Third lamp is amber, do not touch it."
4. **Hester Vaile** — 63, wardrobe mistress, maker and mender of every costume any Position has ever worn. Wants: her name on the call sheet in the same ink as the Positions she dresses. Flaw: she is so proud of her work that she cannot stop improving it, and for two seasons she has been quietly altering the leads' garments to fit the understudies' bodies, so that the wrong person looks correct. Detail: a steel thimble riveted to her right thumb, a burn-blackened smoothing iron on a hook, and a tape measure worn soft and grey round her neck. Speech habit: identifies everything by cloth and will not be argued off it — "That is serge, not wool, and serge does not forgive you the way you are standing."
5. **Nell Tarrant** — 17, the youngest member of the corps, understudy for the Position of the Correspondent, four words in the second act. Wants: to be called for a part and to be paid a name on the call sheet, just one line, just her own. Flaw: she is the first to refuse, and she does not yet understand that refusal is contagious. Detail: a length of bright red thread knotted round her right wrist, worn by every member of the corps who has decided not to go on, and the knot is always a single one. Speech habit: repeats the last word she has heard before she answers anything — "Curtain? Curtain. Then I am not going on."
6. **Amos Derwent** — 15, the lamp-hand, trims and re-trims the hot scene-lamps and climbs the rope to the lighting gallery alone, and is the only person in the building who can see the whole house at once. Wants: to be allowed to work the gallery by himself, unsupervised, all the way to the top batten. Flaw: he has begun trading the house lights for messages — dimming one row of the house so a note can be passed, or kept bright so a courier is not seen leaving. Detail: one burnt black eyebrow and a thumb with a shiny new scar, plus a strip of oiled silk wound round his forearm like a bandage on nothing. Speech habit: names every object in the building by its light, and gives all time as "two minutes, no more" — "The fourth is amber, the fifth is green, and the gallery is red, and you have two minutes, no more."

## Places

- **The Understudy Bench** — Two metres of bare board in the wings, stage left, in total dark behind the heavy drape, with eight hooks above it and a hot pipe running underneath. Eight people sit here in numbered order through every single performance, in their coats, watching one twenty-metre crack of lit stage. It is the most uncomfortable place in the theatre and the only place where nobody is performing.
- **The Green Room** — A low panelled room behind the stage door with a long mirrored table, six chairs for eight people, a kettle that is always just off the boil, and a permanent fog of greasepaint that has soaked into the wallpaper. Warm, cramped, argumentative, home.
- **The Lighting Gallery** — A low iron catwalk under the stage ceiling, reachable by a rope, five feet above a floor of hot ash and gas pipe. Five scene-lamps in coloured glass, a live flame in an open trough, and a view through the slot under the proscenium of all four hundred and twelve faces, which from up there look like one field.
- **The Subscription Office** — A small panelled room off the lobby with a barred window, a brass grille, a cold desk, and the Season Book: a quarter-bound volume in teal cloth with a gilt edge, opened every day of the year at the same page, its columns of names in eleven different hands and one — the head of the list — in Ilse Karrow's.
- **The Scene-Lamp Room** — A brick chamber under the boards where the gas for the lamps is regulated by hand, hot enough in July that the stagehands work with their sleeves cut off, smelling of scorched dust and hot iron. The regulator wheel is the size of a cartwheel and the gas hisses when the house is told something.
- **The Empty Chair** — Not a place but the thing every place in this world is measured against: a plain deal chair on centre stage, lit, for the ninety seconds of the third-act scene change when a Position is genuinely absent. No one has seen it lit in twenty years. Every member of the corps knows exactly how long ninety seconds feels.

## Visual Style

Gaslight and golds: amber, brass, honey and gas-flame orange from above, with a low cold green-teal spill from the footlights lying on hands and props, so every figure is lit warm on the face and cool underneath, like something standing in shallow water. Everything else sits in oxblood, tobacco, soot-black and old bone — red velvet worn pale at the front row, brown-hemmed drapes, black kid leather, brass rails, brass lamp housings, unbleached calico and serge. Lighting is always theatrical and always wrong somewhere: bare bulbs and cold worklights visible at the edges of frame, hot lamps flaring, deep black wings swallowing anything that leaves the light. Materials are deal, brick, brass, serge, greasepaint, horsehair braid, gilt edging and paper programmes gone soft with handling. The era feel is late-Victorian provincial — gas mantles, a booking clerk, a call sheet, no electric light anywhere in the building. The recurring motif is a number: a chalked cue number on a brick, a number on a call sheet, a number tied round a wrist, and the eight on the bench hooks.

## Motifs

1. The cue number — chalked on brick, pencilled in a prompt book, written on a call sheet, and read out before every entrance.
2. Greasepaint: sticks, smudged thumbs, the permanent tan, tape used to hold a face together across a run.
3. The Season Book in teal cloth with a gilt edge, and the black kid glove of the woman who writes in it.
4. The understudy bench in the wings: eight hooks, one hot pipe, eight people in their coats.
5. Coloured glass and flame: amber, green-teal, deep red, seen from below and through the slot, the only light in the world that is meant to be looked at.

## The Nine Stories

| 201 | The Cue Nobody Gives | accessible | short | Amos climbs to the gallery to trim the fourth lamp and finds a cue number chalked on the brick for a scene that has not happened in nineteen years, and that nobody in the wings has ever given. He works out who would have to call it, and the answer is a person who is not in the building, and the resolution is a small, careful, entirely illegal errand carried out by a boy with a bucket of hot water. |
| 202 | The Coat Off the Second Hook | accessible | medium | Hester has spent a season quietly taking in the Younger Brothers coat so it fits Bram and nobody, not the wardrobe committee nor the leads nor the book, can say a word about it. When the alteration is found out she has to choose between the work that is hers and the rule that made the work possible, and she resolves it by unstitching the whole garment in front of the entire company, at two in the morning, with her own thimble. |
| 203 | Night One Thousand Nine Hundred | accessible | long | Bram plays the nineteen hundred and sixth night and decides, in the middle of the second act, that he is finished. What he finds in the next two hours is that his refusal is not his alone to hold: it moves down the bench, hook by hook, and by the curtain four people have not gone on and the corps has quietly divided into the frightened and the certain. The story ends with Bram still on the stage, alone with the only thing he has ever wanted, which is to be asked. |
| 204 | The Forty Supplied Lines | intermediate | short | Corvin discovers that forty of the lines in his prompt book are marked only with the word supplied, and that he wrote them himself, years ago, and that the theatre has been performing his invention ever since because nobody checked. One of the forty is a lie about a dead character, and when he speaks it aloud in the wings the stage goes on without him and he wakes on the bench with a Friday wage strike waiting. |
| 205 | Seat Fifty-One, Reissued | intermediate | medium | A subscriber takes the wrong face in the wrong costume, works out what he is looking at, and says so to nobody, and is not thrown out at all — nothing in the theatre permits that. Instead his seat is resold in the middle of the interval, his subscription is cancelled at the counter, and the new holder of seat fifty-one walks in during the second act. A procedural story about paperwork, quietly observed, in which the only frightening moment is a signature. |
| 206 | The Empty Chair at Act Three | intermediate | long | Three members of the corps refuse in the same house on the same night, and Rule 8 arrives: the house lights rise on a deal chair at centre stage, the performance is a failure, and the season closes. What follows is the longest forty minutes of Nell Tarrant's life, in which nobody is villainous, everybody is reasonable, and the corps learns exactly what it costs to be right, and then six of eight stand up. |
| 207 | What the Text Does Not Say | advanced | short | The Position of the Younger Brother has begun to require things the play does not contain: a limp, a childhood, a way of holding a door. Bram has been inventing them nightly for four years, and inventing has become part of the Figure, and part of the Figure is now in the book, and the book may not be edited. The story is the discovery of that, made in front of an audience, and the terrible arithmetic of knowing you have already lost. |
| 208 | The Season of Small Print | advanced | medium | Ilse Karrow is asked, by a clerk from a rival house, to confirm that a Position is a contract; and confirming it is easy, because she has never read the stage version. The investigation goes back through the book, and through a hundred and forty seasons of entries, and finds that the theatre has been filling Positions since it was built: not only the ones on the programme, but the ones in the audience, and the ones in the office, and the one in the book itself. Nothing supernatural happens and the last page is still in her gloved hand. |
| 209 | The Night of the Whole Bench | advanced | long | On the last night of the season every member of the corps refuses before the curtain, which is the only way an entry can ever be struck, and the Season Book obliges them by closing. The house of four hundred and twelve people watches the stage stay dark and the cast walk out in their coats, and every subscriber in the building is refunded in full, which means everyone in the story loses everything they had. The resolution is not a victory but a hand-over: the theatre goes to the company as a building, with the Positions still written down, and somebody has to be first into the dark. |

## Thematic Spine

All nine stories are about the difference between a person and the description of a person, and about who is owed the truth when the description is what pays the bills. The accessible level treats it as recognition — a lamp that needs trimming, a coat that fits the wrong body, a man who finally says the number out loud. The intermediate level treats it as consequence — what refusal costs, on a Friday, in a strike written in a clerk's hand, to the seven people who did go on. The advanced level treats it as ownership — who the text belongs to once the text has been performed long enough by somebody who is not in it, and whether a body can be freed by a rule the rule-makers never intended to write.
