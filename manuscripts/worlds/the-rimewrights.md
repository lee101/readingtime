# World: The Rimewrights

On the Coldmark terraces a guild of coldwrights does not wait for winter — it sows it. Frost is sown into prepared ground in autumn like barley, it is drawn down into granite voids cut into the hillside, and it is sold at the Frostgate by the yard, in bars, in rink-plates, in whole winters of stored cold for anyone who can pay the assay grade. The world is fur, iron, lamplight and breath; a good day is one where the sledges run and the assay office is warm enough to argue in. And this year the harvest is failing — the sown ground is coming up thin and warm, the voids are drawing down slow, and the guild's own Assay Book, which records every degree and every grade to two decimal places for two hundred years, says the harvest should be the best in living memory.

## Setting

The Coldmark is a grey shoulder of granite above a long frozen river valley in the far north country, where the sun clears the ridge for four hours a day in midwinter and the daylight is the colour of watered tin. The settlement is a working one: eleven long low stone houses of split grey granite, roofs weighted with iron ballast against the wind, every one of them with a shuttered air-well cut into the north face and a lamp burning in it from Michaelmas to Candlemas. The Guildhall is the only building with glass in it, a two-storey block of dressed stone with a copper-capped assay chimney and a yard in front where the sample blocks sit on pallets under a tarpaulin. Frost is not weather here, it is stock: it stands in a barrow, it is weighed at the gate, it is invoiced.

Winter food is what you can keep: salt mutton, turnip, barley, cloudberry jam, seal oil rendered in the dark, and a hot bitter drink the wrights brew from boiled barley and rime-ash that tastes of a stove. Cattle do not survive a Coldmark winter outside the wall, so almost everything moves on sled and runner: freight sledge, flat sled, hand barrow, and a single narrow-gauge line the guild built to bring void-stone down from the quarry terraces. Market day is the Frostgate, held on the second market of each month, and it looks like an ordinary northern market until you notice that half the stalls are selling cold — stacked rink-plates wrapped in sacking, bar-rows of seeded ground sold like seed corn, a hired winter sold by the season. Everything is written down. Every stall holder has a grade card nailed to the barrow and a brass disc on a string; the disc is stamped with a number and the number is the whole argument.

## The Magic

1. **Cold can be sown, but only into prepared ground.** A rimewright breaks ground to a depth of three feet with a heated iron share, lays rime-needle in the furrow at a stated grains-per-yard, and closes it. Sown ground will not lie. It holds a sown cold through any mild winter, and it comes up thin and warm if the seeding is short.
2. **Sown cold has a yield, and the yield is the harvest.** Cold comes up out of ground in late winter as a standing cold over roughly one square yard per pound of seed. Nothing else comes up with it. A thin harvest is a real, measurable, physical shortage, not a mood.
3. **Cold can be stored in stone, but only in a cut void.** A void is a worked granite cavity, lined with black iron and sealed with a frost-plate. A sealed void holds its cold indefinitely with no loss. A void with any crack in its frost-plate bleeds its charge into the hill and must be recharged by hand.
4. **A void cannot be carried.** Cold in a void is fixed to the place it was cut. This is why the guild owns the terraces and why every plot has a family name cut into the lintel above the frost-plate.
5. **Cold is sold by the yard, and selling it is the only way it can be moved.** The guild's trade is the transfer of cold from a place a person does not live to a place they do. Every yard sold is a yard that somebody else's ground will not get this season.
6. **The Assay Book records what is, never why.** Two parallel columns: degrees, and grade. Degrees are measured and undisputed. Grade — first, second, third, outturn — is assigned by the assay clerk and written down, and a grade is a decision about who gets the first of it, recorded in a column with a heading and no place for a reason.
7. **The limit: no cold may be made.** There is no lesser, cheaper, cleverer way to produce cold. The only question a rimewright has ever had is how much was sown, where, and whose.
8. **The cost is sleep and body heat.** A wright standing in a live void or a charging furrow loses feeling in the face and hands, and a season of it costs them roughly a decade of sleep afterwards. Wrights retire early, badly, and quietly, and the guild has a pension fund that is chronically late.
9. **Cold will not take a claim it is not given.** A yard of cold that is charged, stored, and sold without a name against it is called orphan cold: it bleeds out of the hill within a season. The guild's rule is that every void has a name on it, and the exception is always an old practice and always someone's fault.
10. **Breaking the rules does not explode. It just stops being true.** A cracked frost-plate does not punish anyone; the charge goes quietly into the mountain and the assay office writes it off as loss, and the loss is always attributed to the last person whose name was nearest the plate. The magic is indifferent; the ledger is not.

## Cast

**Halla Rimewright**, 47, guild assay clerk, twenty-two years at the desk. *Want:* to be right, in writing, at the end of her career, in a book that will still be open in twenty years. *Flaw:* she has signed grade lines for twenty-two years without once asking what the grade column is for, and she knows exactly what it is for. *Detail:* a split thumbnail on her right forefinger, kept split deliberately so she can turn a page in a cold room without warming her hand, and a shallow groove across the pad of the same finger. *Speech habit:* states numbers out loud before she states anything else — "Fourteen. Eleven. That is a difference."

**Bram Ostmark**, 34, wright and seam-holder for the Ostmark ground, third generation. *Want:* to make his own ground the first cut this year, which it has not been in his lifetime. *Flaw:* the Ostmark void is the finest cut on the hill, and he keeps sealing its plate during the thaw because the older men showed him, and he cannot say where he learned it. *Detail:* a fur-seam across his right eyebrow, healed white, and a scoring of salt-frost scars on both palms like a rope burn. *Speech habit:* answers questions with a measurement, never a yes or no.

**Ilva Sorne**, 29, frost-plate smith, works the Iron Row of the Guildhall yard. *Want:* to be let in on the design of the new plate alloy, which two senior smiths are keeping from her on grounds of seniority. *Flaw:* she can read a plate for cracks by eye and a ledger for lies by eye, and she trusts the second more than the first. *Detail:* burn-scarred forearms, and a habit of holding cold things in a folded square of oiled wool, never bare-handed. *Speech habit:* says "that is not a crack, that is a plate" when someone points out a flaw.

**Coen Halloway**, 12, errand at the Frostgate, son of the wrights' row of cottages. *Want:* to be allowed inside the assay office, and specifically to be allowed to touch the Book, which is fenced off from children with a rail and a notice. *Flaw:* he can read the degrees column perfectly and believes, sincerely and for years, that the numbers will tell him who is lying. *Detail:* a wool scarf wound twice and tucked, so long that its end has to be stepped on, and chapped lips. *Speech habit:* reads numbers aloud to himself in the street, softly, and gets annoyed at anyone who corrects him.

**Sura Vane**, 61, pension clerk, one of the guild's nine minor officers, holds the wrights' pension book. *Want:* the fund to be solvent before the spring committee so that the twelve oldest wrights get paid in one go. *Flaw:* she has been advancing small sums from the fund for eleven years and documenting them correctly in a separate column, and the column is the only reason she still sleeps. *Detail:* reading spectacles on a broad beaded cord, and one hand kept permanently in her coat because of an old plate burn. *Speech habit:* answers every question with the date, exactly, including questions that were not about dates.

**Old Ansel Rime**, 74, retired wright, unsold labourer now, the last man who cut a void by hand. *Want:* to be asked, before he dies, whether the terraces are still true. *Flaw:* he has told the founding story in nine different versions and is not sure which of them happened, only that the Book's numbers all agree with the worst one. *Detail:* a white beard, and the tops of all ten fingers gone blue and shiny with old cold-burn. *Speech habit:* tells a story, then stops and adds, "but the numbers say the other thing, and the numbers are never wrong."

## Places

**The Guildhall and Assay Office.** A two-storey block of dressed grey granite with the only glass in the Coldmark, an iron stair, and a copper-capped chimney that steams in the coldest weeks. Inside, the Assay Book lies on a long table in a railed alcove, chained to a frame, with a lamp on a swing-arm over each page; the room is held at minus nine all winter and the clerks work in fur coats and mitts with the pens tied to the table.

**The Frostgate.** The market square at the foot of the terraces: bare granite setts, one lamp post, a weigh-beam on a stone pier, and a hundred barrows of sacking-wrapped cold. Sellers wear grade cards on their chests and brass assay discs on strings, and the whole business of the place is conducted by reading the numbers off each other.

**Voidstone Terrace.** The upper quarry, eleven stepped benches cut into the mountain, each bench a line of black iron frost-plates let into the granite face with a family name above each. Charging day is a business: a full line of plates hissing and steaming in the cold, men in soaked sealskins, and a smell of iron so strong you can taste it in your teeth.

**Rime Barrow Nine.** The working seed ground, a flat four-acre field of ridged grey earth drilled for sowing every autumn, the only place in the Coldmark where a person can stand on cold that has not been assigned to anybody yet. A frozen furrow here sounds like a plank walked on with a hammer, and the smell of it is a dry mineral cold with no snow in it.

**The Empty Tenant Plot.** Number Eleven on the Sorne line, a walled rectangle of untouched ground in the middle of a sown field, three seasons ago. Nothing in it has ever come up, the ground is bare in a way that is not a late spring, and the wrights will not drive past the gate; they go round by the ditch.

**The Retirees Bench.** A stone bench along the Guildhall's south wall, out of the wind, where old wrights sit in a line from noon and are not asked to move. The Guildhall has no other seating, which was an accident of the build, and has been the most important room in the Coldmark for thirty years.

## Visual Style

A northern mining-and-farming palette of wet granite grey, lichen black-green, iron blue, and the two accents that matter: rime-needle white and lamp gold. Lighting is low, overcast, and unforgiving — four hours of tin-coloured sun in midwinter, otherwise flat blue ambient with a lamp making a small hot circle at chest height, breath always visible. Materials are split granite, black iron, frozen sacking, salted leather, sealskin, oiled wool, and frost the colour and texture of sugar on a window. The era reads late-northern, pre-industrial, hand-and-fired: hand plows, sledge freight, nailed timber, no engines, no glass, no shiny anything. The single recurring motif, reused across every cover in this world, is a barrow of seeded grey ground on a white field, with one furrow open and one lamp behind it.

## Motifs

1. **The Assay Book**, chained open on its frame, brass assay disc resting in the gutter of the page like a paperweight.
2. **The frost-plate**, black iron let into granite, a family name cut in the lintel above it, and the hiss of it charging.
3. **The grade card** worn on the chest, with its number, at a market.
4. **The furrow**, opened with a heated iron share, three feet deep, in a field that is not white yet and will be.
5. **Chapped hands and split nails**, the visible price of the work, in every person who stands in a live void.

## Thematic Spine

All nine stories are about cold that is made rather than fallen, and therefore about the fact that making it puts somebody at the end of it — and about the paperwork that lets a harvest fail quietly for three seasons while everybody looks at a column of numbers. The accessible band treats that as attention: notice the disc, notice the crack, notice the empty plot. The intermediate band treats it as responsibility: whose sheet, whose fund, whose pen. The advanced band treats it as the meaning of a grade, which is that a table of arithmetic is also a queue, and a queue has an order, and the order was chosen by somebody.
