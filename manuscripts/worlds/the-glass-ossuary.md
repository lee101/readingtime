# World: The Glass Ossuary

In the valley of Vesset Grail, the dead are not buried. They are gathered into blown glass bells and hung in a nine-bay colonnade on the north side of the village, and the wind that comes down the grail in the evening is what sounds them, so that the dead are the one thing in the valley that can be overheard. Sixty-one bells hang between the pale limestone pillars, each one holding a thimbleful of grey ash and a name cut into a lead seal on its crown, each one tuned to the last thing its person said aloud in the valley before they died. Daily life in Coldrow is therefore an exercise in not listening: shuttered windows on the windward side, bread dough proved under a cloth, arguments held in the lee of a pillar, and a bell that has begun sounding a note nobody in the valley has ever spoken.

## Setting

The valley of Vesset Grail is a cold limestone trough four miles long, walled by grey rock with thin scrub, with the stream, the Grail, running its length and freezing in a bad winter. The village of Coldrow sits on the lee side so the wind crosses the roofs before it reaches the colonnade, which stands alone on the north strip: nine bays, sixty-one pale blue-green bells, each the height of a person, hung on wrought-iron yokes between fluted pillars of local stone. Buildings are low, limewashed, shuttered, and shingled in grey larch.

Three institutions govern everything. The Crown Kiln, a low furnace hall east of the stream, blows every glass bell in the valley and refuses to blow a second one. The Chart House, a small stone room at the colonnade end with a slate floor, holds the tuner rota on a whitewashed wall. The Ash Yard, down by the Washing Steps, is where a name is certified dead before anything is blown.

Food is barley, oatcakes, hard cheese, a sour cream soup, smoked eel, and hedge honey. Everything tastes of cold stone and smoke. Transport is a cart, a handbarrow, and a two-wheeled ash sled; nobody from outside the valley comes in winter, and the far end of the colonnade is reachable only along the pillar walk.

A market day is the first Thursday after the first hard frost. The Chart House courtyard fills with capped and shawled people carrying jars, and a boy-tuner stands in the middle of it, holds up a slate, reads the wind direction out loud, and the crowd stays quiet for exactly as long as it takes him to finish. Then the yard makes the noise of a market again, and everybody pretends they were not just listening for the note.

## The Magic

1. **The dead are kept in glass, not in ground.** When a person dies in the valley, the ash reader certifies the name, the ash yard burns and sieves the body, and the Crown Kiln blows a bell with the ash dusted into the molten gather. One bell, one person. The bell is signed with a lead seal on the crown carrying a name and a rota number. There is no ground in the valley; there is a kiln.
2. **A bell holds one thing: the last sentence its person said aloud in the valley, and only as pitch.** Not the words — the words died with the body. What the glass kept is the *tune* of that last sentence, the exact interval the mouth made, ground into the lip by the blower's judgement. A bell can say only one thing and can only say it as a note.
3. **Only wind sounds a bell.** A bell hung in the colonnade sounds when the wind takes the valley's evening angle through the nine bays, and a bell struck by hand makes no sound at all. No living person can call the dead, ring them, silence them, or ask them anything. This is the law the whole valley rests on, and it is why the rota matters so much.
4. **A bell may be re-pitched once, and the pitch is bought with the words.** To re-pitch, the crown is opened and the lip ground. A re-pitched bell keeps the tone and loses the last sentence for ever; the last sentence is ground away with the glass. No bell is re-pitched a second time. Re-pitching is a legal, dated, minuted act with the ash reader and two witnesses.
5. **The rota is the only instrument that exists.** The tuner rota is a wall chart in the Chart House: a column for the date, a column for the bay, a column for the bell, a column for the ringer, and a column for who is called. It has no column for who is in the bell. The chart can therefore record a ringer perfectly and cannot record whether that ringer is standing in the courtyard or hanging in the colonnade, and every consequence in this world comes out of that missing column.
6. **The cost is a sound out of your own life.** Every time a person hears a bell and hears it *correctly* — recognises the note as a person, not as weather — the valley takes one sound out of that listener for good. A bird call, a stair creak, a name spoken once. The valley is full of humming adults and near-silent children, and the taking is fair, silent, and never announced.
7. **The limit is one note and no instruction.** A bell cannot warn, cannot name the living, cannot answer a question, cannot say a name. It can only repeat, once per evening wind, the pitch of the sentence it holds. Anything that seems to be a message is the listener's own ear finishing the job.
8. **Answering a bell with the same note is how a voice gets taken.** If a living person sings or speaks the pitch a bell is sounding, the bell takes that voice, and the person can no longer produce it — and will be credited with the last sentence in the bell. This is how the Chart House always knows. It is also, over four hundred years, how a handful of living people came to be listed among the dead.
9. **Break a bell and you pay for the valley.** A bell that is cracked, dropped, or struck on the lip rings one unbroken loud note heard by everyone in the valley at once, and the sound of the person who broke it is taken the same hour. The Crown Kiln will not re-blow a cracked bell. It stays hanging, empty, silent, and listed.

## Cast

**Ione Sarrack**, 58, Head Tuner of Vesset Grail, keeper of the rota. Wants: for the chart to be exact for the whole of her remaining tenure, exact in every column, with no gap in it. Flaw: she re-pitched a bell once, thirty-one years ago, and can no longer remember which, and has organised forty years of meticulous record-keeping around not finding out. Physical detail: a slate-grey coat buttoned to the throat with three ball-shaped black buttons, a long plait of grey hair pinned in a coil, one bare right hand kept warm inside her sleeve, and chalk dust on the pads of her fingers that never washes off. Verbal habit: states the date before she states anything else — "Fourth of Sleetmonth, and no —" — and finishes an instruction with "Write it where it stands."

**Bela Truce**, 34, blower at the Crown Kiln, third generation in the glasshouse. Wants: to blow one bell that will outlast every bell she has made, and to hear it sound true. Flaw: she cannot read numbers at all, and keeps the entire valley's rota in her head as wax-marks on the annealing rack, which is why the Chart House has never once fully trusted her. Physical detail: a leather apron burnt shiny at the front, forearms shining with sweat and pale with fine glass dust, one ear reddened from forty years beside the furnace mouth, and a long grey streak in her black hair where a gather dripped. Verbal habit: answers questions about the rota by naming a mark instead of a number — "That is the fourth from the mouth, the one with the split wax."

**Marget Oskell**, 63, ash reader and certifier, the only person who may sign a name into glass. Wants: never again to certify a name she has not seen with her own eyes twice. Flaw: she has certified one name wrong and does not know which one, and she has been quietly re-reading the seal ledgers for six years looking for it. Physical detail: a heavy black apron worn white at the knee, spectacles on a knotted grey cord, burnt fingertips stained permanently smoke-brown, and a shallow tin of dry ash she carries everywhere and taps with her thumbnail when she is thinking. Verbal habit: asks "Have you seen it twice?" and accepts no other answer, and counts her words on her fingers when she is frightened.

**Ansel Roke**, 49, schoolmaster and compiler of the Register of the Living, a list of everyone in the valley who is not dead. Wants: a document that would settle, once and for all, that no living person is hung in the colonnade. Flaw: he compiles the list, so the list can only ever agree with him, and he knows it, and has gone on compiling it for nineteen years. Physical detail: a long black coat with ink at both cuffs, a green-shouldered satchel stuffed with rolled paper, a blue ink stain along the side of his right hand, and spectacles pushed up on his head while he thinks. Verbal habit: says "It stands in the record as follows —" before he tells you anything, and adds "which is not the same as true" if you press him.

**Perrin Vole**, 14, an errand-runner and self-appointed junior tuner, three years on the rota as a winder's mate. Wants: to be called in the wind, to have a column of his own, to be a name the valley says out loud. Flaw: he hums constantly and has begun, without noticing, to hum the notes the bells sound. Physical detail: a coat four sizes too large with the sleeves rolled four times, a permanently chapped red nose, one bare foot through a hole in a boot sole, and his own name scratched into the Chart House doorframe where he could reach. Verbal habit: repeats the last three words somebody said to him, then answers them — "Called at dusk, and yes?"

**Tamsin Quill**, 16, an errand girl of the Ash Yard, and the person the new note belongs to. Wants: to be proved, on paper, by somebody else, that she is alive. Flaw: she was certified dead eleven years ago on a bad seal, and since the new note began she can hear the colonnade all day long whether she is in the valley or not. Physical detail: a grey shawl over a green dress, hair cut at the jaw and uneven because she cut it herself, a broad strip of cloth wrapped round her right hand and forearm that she never removes, and a shallow crescent scar at the edge of her left eyebrow. Verbal habit: answers questions a half-beat late, and when she is startled she hums — one note, always the same one.

## Places

**The Colonnade of Nine Bays.** Sixty-one pale blue-green glass bells, each the height of a person, hung on wrought-iron yokes between nine bays of fluted local limestone, standing alone on the north strip of the valley floor. The paving beneath is worn into shallow bowls by four hundred years of people who came to stand under a particular bell and were careful where they put their feet.

**The Chart House.** A small stone room at the south end of the colonnade with a slate floor, one north window, one long table, and the rota on the limewashed east wall in ruled black chalk: date, bay, bell, ringer, called. A courtyard of packed gravel opens south of it, and the yard is the only place in the valley where nine hundred people can stand and hear nothing happen.

**The Crown Kiln.** A low furnace hall of firebrick and soot east of the stream, with three annealing racks, a water trough, a wall of cooling bells laid out in rows like sleeping animals, and one gather swinging on the end of a blowpipe. The floor is a black glass crust underfoot, and the light inside is the orange of a foundry, not of a lamp.

**The Ash Yard.** A walled enclosure of pale stone and larch board down by the Washing Steps, with a long brick kiln-smoke flue, a set of graduated brass sieves, a bench of sealing tools, and lead blanks and a hand punch on a leather apron. Ash from every bell the valley has ever blown is banked here under a corrugated lid, and it is the warmest place in Coldrow in February.

**The Washing Steps.** Nine broad weirs of dressed stone stepping down the Grail, running edge to edge across the stream, with the water going glassy and green over them. In thaw they roar and drown out the colonnade; in hard frost they carry the whole wind along the valley floor and hand it to the bays.

**The Pillar Walk.** The flagged route behind the colonnade, a kilometre long, from the Chart House to the far end of the ninth bay, with a low wall on the valley side and a rail of black iron on the colonnade side, and nobody's window opening onto it. It is the only way to reach the end of the colonnade on foot, and it is where wind and hand and weather are most worth measuring.

## Visual Style

Cold north-light limestone and blown pale sea-glass green: bone white, wet slate, ash grey, with one deep madder-red accent in a lining, a door, or a scarf, and warm orange reserved for the kiln interior alone. Light is a hard low sun in narrow shafts through the colonnade, full of suspended glass dust, and cold blue shadow everywhere else; the courtyard reads in single echoes, one voice out of time. Materials are honest and abrasive — unwashed limestone, wrought iron gone to orange bloom, tarred hemp line, larch shingle, brass sieves, soot on every hand. The era feel is a working agricultural valley at the tail of a craft economy: no glass decoration, one furnace, one chart, one kiln, and four hundred years of the same arguments. The recurring motif is the hanging bell itself — a bell-shaped mass of cold light and fine green dust, always half a tone from being an answer.

## Motifs

- The **lead seal**: a lead disc on every bell crown, one name and one rota number, punched by hand, slightly crooked.
- The **rota wall**: five ruled columns of black chalk on whitewashed plaster, and the six inches of blank wall to the right of the last column where the missing one would go.
- **Glass dust**: fine green powder on the sill, the anvil, a sleeve, a loaf of bread, hanging in every sunbeam in the colonnade.
- The **hand-bell** on the Chart House table: a plain little tuning bell that makes no sound when struck, kept because it is useful for nothing and therefore believed.
- The **brass sieves** of the Ash Yard, in a graduated row, and the grey ash in the palm of a hand that will not wash out.

## Thematic Spine

All nine stories are quietly about the difference between a record and a fact, and about what happens to a community that has arranged itself so that love, duty and grief all flow through a chart with a blank space in it. The accessible level takes the listening shape: something is sounding that should not be, and the work is finding out which bell and which name. The intermediate level takes the procedure shape: lawful acts, signed and minuted and witnessed, grinding a human being into a legal object one column at a time. The advanced level takes the institutional shape: an audit of a valley that cannot audit itself, and the discovery that the missing column is not an oversight but the arrangement.
