# World: The Tide Library

The Tide Library stands on eighty-one iron stilts on the Sarn Shelf, a black sand flat two miles wide that the sea returns to every seventh night. The people of the coast have stopped calling that a disaster: they light lamps early, tie their doors, and go up to the Ridge Road to watch the water come in. Between floods the Library is the dry, hush-hushed heart of the place, and its only real business is lending. It has three hundred years of borrowers in its Night Ledger, and it has never before tried to keep a person. It is keeping them now, one page at a time, and the eleven Rooms have never held so much. The books have noticed. They are slower to swim home this year, and one small green book has learned to stay dry in the dark.

## Setting

The Sarn Shelf is a drowned coastline: black sand ribbed by every tide, threaded with bleached whale ribs and the stumps of an older village. The nearest working town is Greyhaven, three miles up the stone, a huddle of slate roofs and chimney-smoke, and its market day is the morning after a flood night, when the square smells of salt-bitten boots. The Library itself is a low, black-basalt building raised on stilts, roofed in oiled slate, with seven shallow steps up from the flat and a porch lintel rubbed pale by three centuries of shoulders. Inside there is one long corridor called the Spine, a gutter running its whole length, and eleven numbered Rooms opening off it: the Quire, the Atlas Room, the Press, the Nests, the Stacks, the Herbarium, the Maps, the Biography, the Servery, and the Gallery. The stairs are steep, the doors are low, and the building smells of wet wool, cold iron, and old dust. The highest reading room is the Servery, which holds no books at all: a scarred amber table, storm lanterns on hooks, a singing kettle, a red-silk wall bell, and racks of tea tins worn down to their labels. The lowest room is the mud Shelf, which is a bookcase on the ebb and a drowning on the flood. Paper is expensive here, so lamps burn low, and the last page of a wet book is never thrown away: it is rung up on a line behind the Porch like washing.

## The Magic

1. **Reading makes a watercopy.** When a person reads inside the Tide Library, at the moment a page turns, the library takes a *watercopy* of them: not a likeness but a part, held in the very book they were reading, made of ink that lifts off damp paper and will not lift again. To lose a watercopy you must have given a great deal into reading — a person loved, a great fear carried, a song known only to them, a whole secret — and the part taken is always exactly that weight.
2. **No one can make a watercopy. The book does it.** Librarians cannot, parents cannot, the Library does not obey requests. The only way one exists is to read here, unguarded, past the turning of a page.
3. **A watercopy cannot be crossed out.** Tearing out the leaf, burning the book, or throwing it in the sea does not unmake it. It passes to the next volume, or the next Room, and it goes on asking after the person by name.
4. **The tide never breaks its side of the bargain.** It borrows, and it always returns. The Library keeps; the water only moves books. Nobody is drowned, nobody is killed, nothing is broken that was not already being read. Every threat in this world is a social one.
5. **The cost is a part, and the limit is a whole.** Ten small watercopies barely change a life; a ninth part, a tenth, a fifteenth leaves a person correct in every detail and unable to remember why any of it mattered. Most keepers go waxy around the eyes long before anyone notices the shelves filling.
6. **Warding is the only counter.** Spindles (the hot green lamp that eats ink), burnt paper-ash, salt, and cold stone will *blind* a watercopy. Scrape it, salt it, burn it, cut it away, and the person walks out whole and the leaf is blank and the page is fine. This is why the Quire is lined with scraped spines.
7. **You may not ask a person to be a book.** If someone offers themselves to the Library aloud, meaning it, the offer is accepted, and the first price is the voice: the asker never speaks aloud again.
8. **Every seventh night the library grows a Higher Stair.** One step appears somewhere in the building, a step no harder than any other, and it is gone by morning — whether or not anyone takes it. It is how the Library makes keepers, and it is why the Quire exists at all.
9. **A watercopy can be read dry.** Once, in a lifetime, a reader may go down the ebb in one pair of shoes — dry, ordinary, unremarked — by reading their own watercopy aloud, all of it, in their own voice. This is the single exception to Rule 5, and it is also the door. Which is why the Servery is watched, and why the Borrower Wray Imbrie is watched, and why the last of the nine stories is the most dangerous thing in this world.

## Cast

**Keeper Anja Roe**, 61, thirty-four years the senior keeper of the Tide Library. Wants: to be the last keeper who ever had to keep anybody. Flaw: she has read her way through most of a life and can no longer picture her mother's face, and does not admit this even to herself. Physical detail: a heavy black cassock gone stiff and salt-bleached at the hem, a right hand permanently in a blackened wool glove, a hood that has slipped back and stays there. Verbal habit: begins every dispute with "Now then." and closes every story with "Ink dries. Go on."

**Reader Niko Sarran**, 17, unofficial reader of the Servery, born in Greyhaven, reads standing up. Wants: to read every book in the building before he has to decide what to be. Flaw: he is certain that reading is the one thing that cannot hurt anyone, and he is the reason it is. Physical detail: a sea-blue knitted jumper with the cuffs permanently rolled, a wet navy streak of ink across his left palm that has not washed out in a year. Verbal habit: asks questions out loud — "But who decided that?" — and never waits for the answer before asking the next one.

**The Borrower Wray Imbrie**, 34, the only living reader whose watercopy is complete. Wants: to keep walking out of the Library and to be left entirely alone about it. Flaw: he has arranged his whole life around the Rule of One, and any plan with two people in it frightens him more than the Library does. Physical detail: a long dark wool coat with tarnished copper buttons, a long red scarf, ink permanently stained into his right cuff. Verbal habit: says "Say the term and I'll sign." before he will discuss anything at all.

**Wick**, about ten, a library thing of soaked pages, drowned through and readable through, the last book to swim home. Wants: with total sincerity, to be returned to a shelf. Flaw: not a thing of substance yet, and cannot be protected from its own making. Physical detail: a soft-edged boy-shaped outline of pasted wet paper, a small keyhole burned black in the chest, water standing in the shape of him and never falling off. Verbal habit: a paper-rasp whisper, three words and no more, never a finished sentence — "Found you. Good. Book."

**Sister Anwen Tarn**, 29, of the House, in charge of warding: scraping, salting, cutting, burning. Wants: to empty the Quire before the Night Ledger fills. Flaw: she counts everything she touches — pages, people, days — and has quietly begun to count herself, in chalk, on her own cuff. Physical detail: a sea-grey habit with dark blue cuffs, a short grey cloak, a small steel key ring, one permanently cold blue-white palm. Verbal habit: speaks softly and counts out loud before she opens a door — "One. Two. Three."

**Archivist Perran Hesk**, 45, keeper of the Maps Room, later Keeper of the Quire. Wants: the Library's record to be exact, and the exactness to be useful to somebody. Flaw: he believes a thing written down properly is safe, and keeps writing things down. Physical detail: a salt-white coat lined in red, one boot always re-laced with green twine, a satchel with a square-cornered atlas, spectacles on a green string. Verbal habit: dates everything — "It's Thursday. And you're in the mud."

## Places

**The Tide Library, from the Porch.** A low black-basalt building raised on iron stilts, oiled-slate roof, seven shallow stone steps, a porch lintel rubbed pale, a door of green-black wood with a brass knob and no lock. In flood weather the water stands at the third step and the whole building sits in its own reflection, doubled, upside down.

**The Spine.** One long low corridor with a shallow gutter running its whole length, dark walls, a row of hanging storm lanterns, and iron handrails gone orange with rust. It is the coldest, quietest, best-smelling room in the building: wet wool, cold iron, old dust, candle smoke.

**The Quire.** The warding room, hidden behind the Numbers Room and deliberately unnumbered, containing four hundred scraped spines in a long line and a slate one-name ledger on a lectern. The floor is worn into shallow slate bowls by four hundred years of careful feet, and the air is hot and dry from the spindle lamp and stinks of ash and salt.

**The Atlas Room.** The widest Room, with the long oak table, the great terrestrial globe on its brass meridian, charts on every wall, and the tall barred window the tide does not reach. It is the only place in the Library where the rain does not sound, and where the floor stays level.

**The Servery.** The highest reading room and the only one with no books: a scarred amber-oak table, six storm lanterns on hooks, a kettle that sings, a red-silk wall bell, a rack of tea tins. A slate on the wall lists the Rules of One, and the Rule of One has been scratched out underneath it.

**The Gallery.** A narrow upper room on the far side, lined with sea chests, press-marks, and long ledgers, lit by one shuttered lantern. Two spillways drop from it to the mud Shelf, boarded but not well, and the highest shelf carries a single empty frame.

**The Roof.** The flat slate roof above the high-water line, with the Night Bell in an iron cradle, the lightning vane, and the whole black flat laid out west of it under the moon. Dry, windy, and the only place you can see how far the water has come.

**The Verandah.** The open south-facing deck along the low side of the building, with white railings, green bench seats, and a long run of sixty-one steps from the Verandah up to the upper Stair. The lantern hooks along it are all dark on the morning after a flood, every one, and no one can say who put them out.

**The Mud Shelf.** The lowest tier, an unnumbered Room opening straight onto the flat through a wooden hatch, its shelves standing in two inches of silt. At low water the finest reading room in the world; at the turn, the water reaches it first and last, and it holds the Library's oldest and wettest books.

## Visual Style

Pre-industrial, salt-eaten, low-lit: black wet basalt and grey-green slate, verdigris copper, oiled canvas, tarred rope, black sand, and wool in every colour the coast can dye — madder red, ochre, indigo, undyed grey. The light is low and warm against cold: storm lanterns, a spindle lamp burning green, one tall window full of blue rain-light, and moonlight lying flat white across a mirror of water. Materials are honest and worn — iron gone to orange rust, pewter, wax, salt crust, waxed paper, blistered pine. The era feel is a working port library that never got rich: no glass, no gilding beyond gilt on spines, one stair too narrow for two people. The recurring motif is the tidemark, a pale salt line at exactly the same height on every stone, post, boot, cheek, and wall — a tide that is a line, a boundary, and a ruler.

## Motifs

- The **green book**: small, a gilt capital **G** on the spine, no title — the one volume that stays dry when the water comes.
- The **tidemark line**: a pale salt line at the same height on every surface, boot, cheek, and stone.
- The **spindle lamp**: a green glass lamp that eats ink; the smell of hot ash and salt; the sound of a blade on a wet page.
- The **Night Bell** and the seventh night, with its empty iron cradle going on ringing over water.
- **Salt**: in the white bowl on every sill, in the sea chests, on the Ledger page, in the air, on people's sleeves.

## Thematic Spine

All nine stories are quietly about the difference between what a person owns and what they have merely been read into, a difference that feels like nothing at all until it is the only thing left to lose. The accessible level takes the grief shape: things you cannot remember saying are being said in your voice while you are not speaking. The intermediate level takes the bargain shape: reasonable rules, offered by someone kind, accepted in small payments, until the total comes to a person. The advanced level takes the ledger shape: who wrote the first rule, who is permitted to write the next one, and what a library is actually for.
