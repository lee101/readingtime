# World: The Gilded Menagerie

The Gilded Menagerie is a travelling circus that runs on contracts, candour, and feathers. Every performer who signs with the Director signs away their name and receives, in return, a body made of the animal they most resemble inside — a lion for the proud, a sparrow for the quick and eager, a fox for the sly and watchful. They are billed by their animal, they live in cages on wheels, they perform two nights in every town, and they are, on the whole, happier than they were before. The wonderful thing is that the magic works at all. The wrong thing is that it has stopped stopping. Contracts that used to hold a performer at half-transform, human enough to talk between shows, are quietly deepening: more mane, more feather, more tooth. The circus is quieter each season than the last, because the circus is running out of people to become.

## Setting

A temperate river country called the Tansey, worked in early autumn through high summer's dust. The circus travels one road only — the Salt Line, a raised causeway of packed chalk between drainage ditches — so the whole world is always either arriving or leaving, which is why showfolk never say *here* and always say *on the road*. Cities are grey stone and brick; the Salt Line towns are chalk-white, thirsty, and proud of their one enormous tree. Climate: dry summers with dust in the seams of everything, wet mild springs, and a first week of fog in the small hours that the gasjets turn to gold.

Settlements on the road: Harrow's Landing, where the circus winters in the old brick ropeworks; Pettifer Bridge; the fairground dorm town of Nine Elms; and Saltcombe, a seaside terminus with a pier the menagerie uses to unload its wagons. Architecture is travelling architecture — canvas, hoop-poles, painted board, brass — but the winter quarters are heavy, riveted, soot-coloured Victorian sheds with tin roofs and no windows at eye level. The Big Top is sixty feet of unbleached canvas and a forest of gas pipe.

Food is honest and mostly fried: mutton in dripping, chipped potatoes, yesterday's bread turned to rissoles, tea so strong it stains the cup, and, once a season, a whole lamb turned on a spit in the fairground for the townspeople. Beer travels in barrels; the clowns drink broth and complain.

Transport is horse-drawn. Eighteen wagons plus the Big Top, moved at night over the Salt Line by teams of heavy draught animals, six of whom are on paper as horses. A market day at Harrow's Landing in October: nine a.m. the clowns in their paint going house to house with handbills, the Rope Tights out by noon, the band tuning in the square, a queue at the ticket booth that goes around the obelisk, the smell of trodden sawdust, hot iron, spilled beer, and eight thousand people deciding they will come to the evening show. Six of those eight thousand are the circus.

## The Magic

The magic is contractual and clerical. It obeys the wording, not the intention, and every rule below has been true since the first signature — even when the circus stopped honouring the last two.

1. **A signature is the whole of it.** No ink, no transformation, ever. A contract written in lead pencil, charcoal, jam, or dry leaves is a legal curiosity: it creates an obligation with no mechanism behind it, and it can be argued with, and sometimes lost. This is the only known way to hold a clause still.
2. **The Clause of Resemblance.** The body takes the shape of the animal the signer most resembles in character, not in bloodline. A frightened quick person gets a sparrow. A proud slow one gets a lion. A watcher who is always counting things gets a fox. Resemblance is judged once, at signing, and never re-judged.
3. **The Stop.** A signed performer is meant to remain half-transformed: human in the face, animal in the hands and the gait, able to speak, eat, and be cross with each other backstage. The Stop is held in place by one **Named Keepsake** — the single object the signer brings to the contract and names aloud at the moment of signing. The Keepsake keeps the human half answerable.
4. **The Ledger is the Machine.** The magic does not live in a person or a wagon. It lives in the Contract Ledger kept in the Paper Caravan, and the Bookkeeper's ink is what holds each signature in place or lets it slide. A name not entered in the Ledger is a name that never signed. A name rubbed out of the Ledger is a name nobody is looking for.
5. **The Cost.** Transformation spends memory, and it spends it in the same currency for everyone: the performer loses one human day for every night performed in animal form, and cannot remember that the day happened. A transformed performer is not in pain. They are simply, and increasingly, a person with less and less behind their eyes — which the circus jokingly calls *keeping fit for the season*.
6. **The Limit.** Transformation cannot pass between people. It cannot be given, lent, sold, or inherited while a name is unstruck. Only the Ledger, or the Director's own countersignature, can move a transformation from one person to another. There has never been a way to un-make one.
7. **The Renewal.** A contract may be renewed. A renewal does not reset anything. Every renewal in Clause Nine deepens the transformation by a set, fixed amount, and lengthens the term. This has always been legal. It is also the mechanism of the whole trouble, and every performer who has been renewed more than three times knows it on some animal level they cannot put into words.
8. **The Break.** If a clause is broken — a Keepsake destroyed, a name struck from the Ledger, a contract read aloud where it was not meant to be read — the transformation does not stop. It *accelerates*, and it takes the signer's nearest living relative with it, at one degree removed, in the same night. This has happened twice in the history of the circus. Both times it was legal. Both times it was an accident of wording.
9. **The Warden Rule.** The Director may not sign a performer directly. Every contract is witnessed by the audience — a contract in a Gilded Menagerie tent takes effect only where at least one paying member of the public is close enough to hear the words read. This exists to prevent magic done in private. It is, of course, extremely useful to a Director who knows how to work a crowd.
10. **What nobody writes down.** The oldest contract in the Ledger is dated thirty-one years and bears one signature at the top, above the words *in consideration of the Show*. Nobody in the circus talks about it. It is not in any of the nine stories except the ninth, and it is not a surprise: everyone in the company already suspects and nobody can prove it, which is the reason the whole company is the last line of defence in a very small, very crowded tent.

## Cast

**Nell Ferrule** — 13, apprentice in the ticket booth, three years into a signing she did not understand. **Want:** to get her brother Tomas back from wherever the contracts took him. **Flaw:** she learned her trade by watching the handwriting, not reading the words, and she forged her own way into a signature in a way she still cannot fully describe. **Physical detail:** a length of red thread knotted round her left wrist, knotted over so many times it has formed a small knotted cuff. **Speech tic:** counts out loud when she is frightened — tickets, planks, breaths, steps — and the count sometimes goes past ten and keeps going.

**August Vane** — 34, ringmaster, nephew of the Director, billed in smaller type than he deserves. **Want:** to be loved by his aunt in any form at all, including as a professional. **Flaw:** he admires her out loud, in the ring, in front of the crowd, and calls that a compliment. **Physical detail:** an oiled black top hat with a worn brass band, and a single white glove on his left hand, its cuff threaded with red. **Speech tic:** refers to himself in the third person when he is performing — *August can hold a room; August can hold a room* — and slips back to *I* the moment he forgets he is being billed.

**Mateo Brill** — 41, beast-tamer, eleven years contracted, three renewals deep. **Want:** to be a man on the far side of the tent again, just once, for one act. **Flaw:** too proud to ask anyone for the one thing that would help him. **Physical detail:** a grey lion's mane he keeps shaved short at the sides, and a tail he tucks through the back of his belt so it will not catch. **Speech tic:** a low thoughtful *Mmm* before every answer, as if the answer were worth weighing twice.

**Junie Sparrow** — 10, quick-change acrobat, the youngest signature in the company and the most enthusiastic about it. **Want:** to be the best act on the road, and to be told so. **Flaw:** she agrees to things she has not understood, at speed, with her hand already up. **Physical detail:** freckles across the bridge of her nose and a single soft feather, black-tipped, tucked behind her left ear, which she has started to preen without noticing. **Speech tic:** a rapid under-breath of *is it fine? is it fine? is it fine?* before she commits to anything at all.

**Halloran Quill** — 58, Bookkeeper of the Ledger, thirty years in the Paper Caravan and the only man in the company who can read the whole of any contract in the company. **Want:** to be forgiven by somebody specific, and he has not decided whether that person is a client or a relative. **Flaw:** he has never once refused a superior; he only ever wrote down what he was told, carefully, in the correct column. **Physical detail:** a green eyeshade, cuffs inked black to the elbow, and one spectacle lens replaced with a disc of shell that gives everything a faint amber blur. **Speech tic:** begins corrections with *strictly speaking* and *I would argue*, and cannot hear a lie without a hedge in it.

**Odette Vrain** — 22, bareback rider, the only performer in nine seasons who signed in pencil. **Want:** to prove that a contract can be beaten, photographed, copied, and read aloud in front of a paying audience. **Flaw:** she signed once, in a bad year, and has been the guilt of it ever since, and knows that running is why she is safe. **Physical detail:** a scarlet-and-gold riding coat with cuffs gone threadbare at the seams, and a riding crop she has never once used on anything. **Speech tic:** speaks in the imperative under stress — *Do this. Now. Again* — and lapses into a low sweet coaxing voice the rest of the time.

## Places

**The Ivory Tent (the Big Top, billed as the Ivory Tent of the Gilded Menagerie).** Sixty feet of unbleached canvas on ring-pole iron, lit by forty-one gas jets in a brass corona, with a sawdust floor raked into a spiral rosette twice a night by two men with wooden rakes. Under the canvas it is hot, white, and roaring; from the road it is a single lit hill moving under the stars, and the whole Salt Line sees it pass.

**The Scented Row (Cage Row).** The wagon-park behind the Big Top where the contracted sleep: six long barred wagons in a crooked line, each with a nameplate, a straw bed, a tin cup, and a small personal shelf. It smells of straw, oiled iron, and every animal at once, and it is the only place in the circus where nobody performs.

**The Paper Caravan (the Ledger wagon).** A black varnished wagon with a single window, curtained green, containing eleven hundred bound ledgers in cedar cases and one long desk. Quill's domain. Visitors are made to leave matches at the door, and the door is unlocked with a brass key that is the largest object any of them own.

**The Lantern Works (Boiler and Gas Plant).** A low brick shed behind the far side of the tent, all pipe and pressure-valve and hot brass, where two lanternists keep the corona lit through wind and vegetable accidents. It smells of gas and hot iron, it is strictly no-entry to performers, and it is louder than the band.

**The Widow's Pew (the Salt Line road, anywhere, on the move).** A long green-painted bench wagon, walled and curtained, that the company uses as a dressing room and that the company pretends is a bench. It is where the quick-change artists change in forty seconds, where Mateo sits, and where, three times a season, someone sits alone in the dark and will not say why.

**The Tenting Grove, Harrow's Landing (winter quarters).** Nine elms on the churned ground of the old ropeworks, guy-ropes, mud, tin sheds, the smell of cold iron and soup. A wire on the packing shed reads MENAGERIE — HEATED. It is where the show is repaired, where the argument that will end in the ninth story has been building for three winters, and where the company spends winter Sundays in the open air, and cold, and not talking about the summer's contracts.

## Visual Style

Warm gaudy Victorian showmen's brass: gaslit amber and nicotine cream, oxblood red, tarnished gold leaf, and the specific bruised teal of a tent seen from the inside. Materials are canvas, painted hoop-pole, sequined wool, oiled black leather, brass gone green at the screws, and cheap red sealing wax. Lighting is always one warm source — a gas corona, a carriage lantern, a single window — throwing long spidery shadows up the tent canvas, with the outside world a cold blue-green night. The recurring motif is a **paper contract with a red wax seal**: torn, folded, nailed to a wagon, held up to the gaslight, dropped in sawdust, or floating in a punch bowl.

## Motifs

1. A bound ledger, ruled in three columns, with one crossed-out name in fresh red ink.
2. Red sealing wax and a brass key on a string.
3. A small hand mirror, cracked across one corner, showing a face mid-morph.
4. The brass gas corona: forty-one jets, one sooted dark, a permanent guttering shadow that walks around the ring as the pressure drops.
5. Sawdust: raked into a rosette twice a night, trampled flat by the third act, and swept into the fire at dawn.

## Thematic Spine

Every story in this world is about the difference between agreeing to something and understanding it, and about how a kindness you never actually speak is still a debt. The recurring question is what people sign for when they are young, frightened, dazzled, or in need — and whether a contract can be talked back out of the room it was read in.

The **accessible** stories take it at the scale of a single small honesty: being found out, and not being ruined. The **intermediate** stories take it at the scale of a company — a ledger, an audience, a rehearsed show that finally tells the truth. The **advanced** stories take it at the scale of a signature that predates the circus itself: the legal architecture of a trap, and whether a person can be released from a bargain that has been kept faithfully, exactly as written, for thirty years.
