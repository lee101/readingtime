# World: The Reference Pea

Featherstone is a town of mattresses, and its Bureau of Standards keeps in a locked room a single dried pea, and that pea is the reason the royal family exists. Not a crown, not a palace: a pea. In Featherstone a standard is a thing agreed to be itself, kept in the Bureau, and compared against, never used, never touched, never once in two hundred years. A pea under ninety mattresses is therefore not a comfort; it is a test, and the person who feels it is an instrument, and an instrument is used up. The story of the princess and the pea in Featherstone is remembered as the founding joke of the Bureau, and it is funny, and nobody in the Bureau will tell it at work.

## Setting

Featherstone lies on a flat chalk shelf over a river valley of soft land, with the Bureau at the top of the town in a plain stone building and the stuffing works along the towpath below, where the flax is retted and the hair is curled in a shed that smells like a hundred thousand small animals. The trade is beds. Featherstone supplies four counties, and it does so on a system that nobody outside the trade can explain and everybody inside it cannot live without: a house does not buy a bed, it buys a tolerance, and a tolerance is a figure, and the figure is the promise.

The Bureau is a public body with a charter, nine examiners, a long room of numbered drawers, a test floor with ninety-one beds laid out in rows, and a strongroom for the standards. The examiners are called testers. They are trained for four years, they are the most respected people in the town, and there is exactly one thing they are allowed to say about a bed, which is whether it is in tolerance or out, and one thing they are not allowed to say, which is why it is a hard job.

## The Magic

1. **A standard is a thing agreed to be itself, kept in the Bureau, and compared against.** A standard is never used. It lies in a drawer, in a box, in a case, and other things are brought to it.
2. **A standard that is used is a spoiled standard.** A pea that has been under a mattress is a proof, not a standard, and the whole of the Bureau's authority rests on that distinction and on the fact that nobody has ever been careless enough to break it.
3. **A tolerance is a promise about how wrong a thing may be.** Every bed sold in Featherstone carries a figure, and a bed that exceeds its figure must be returned, opened, emptied, burnt, and re-stuffed, and the cost falls on the shop that sold it.
4. **Sensitivity is a trade, and it is learned.** There are testers. A tester who cannot feel the reference pea is finished, pensioned, and quietly never spoken of again, and there are four such names in the Bureau's history and no photographs.
5. **A great house is a set of measurements.** When a child is born into one, the household is re-certified: every bed, every door, every stair, every floor is re-tested against the family's declared sensitivity, and one child is designated the Sharp One, who is the house's instrument.
6. **The Sharp One cannot be retested.** The instrument is used up by the certification, and a household that loses its Sharp One is a household that has gone blunt, which is a real and permanent loss and the reason nobody in Featherstone will say the word *tender* in a shop.
7. **A thing found under a mattress never goes back under a mattress.** It goes to the Bureau, and it is a finding, and a finding is permanent.
8. **A finding is public and permanent in the language.** Once a household is found sharp, its name becomes a word meaning sharp, forever, in every town that has ever bought a Featherstone bed.
9. **Tolerance binds at the declared figure, not the true one.** A bed that is within its real tolerance and over its declared one is out, and no amount of arguing about the truth will move it, because the promise is the thing that was sold.
10. **A tester who declares a bed out when it is in is a liar. A tester who declares a bed in when it is out is a criminal. There is no neutral act,** and there has never been a way to say nothing, and the Bureau's hardest discipline is not the pea but the requirement to finish.

## Cast

**Marguerite Fenn**, 15, a tester's daughter and the only person in Featherstone who has felt the reference pea, once, on her thirteenth birthday, under supervision, in the strongroom, as a calibration. *Want:* to be tested herself, formally, and to fail. *Flaw:* she can feel the pea and cannot stop feeling it, and can no longer sleep in any bed the Bureau has ever certified, and has not told her mother. *Detail:* the two small red marks on either side of her fourth finger from holding the calibration case, and a habit of lying on the floor to read. *Speech habit:* gives tolerances to strangers, unprompted, in the street.

**Aldis Prome**, 47, the Bureau's chief tester, twenty-two years on the test floor and the only man alive who can feel the pea on the first pass. *Want:* to be replaced while he is still accurate. *Flaw:* he has trained eleven testers to be exactly like him and has therefore made himself the Bureau's single point of failure, and knows it, and has been saying nothing for six years. *Detail:* an enormous careful hand, and a habit of pressing the heel of that hand flat on a mattress before declaring anything. *Speech habit:* says the figure, then the verdict, then the figure again, and will not discuss a bed he has not personally slept in.

**Nell Crumm**, 62, a stuffing-shop owner, the only person in Featherstone who can say what a house is actually like to sleep in, and who has said so, repeatedly, to nobody in authority. *Want:* to be asked to build one bed that is deliberately wrong. *Flaw:* she has spent forty years stuffing to a figure and can no longer stuff to a feeling, and when she tries she over-stuffs, and has ruined eleven beds and paid for all of them. *Detail:* a hip that has been out of alignment since 1979 and a way of sitting on one buttock on a bench. *Speech habit:* narrates the stuffing in the third person, as if it were somebody else being stuffed.

**Jonah Prine**, 9, a boy from the terrace, on whom the Bureau discovered, at a school inspection, a sensitivity nobody had looked for. *Want:* to go home without anybody measuring him. *Flaw:* he is now, formally, an instrument, and has worked out that this makes him important, and has started being difficult on purpose. *Detail:* a caliper-shaped bruise on his own forearm from practicing on himself, and a habit of standing with his back to a door. *Speech habit:* asks what a thing is for before he lets anybody do it, in a small flat voice, and it is unnervingly effective.

**Lady Hesp Vantry**, 41, of the great house, certified in 1974 and blunt since 1989, and therefore the reason her household is in the state it is in. *Want:* for her family to be ordinary. *Flaw:* she has certified two of her own daughters and cannot now speak to either of them, and has not explained this to her husband, who believes she is unwell. *Detail:* a cold face and a very warm hand, and a habit of turning her palm down when she introduces people. *Speech habit:* says "we are not a delicate house" in the past tense.

**Cobb Tarn**, 33, the Bureau's registrar, keeper of the findings, who has written nine hundred findings in eleven years and has read every one of them twice, and who has begun to notice a pattern nobody was supposed to notice. *Want:* to be told he is wrong. *Flaw:* he is not wrong, and he has discovered it in a way that makes him complicit, and has said nothing to anybody because the alternative is a public report he cannot write. *Detail:* a very small hand in a very large book, and a habit of counting entries rather than reading them. *Speech habit:* recites findings from memory, exactly, and stops in the middle when he reaches one he does not know.

## Places

**The Strongroom.** A low vaulted room in the Bureau's cellar, one north window, a wall of numbered drawers, and in drawer one the pea: dried, in a glass case, on a card with a date and a signature. It is nine feet of air around the case and two people in the room at a time and it is the reason the Bureau is what it is.

**The Test Floor.** Ninety-one beds in rows, a wooden floor worn in tracks, no rugs, no light except high north windows, a hundred yards of walking between bed one and bed ninety-one. Nobody hurries on the test floor and nobody talks above a murmur.

**The Stuffing Works.** Long sheds along the towpath, hot and loud, full of retted flax, curled horsehair, and girls of fifteen working a tearing machine. The smell of the works is the smell of the whole town and the town is proud of it in a low way.

**The Terrace.** A row of forty houses below the works where the people who cannot be sensitive live because they are too dull to qualify, which is stated as a compliment in Featherstone and is not one.

**The Great House Kitchen.** The one place in the great house where nobody lies down. Copper, steam, a long table, and the only room in which the Sharp One is a child rather than an instrument.

**The Burn Yard.** Behind the works: a brick kiln, an iron cage, and the straw and flock from every returned bed in the county. A tester can stand at the fence and watch four hundred mattresses burn, and the examiners are taken there in their first year, and there is no examination of their faces.

## Visual Style

A palette of chalk white, ticking cream, unbleached flax, iron grey, and one deep madder red in the strongroom, lit almost entirely by high cold north windows, so that the light is flat, shadowless, and entirely unsentimental. The only warm light in Featherstone is at the kiln. Materials: old ticking, brass and iron bed frames, horsehair, coir, a glass case on a card, lead pencil on a findings card, chalk dust, and the black smoke of the burn yard. The era reads 1870s-1900s, pre-electric: gas in the kitchens, a hand bell in the test hall, no motor, a handcart. The recurring motif is the figure — a number written in pencil on the foot of a bed, in a hand, the way a promise is written down.

## Motifs

1. **The figure on the bed foot** — a tolerance written in pencil in a testing hand, never crossed out, and legible to whoever sleeps there next.
2. **The drawers** — nine hundred numbered drawers of findings, each with a card, each with a name, and the small number of cards in drawer one.
3. **The test floor** — ninety-one beds in rows, high windows, no rugs, and a hundred yards of walking between the first and the last.
4. **A mattress opened at the seam** — ticking cut along one edge, stuffing out in a grey heap, four hundred of them in a burn yard.
5. **The two red marks on a finger** — held too hard, too often, by a child who was being calibrated.

## Thematic Spine

All nine stories are about a measurement that is only ever as fair as the use made of it, and about what a person becomes once they have been declared an instrument. The accessible band treats it as a purchase: a figure, a returned bed, a test floor. The intermediate band treats it as a rule: a drawer, a tolerance, a lapse. The advanced band treats it as an institution: a pattern, a name, a reform.
