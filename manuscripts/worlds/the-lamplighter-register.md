# World: The Lamplighter's Register

Ninehook is a harbour city of four hundred and eleven lamps, and the lamp is not owned by anybody. It is leased. A lamp in Ninehook is a document with a flame in it, and the flame obeys the lease and not the holder, and the register in the lamp office keeps every lease ever made on every lamp since 1841, including the ones for people who are dead, forty-one of them on Lamp Nine alone, which is the lamp at the bottom of Wharf Street and the one everybody tells the Aladdin story about. The story everybody tells has a boy with a magic lamp and three wishes and a bad old man who is thrown into the sea. What Ninehook says is that there was no bad old man. There was a lease, and a register, and a man who had read the register and knew exactly what the lease would not permit, and forty-one people before him who had held the ring and been responsible for it.

## Setting

Ninehook is built on a slip of hard ground between a river and a sea-cliff, with the water on three sides and the lamps set on iron standards along the quays, up the four streets, along the cliff path, and along the harbour wall where the wind comes off the water and takes the glass out. The trade of the town is the lamp: nine lamp-wards, a lamp office on the quay, a store of burners and mantles and wicks, a glass yard where the chimneys are blown, and a small, quiet, extremely literate body of clerks who maintain the register, which runs to four hundred and eleven lamps and about nine thousand entries.

The town's life is arranged around dusk and nothing else. The lamps are lit at four bells in the winter and six in the summer, and the lamplighters walk fixed rounds, and the round is a piece of knowledge that is passed on and never written down except in one ward, where it is written down, and that ward is the one people make jokes about. Salt, tar, coal gas off the point, fish, rope, and a very large quantity of wind. The one warm public room is the Lamp Office, which is warm because it has a hundred and forty years of fires stacked in it, and it is the only room in Ninehook where you may not smoke.

## The Magic

1. **A lamp is leased, not owned.** The lease is a document and the flame obeys the lease, not the holder. A man holding a ring in a lamp he does not hold will get light, and the light will be the light the lease allows, which may be very little.
2. **A genie is bound by every lease ever made on the lamp.** It cannot be freed by breaking the lamp and it cannot be freed by drowning it. It is freed only by extinguishing the flame with the holder's consent, and a holder under a life interest cannot give that consent, so a lamp under a life interest cannot be ended by anybody.
3. **A wish is an entry.** It is performed and it is written, and the writing is the true version. A wish as remembered is not the wish, and a wish as reported by the holder is not the wish, and a wish whose written term is worse than the reported one has been performed as written.
4. **A wish may be refused where the lease forbids it,** and refusal costs the holder the whole lease, and the lease does not come back, and refusal is not a negotiation.
5. **A lamp with no lease has no flame, and a flame with no lease is an accident,** and accidents in Ninehook are put out within the quarter hour by whoever gets there, and the register is left with a line that says *unlet* and the date.
6. **The dead hold leases.** A lamp leased to a man for his lifetime is still his, and the register keeps the entry, and his wishes still take effect, forty years on, on behalf of whoever is holding the ring that evening.
7. **The genie may be asked what a lease says. It may not be asked what it wants,** and it will answer the question nobody asked, at length, and nobody in Ninehook has ever been ready for it.
8. **A holder who does not know what the lamp is cannot hold it,** and the ring does nothing in the hand of a person who has not read the entry, which is why a lease is signed in the clerk's presence and the clerk watches the signer's face.
9. **Two holders at once is possible and is called double occupancy,** and the register does not prevent it, because the register records and does not adjudicate, and a double occupancy resolves itself in about eleven minutes and the office is then closed for a fortnight.
10. **The last entry in every register is the same word.** It is not a name. It is the word the city uses for the next person, and the office has never written it out in full on a single page, and there are four pages in the register where it has been written out in full, and every one of them is in the press.

## Cast

**Wren Alsop**, 16, a lamplighter's boy on the cliff-path round, one of nine, carrying a taper-pole and a box of matches and a memorised round. *Want:* to be given the ring ward, which is the harbour wall, which is the worst round in the town. *Flaw:* he has read forty-one entries in the register without having read the lease, and has begun to recognise things, and cannot stop looking. *Detail:* a scorched left thumb from a taper that blew back off the sea, and a habit of counting lamps out loud under his breath. *Speech habit:* gives the time in bells and the ward number before anything else, and cannot be stopped.

**Hettie Rowntree**, 41, a register clerk, nineteen years copying leases into the big book and the only person in the office who can read the 1841 hand. *Want:* to be asked to close Lamp Nine. *Flaw:* she has been quietly completing the four pages that are missing, out of her own head, at night, for six years, and has not entered them. *Detail:* an ink-blue right hand, and a habit of holding the pen above the line and not writing for a beat too long. *Speech habit:* reads entries aloud exactly as they stand, including the crossed-out parts, and will not paraphrase.

**Sil Marrow**, 52, harbourmaster of the third quay, keeper of the point, and the man who has been letting the same unlet lamp to the same fisherman for six years without a lease, because the man is old and poor and it keeps the corner lit. *Want:* to be told to stop. *Flaw:* he cannot stop, because the register shows the corner unlet and the corner is not, and he has preferred the corner. *Detail:* a bad left shoulder from a hawser, and a habit of checking the tide on any question at all. *Speech habit:* answers with a time and a state of sea, whatever is asked.

**Aubry Vell**, 13, a chimney-glazier's girl in the glass yard on the point, who blew a chimney for a lamp in 1898 and got a man to sign for it who did not read it. *Want:* her father to be released from a lease he does not know he is under. *Flaw:* she has been trying for three years and has not told him what she is doing, and the man is perfectly happy, and that is the difficulty. *Detail:* a blowpipe in her hand from the moment she gets up, and a habit of turning glass constantly. *Speech habit:* states the glass before the request — "that was a hard one" — and then makes the request.

**Constable Iven Pyle**, 34, night patrol, the man who arrives at double occupancies, and who has been to Lamp Nine eleven times and has written the same eleven reports. *Want:* to write one report that is different. *Flaw:* he has decided not to write it, and a report not written is a night in which nothing happened, and eleven such reports are a pattern a good lawyer would build a house on. *Detail:* a lamp that is not police issue and that he carries anyway, and a habit of standing under the lamp he is writing about. *Speech habit:* times and names, exact, and a total absence of adjectives.

**Old Perrin Oll**, 78, retired lamplighter of the harbour wall, forty-one years on the worst round, and the man who was on Lamp Nine in 1889 and handed the ring on and did not read the lease. *Want:* to say one thing out loud before he dies. *Flaw:* he does not know what it is, and has been assembling it for forty-one years, and every version he has tried is one clause short. *Detail:* a hand permanently curled around a taper-pole grip, and a habit of lighting a taper indoors and letting it burn down. *Speech habit:* starts every sentence with the ward number and cannot remember ward numbers.

## Places

**The Lamp Office.** On the quay: a long whitewashed room, a hundred and forty years of fireplace, four desks, twelve presses, and the register — four hundred and eleven lamps, nine thousand entries, in eleven bound volumes and a day book. Clerks work in coats indoors, because the room is cold at the top and hot at the bottom, which is a hundred and forty years of building doing what it does.

**Lamp Nine.** At the bottom of Wharf Street, on the corner, the busiest lamp in Ninehook, with forty-one living and dead entries and an iron standard with a ring on it that has been polished so many thousand times it has a hollow in it. The glass yard is forty yards away and the chimney there is the one in the 1898 lease.

**The Cliff Path.** Four miles of iron standards along a sea-cliff, unlit in a westerly, walked in a gale with a taper-pole and a box of matches and a memorised round of two hundred and eleven lamps. The hardest round in the town. It is also the round nobody can be tested on, because the lamps are lit by hand, not by lease.

**The Glass Yard.** On the point: three blowpipes, a glory-hole, an annealing oven, and a yard of finished chimneys in straw. Wet, filthy, hot, and the only place in Ninehook where you can see a flame in daylight without a lamp.

**The Unlet Corner.** Third quay, where a fisherman's lamp has been burning for six years with no lease, three streets from the Lamp Office, and the register shows the corner dark.

**The Double Door.** The Lamp Office's back door, off the quay, where a person carrying a ring comes in. There is a slate, and a chalk, and a sentence cut into the lintel: *the holder is named and the lamp is read*. Under the sentence, forty years of chalk marks, one per attendance, none of them explained.

## Visual Style

A palette of wet stone grey, tar black, brass and sodium orange, and one very cold sea-blue, lit almost entirely by the lamps themselves: a small hard orange point in a large dark frame, with the flame's halo caught in wet air and the rest of the picture nearly gone. Sources are always low, and always at the edge of the frame. Materials: iron standards, glass chimneys, brass rings worn hollow, tarred rope, wet cobble, oil, salt, a taper-pole, an ink-blue hand, and nine books four hundred years deep. The era reads 1870s-1900s, pre-electric: gas off the point, a handcart, oil lamps, boots. The recurring motif is the ring — a brass ring on an iron standard, worn into a hollow by hands, and the only object in Ninehook that a person can hold and cannot own.

## Motifs

1. **The ring worn hollow** — a brass ring on an iron standard, polished into a dish by a century of hands, exactly the shape of a grip and nothing else.
2. **The entry** — one line of ink, a date, a hand, a term, and the crossed-out parts left legible.
3. **The taper-pole** — a long pole with a taper clipped to the end, lit, carried flat, in any wind.
4. **The unlit corner** — three streets from the office, and a lamp burning, and the register showing dark.
5. **The chalk marks on the back-door lintel**, one per attendance, forty years of them, unexplained.

## Thematic Spine

All nine stories are about holding something you cannot own, and about the difference between what a person intended and what a document says they intended. The accessible band treats it as a light: a lamp, a ring, a wish. The intermediate band treats it as an entry: a dead man, a book, an unlet flame. The advanced band treats it as an institution: an audit, an answer, and eleven minutes that four people describe differently.
