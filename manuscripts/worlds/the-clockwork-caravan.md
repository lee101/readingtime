# World: The Clockwork Caravan

The Caravan is a single line of steam-and-balloon trains that crosses nine hundred miles of sun-baked desert, the Ochre Basin, hauling cargo nobody else will touch: bottled weather, spare hours, and memories sealed in glass and sold by weight. In the towns strung along the Line, a ticket is not bought with coin. It is bought with minutes of your own life, cut cleanly and burned in the engine, and the arithmetic of that bargain is written in a green-ink Ledger that no driver can erase. Life in the Basin is loud, dusty, brass-and-canvas busy — market days, bottled thunder, brass bells, children racing hand-carts under a balloon mast. And underneath all of it runs one slow wrong thing: every mile the Line travels is a mile paid for by somebody who will never get those minutes back, and cannot remember, to this day, where they went.

## Setting

The Ochre Basin is a bowl of rust-coloured sand two thousand miles wide, ringed by salt flats and crossed by one railway. Days are hard white light from above and long orange smears from the side; the sun does not so much rise as switch on. Nights are cold, and caravanners sleep under waxed canvas that crackles like pastry. Settlements are low, wide, shade-heavy buildings of sun-bleached brick and riveted brass, built around water towers and bell posts. Emberwhistle Halt, a single platform and a copper bell, sits at the western end; Sirocco, a market town grown enormous under a dune's lee, sits in the middle; Kellvetch, the Hour Yards, sprawls at the eastern end in clay pits where the jars are stored.

Food is long-grain bread, salt mutton, dates pressed into honey-slabs, and thin bitter tea brewed from desert sage. Milk comes powdered and reconstituted; eggs, when they appear, are a market-day event. Transport on the Line is by train only, or by the two-wheeled hand-cart crews who meet it at every stop. A market day in Sirocco begins before dawn: the Underdune Bazaar opens when the first train releases a jar of stored morning light, and stalls light their own lamps with bottled sunrise, so that the whole market runs in low amber light until noon. Buyers haggle over thunder flasks, over jars of spare afternoon, and — quietly, in curtained booths at the back — over sealed memories.

## The Magic

1. **Time is cargo, not a resource.** In the Basin, an hour is a substance. It can be bottled into a brass hour-jar, stacked, sold, spent, and burned. The Line runs on stored hours; a train with empty jars is a train that cannot move.
2. **A ticket is paid in the payer's own life.** Passage costs minutes cut from the traveler's own body-clock. The cut is permanent and exact. Nobody ages visibly, but everyone has a number, and everyone who is honest knows theirs is smaller than it was.
3. **A minute taken is never explained to the person it was taken from.** The traveler cannot account for the gap — not as amnesia, but as a smooth patch: no memory, no ache, no scar, nothing. The mind routes around the hole. This is the Line's central cruelty, and the reason nobody in the Basin can prove what happened to them.
4. **Every taken minute fills with a blank hour.** A blank hour is the time that no longer belongs to anybody. It drifts, and it is drawn to warm, living people, and if it settles in someone unaccounted for, they will not notice the change. Blank hours are the reason the Line cannot be run honestly and the reason drivers are frightened of their own Ledgers.
5. **Weather is bred, not summoned.** No one can call rain out of nothing. Weather can only be bottled, bred in the deep-mirror vats, carried, traded, and released. A jar opened in the wrong season sterilises the ground it falls on for a year, and the fines for that are ruinous.
6. **Machines cannot lie; ledgers cannot either.** Every engine keeps a Ledger, and the Ledger is written in the driver's own hand at every stop. It cannot be erased, only carried forward. A driver may miscount. A driver may not un-write.
7. **Balloons lift by time, not air.** The great gasbags are ballast-lighted: a train rises as it spends stored hours, and the more ballast it carries the slower and more honestly it runs. Over-ballasted trains are slow, stubborn, and truthful.
8. **The Line's length is its debt.** The whole railway exists only because the total hours taken exceeds the total hours spent. If the debt were ever repaid in full, the Line would lose coherence and its carriages would come apart. The crew therefore cannot simply stop. This is the trap at the centre of everything.
9. **Spending an hour you do not own sends the blank hour to you.** If a person rides on time that was not cut from them — stolen, borrowed, or resold — the Line takes the payment out of their body instead, and their face loses a day they will never see. The penalty is never blood or violence; it is a person quietly arriving at a station ten years older than they left.
10. **A true name, spoken aloud into a Ledger, cancels one hour of debt, once, and never again** — and the speaker is then barred from the Line for a year. It is the only legal discharge the Basin knows, and it is nearly unusable, because everyone suspects everyone.

## Cast

All six recur across the world's nine stories. Individual stories may add one or two local figures — a stationmaster, a jar-packer, a passenger — but the six below carry the voice of the world and must feel present or referenced in every tale.

1. **Ivo Tarrow**, 62, retired driver and keeper of the ticket booth at Emberwhistle Halt. *Want:* to finish the one run he has owed his dead brother Emrys for forty years. *Flaw:* he believes every debt is dischargeable if you simply count carefully enough. *Physical detail:* a white left eyebrow burned to a stub at the tip, permanent and impossible to explain. *Verbal habit:* ends every judgement with "that is the arithmetic of it."
2. **Nim Ferrous**, 13, junior courier riding the brake van, still not yet on the registry. *Want:* her name written into the Caravan's registry in her own hand, with a date. *Flaw:* afraid of being told she is not ready, so she agrees to things before the question is finished. *Physical detail:* a single copper braid down one side of her head, tied off with blue twine. *Verbal habit:* repeats the last two words she has heard before answering anything.
3. **Sol Brannen**, 40, Ledger-keeper of the leading engine, the Long Noon. *Want:* for the Line to keep running, whatever it costs, and to never be the man who stopped it. *Flaw:* has never once refused to sell a ticket, and has begun to notice what that makes him. *Physical detail:* white gloves, with a permanent green ink stain ground into the right thumb. *Verbal habit:* reads a figure aloud, then asks you to confirm you heard it correctly.
4. **Marmalade**, 9, a foundling who lives under the brake van and is fed by an unknown arrangement with the cooks. *Want:* to see the far end of the Line, and to find out who is supposed to be looking after her. *Flaw:* has decided, on excellent evidence, that every adult is lying. *Physical detail:* one boot a full size too small, its cuff patched with cut tin. *Verbal habit:* offers a fact she has only half heard, immediately, in a confidential whisper.
5. **Captain Rell Ferrite**, 55, chief engineer, responsible for the engines and the gasbags. *Want:* the Line repaired and watered before the dry season turns the clay pits to brick. *Flaw:* thinks of people as parts, and parts as tradeable. *Physical detail:* a braided beard with three fine copper wires run through it, singed at the ends. *Verbal habit:* begins every instruction with "Right. Listen."
6. **Septimus Vail**, 50, travelling broker in bottled weather, reselling surplus hours cheaply to people who would rather not age. *Want:* to believe he is running a comfort business and not a famine. *Flaw:* he does believe it, sincerely, and that is worse. *Physical detail:* a white panama hat and sunglasses cut from smoked window glass. *Verbal habit:* states his price out loud before agreeing to anything, and says "same again?" at the end of every deal.

## Places

1. **Emberwhistle Halt.** A single platform under a copper roof gone green at the edges, with a bell the size of a barrel hung on a timber frame and rung by hand at every arrival. Ivo Tarrow's booth is a plywood cabin with a brass ticket window, a spike for hanging spare coats, and a chalked board of fares in minutes. The platform stones are warm even at night; beyond it there is nothing at all in any direction but sand.
2. **The Hour Yards of Kellvetch.** Vast dry clay pits in the eastern desert, stacked four hundred jars deep in rusted iron racks that go down into the ground like corkscrews. The air hums faintly and smells faintly of hot iron and cut grass. Packers move through the aisles in shirtsleeves, and every jar handle is tied with a loop of blue twine so that a bad one can be spotted from the ridge.
3. **Balloonmast Ridge.** A bare spine of red rock where six mooring masts stand eighty feet tall, each guyed with a hundred and forty yards of cable. At dawn the gasbags come over the ridge in slow descending columns, backlit and enormous, and the ridge roars with the hiss of valves. Children climb the lower mast for free, which is why the rock is polished smooth.
4. **The Underdune Bazaar at Sirocco.** A market sheltered in the lee of a three-hundred-foot dune, roofed with stretched canvas and lit entirely by bottled sunrise, so that the whole bazaar runs in low amber light no matter the hour. Stalls sell thunder flasks, salt, cloth, and — in curtained booths at the back — sealed memories, unwrapped only for the buyer.
5. **The Ledger Room of the Long Noon.** A narrow lined-steel cabin behind the coal door, one desk, one lamp with a green glass shade, and forty years of green-ink registers in oak shelves. The engine's heartbeat comes up through the floor as a slow tick, half a beat behind the writing. The room is always warmer than outside and always smells of ink and hot brass.
6. **Coach Nine, The Empty Carriage.** A coach parked on a siding at Sirocco, sealed with a painted band, its windows filmed over from the inside. It is not haunted and it is not frightening: it is simply a carriage whose hours were spent without being counted, so it has come apart into a corridor of slow warm sand, and the sand goes up to your knees and settles warm and weightless. Nobody keeps it. Everybody checks it.

## Visual Style

The visual identity of the Clockwork Caravan is **sun-bleached brass and canvas against an ochre sky**: rust, dust-orange, sun-bleached teal, cream canvas and hot copper, lit by a hard low sun that throws long hard shadows and blows out the highlights to white. The materials are always riveted brass, waxed cream canvas, scorched red leather, bone-dry pale timber, and glass that holds light, not water. The era feel is late steam and early flight — padded coats and goggles in the desert, telegraph wire and canvas awnings, no motors, no plastic, no glass towers. The recurring motif is **a single blue loop of twine**: it is tied on every hour-jar handle, on Nim's braid, on the rigging of the gasbags, and on a thousand crate lashings along the whole Line, so that wherever you look there is one small clean blue mark in all that orange dust.

## Motifs

1. **A brass hour-jar with blue twine on its handle**, glass humming faintly, sand crusted to the shoulder.
2. **A pocket-watch face with no hands** — a smooth dial, a working click, and a number scratched on the back.
3. **The copper bell at Emberwhistle**, green-edged, rung by hand, hanging still in most covers and swinging in a few.
4. **Thunder bottled in a green glass flask**, seen as a crackle of white light frozen inside the glass, the only green in the picture.
5. **A single too-small boot with a cut-tin cuff**, planted in ochre sand, usually at the edge of the frame.

## Thematic Spine

All nine stories are about the difference between what you are owed and what you have agreed to, and about the fact that a debt you cannot remember incurring is still a debt. The accessible band treats it as honesty at the scale of a single afternoon — a jar that must go home, a morning that must be put back, a first journey where the price is small enough to count. The intermediate band treats it as a family problem, where the arithmetic has already been done to people who never agreed to it and the question is what you owe them. The advanced band treats it as a structural question, where the whole enterprise is a beautiful machine running on a lie everyone is complicit in, and the only available answers are confession, discharge, or slow self-annihilation.
