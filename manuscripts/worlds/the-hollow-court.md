# World: The Hollow Court

A fairy court keeps no gold, no land, and no army — it keeps stories, and spends them at a table. The Hollow Court is a winter palace of silvered wood built around a single vast chamber where a teller stands in a pool of lamplight and says a true thing out loud, and a coin of spun silver drops into a tray, warm, shaped on its face like the story it holds. Then everybody eats. That is the whole economy: tell, mint, spend, eat. The wonderful thing about the Hollow Court is that lying is impossible and truth is delicious. The wrong thing is that the Court has been telling the same story about itself for centuries, so thoroughly and so often that every coin in the vault has worn down to a husk worth nothing but the shape of an old memory. The Queen is fading — not dying, fading, going pale and weightless at the edges — because she has been eating chalk for ninety years, and no one in the Hall can think of a single true thing that is worth the coin.

## Setting

The Court stands on a chalk shelf above a cold inland sea, in a building of silvered pine with no corners and no nails: a long oval Hall around one hanging brass lamp the size of a wagon wheel, a domed Treasury lined floor to ceiling with pigeonholes, a dining hall built around one forty-foot table of dark oak, and a low stone mill underneath it all. The climate is late-autumn-forever, wind off the water, frost on the inside of the glass. Inhabitants burn lamp oil, wear fur indoors, and speak in a hush because the Hall is stone. The food is the Court's standing joke: chaff bread, grey stew, flour ground from old tales, salt, and water. Cheerfulness is rationed. Outside there is a market: mud, apples, honest rope, cheap real things, and the Upper Gate, where the guards check every traveller's palm for coin because nothing leaves without paying. Market day at the gate is the only loud day in the world — boots, haggling, a dog — and it is the plot of at least three of these stories, because the Hall is only ever interesting when the weather gets in.

## The Magic

1. **Value follows truth, exactly.** A coin is worth precisely the portion of the story that was true. Nothing else mints, however beautiful, and a partly-true tale mints a coin that is light in the hand and will not go across the Table's rim.
2. **A story must be told aloud, in the Hall, to at least one listener who could contradict it.** Told to nobody, it mints nothing, however true. The audience is not decoration; the audience is the instrument.
3. **A story may be coined only once.** Once it is minted and spent, it is closed forever. This is why the Court owns almost none of its own history and a great deal of strangers' gossip.
4. **Repetition thins truth.** Told the same way, the same tale, again and again, it becomes a *husk*: it still mints, but pale and weightless, and it spends as water and tastes of chalk. The vault is full of husks, sorted into trays by size, like bad eggs.
5. **The Table is the only place coin changes hands.** Nothing outside the Hall is bought with story. The Upper Gate market accepts coin and only coin, and every coin that leaves is gone from the world for good — the treasury, from the inside, is a wound that does not close.
6. **No teller may spend a coin from their own story.** It mints warm in the hand and will not cross the Table's rim. Every teller is therefore their own worst debtor, permanently.
7. **The cost:** when a true story is coined, the true part happens again once in the teller's body. A blistered palm. Nine minutes of not being able to breathe. Small, always real, and paid at the moment of telling. A story already paid for is cheap to tell again — which is exactly why husks are also painless, and why the old ones are everybody's favourites.
8. **Nobody's story may be coined while they stand in the Hall, awake and unconsenting.** It does not merely fail; it *burns*, and the mint rings like a struck bell in every room of the palace until the teller goes back and pays it. Consent is not etiquette here, it is metallurgy.
9. **The limit, and the only mercy:** the Hall has one flame, in the Hall lamp. If a day and a night pass with no true story told, the flame shortens by a hand's span and the Court begins to lose a name from its rolls. **And when any rule is broken outright** — a lie told as a joke, a coin bought from a liar, a story taken from someone asleep — it leaves a *silent*: a ring of dead air about the width of a coin, which hangs where it happened and swells unless it is later paid for in a true story. The Court fills with silents. It is running out of room.

## Cast

Six people recur. The **physical detail** line for each is to be used *verbatim* in every cover-art prompt in this world, so that the cast reads as one family of faces.

- **Queen Ysandre the Long-Waited** — age: nine hundred winters, give or take a bad decade. Role: sovereign, and the Court's oldest customer. *Want:* one true story before her candle gutters out. *Flaw:* she has told so many others' stories that she has lost the thread of her own. *Physical detail:* a crown of hollow gold ringlets like a circlet of split coins, and fingertips gone pale and translucent enough to read a page through. *Speech tic:* speaks in a formal, beautiful cadence and then loses the end of her own sentence — "…and then it was— well. It was something."
- **Tallow Merrick** — age 61. Role: Keeper of the Vault, who signs every mint and sorts the husks. *Want:* to prove the treasury is not worthless, to someone, before she is gone. *Flaw:* she cannot stop counting, and she would rather sort a good story into the wrong tray than leave the shelves untidy. *Physical detail:* a chain of a hundred small iron keys worn over her apron like a necklace, each cut from a different coin, all of them a size too small. *Speech tic:* counts and rates aloud as she works — "four hundred and six, dull. Very dull. Two hundred and eleven, dreadful."
- **Nim Ollowan** — age 13. Role: apprentice teller, the only person in the Hall still allowed to stand in the light. *Want:* to be given a coin of her own telling. *Flaw:* she cannot repeat a thing without adding one detail too many, and the one detail too many is always the true one. *Physical detail:* a strip of unspun silver thread tied round her left wrist, frayed at the ends, always being re-knotted. *Speech tic:* inserts "and then—" into every retelling, always right before the good part.
- **Grist Bellweather** — age: forty-something, dusty. Role: Miller of the Chaff, whose stone mill grinds worn-out tales into flour. *Want:* to keep the Court fed through the winter on nothing. *Flaw:* he would rather grind the good tales than admit the barn is empty. *Physical detail:* flour-white hair and brows, a scorched leather apron patched at the elbow, both thumbs hooked in his belt. *Speech tic:* offers food in the middle of an argument — "have you eaten? No. Sit. Sit, there is broth."
- **Marrow** — age: unknowable, and always has been twenty. Role: the Fool, licensed to say anything and to be laughed at for it. *Want:* to be a teller. To be paid. To count. *Flaw:* he can only tell lies, and lies mint nothing at all, so the one person in the Hall everybody wants to listen to is the one person who earns nothing. *Physical detail:* bare feet, a moth-eaten cloak with two sleeves missing, and a grin of far too many yellow teeth; his shadow on the wall always has one finger too many. *Speech tic:* ends every joke with "— and that is a coin in somebody else's pocket."
- **Cresset Ombre** — age 55. Role: Lampwright, who tends the Hall's one flame and tests every coin on the sill for truth before it is signed. *Want:* to keep the lamp lit through to spring. *Flaw:* she knows exactly what the flame is actually made of, and she has sworn never to say it aloud, and the swearing is eating her. *Physical detail:* a brass lamp-stand fused to her right forearm, so that her hand is permanently a wick-holder, and the skin inside the cuff is scorched brown. *Speech tic:* refers to herself in the third person — "Cresset does not sleep. Cresset cannot afford to."

## Places

- **The Hall of Telling** — A long oval chamber of pale silvered pine with no corners and one hanging brass lamp the size of a wagon wheel. A single pale disc of light lies on the floor in the middle, and the teller stands in it; everyone else stands in the dark, in a ring, at a respectful distance, and the coins land in a brass tray on a plain deal table at the disc's edge.
- **The Hollow Vault** — A cold domed room lined floor to ceiling with pigeonhole shelves, velvet trays sorted by size, hundreds of thin silver coins in neat rows. It smells of cold metal and old paper, the lamps are low, and the shelves give back a faint chalky breath when the door opens.
- **The Long Table** — Forty feet of dark oak, grey linen, laid for nine in a hall built around it, with a long sideboard of bread, salt, stew and vinegar. It is the only place in the world where a coin can be spent, and it is always laid for nine even when the Court is down to four.
- **The Chaff Mill** — Grist's low stone mill under the palace, where husks are milled to grey flour with a sound like distant surf. Millstones, sacks, a hopper tall of shredded tale, and a warm dusty floor lit by one oil lamp hung low over the miller's shoulder.
- **The Upper Gate** — The walled arch where the Court's road meets the outside mud: guards with inked thumbs, a queue of ordinary people, carts of turnips and rope, the smell of rain, and above the arch a lintel carved with the flat relief of every story the Court has ever been told, worn to smooth illegibility.
- **The Ash Garden** — A walled plot behind the Hall of pale ash-grey plants with papery leaves, tended by nobody, growing out of the silents: a fallen coin, a wasted day, a lie told for a laugh. Nothing in it is poisonous and nothing in it is beautiful; in a wind the leaves sound like a room where people are saying nothing at all.

## Visual Style

A cold, ornate, slightly starving fairy court rendered in pewter, bone-white, frost-blue, tarnished silver and very old gilt, lit by exactly one warm point — the amber flame of a huge brass hanging lamp — so that every image has a small gold heart inside a great deal of blue. Materials are silvered pine, glass, velvet worn shiny at the elbows, flour dust, ice on the inside of windows, and coin-metal that looks soft. The era feel is late-Baroque and stuck there: carved lintels, curling wax, fringed coats of fur indoors, no modern object anywhere, everything slightly too ornate and slightly too cold to sit down in. The recurring motif is a pale disc of lamplight lying flat on a wooden floor with one person standing in it and everyone else at the edge of it, watching.

## Motifs

1. A thin silver coin, warm, with a single story-shape raised on its face — a boat, a staircase, a hand, a fox — and the worn blank discs of its husks.
2. The Hall lamp: a huge brass ring of a lamp burning one clear steady flame, its light falling in a hard-edged disc on a plank floor.
3. Grey bread, grey stew, and water: a laid table for nine, most of it untouched.
4. Moth-eaten cloth — a cloak, a sleeve, a banner with the swallows eaten out of it.
5. A wrist or throat printed with a ring of cold air, slightly wider than a coin, hanging in the air like smoke that will not move.

## Thematic Spine

Every story here is about the moment a true thing is spent, and the discovery that the self is the only reliable mine. What the Court keeps mistaking for wealth — tradition, repetition, the story everyone knows, the flattering version of a person — is the one thing the magic cannot pay for, and the whole world is a slow argument about who gets to be remembered honestly. At the **accessible** level that is a matter of simple, private generosity: a good deed needs no audience, and being asked is a kindness. At the **intermediate** level it becomes a matter of consent and evidence, of what you owe the person whose life you told. At the **advanced** level it becomes structural and political: what a court does to itself by existing only to hear about itself, and what is actually lost when the last thing anyone in it can say is true.
