# World: The Bell Foundry

Gethin Bell is a hill town of eleven hundred people that has cast bells for four hundred years, and it casts them badly on purpose: every bell is poured with one deliberate flaw cut into the metal by a moulder who knows exactly what he is doing, and the flaw decides what the bell is allowed to say for the rest of its life. A bell with a seam in the shoulder screams an hour ahead of itself and can warn a burning terrace, but it cannot be rung for a wedding without making the bride's family wince. A bell that hums will count the hours all night and cannot be stopped by a tired hand at four in the morning. The town has survived by writing down which flaw goes with which purpose, one line per bell, in a leather casting book kept in the counting house upstairs — and the book has just produced its first century in which Gethin Bell has a bell for the fire, a bell for the dead, a bell for the tide, a bell for the school, and no bell at all for the thing the town most needs to say.

## Setting

Gethin Bell stands on a wet limestone shoulder above the Corrach valley, two hundred and forty feet above the river, in a climate that is mild, wet, and soaked through in October. The town is a single steep street of grey limestone rubble with slate roofs weighted in wire, lime-washed chimneys, and iron-bellied stoves; the smell is wet slate, cattle, coal smoke, and the specific hot-iron smell that gets into wool cloaks and stays for a season. Rain comes sideways off the moor and the whole town hears it in the bells — a hundred small ringing noises, none of them a bell, all of them the rain hitting the tower frames. Beyond the last house the ground falls away into the Corrach, a fast green river in a limestone gorge, and the valley floor is all pasture, drystone, and a single line of flooded gravel that changes shape every year and is the subject of two lawsuits that have run for a hundred and ten years.

The foundry itself is the town's reason for existing. It is a long, low, soot-blackened shed on the west side with three chimney stacks, a coke yard, a two-storey casting pit cut into the hillside, and a wet scrap field sorted into lead, bell-metal, and useless. Around it the whole town is organised around heat: a coke and coal wharf at the foot, a horse-drawn tram up the hill, water tanks that must be filled before every pour because the moulds cannot survive a bad hour, and a school, a bakery, and a laundry that all have contracts to be quiet between noon and four on casting days. The people who work in the shed are foundery, moulders, founders, gangers, and bell-ringers, and the division of labour between them is a hundred and fifty years of very careful resentment.

Food is hill food: sheep, oatcakes, laverbread in July, damson cheese, a thin brown leek soup, and a barley drink that the foundry drinkers insist is medicinal. Market day is Tuesday at the lime yard: a hard, cold, bright-bellied business of coal, ingots, tin, lead sheet, wire, brass, charcoal, sand, clay, bell-metal scrap bought by weight from every church in four counties, three fiddle players, and one argument about the lawsuit. The single genuinely warm public room is the Cloth Hall, a barrelled timber-framed building that is the nearest thing Gethin Bell has to a parlour and the only place a bell cannot reach uninvited.

## The Magic

1. **Every bell must be flawed.** A bell cast without a deliberate flaw will not ring. It will sit in its headstock a whole year looking like a bell and produce nothing but a faint dead thump that people can feel through the floor and not hear. There is no way to know this before the pour.
2. **The flaw is the bell's capacity, and it is only ever one capacity.** A flaw gives a bell exactly one thing it can do. It can count hours, or it can carry a voice over distance, or it can shatter, or it can sound a note that changes what people do, or it can hold a note until the holder loses the thread. It cannot do two things. It is not permitted to be generally useful.
3. **The flaw is set in the mould before the pour, and it is a decision, not an accident.** A moulder cuts it. A founder cannot argue him out of it. A flaw that is found later in the metal was put there, and the only honest explanation is that somebody chose it.
4. **The town names a bell's flaw in words, and the naming makes it true in that bell.** A bell whose flaw has been named aloud to a person who believes it, and written in a book, will keep to that flaw forever. It cannot be renamed. A bell whose flaw has been described in a book as a defect is a bell the book has finished with.
5. **A bell can be rung only for the purpose its flaw serves.** This is the law of the casting book and it is enforced by nothing at all except shame, which has so far worked for four hundred years. Ring the fire bell at a wedding and the flaw will not perform — the sound will be there and it will be wrong, and everyone in the valley will know precisely whose hall it was.
6. **The cost is the flaw. A bell can never be remoulded, recast, or improved.** Recasting is permitted and is the deepest disgrace in the town, because the recast bell is heard for its whole life as a bell that tried to be something else. No bell in Gethin Bell has been recast since 1788.
7. **The limit is the hour.** No bell can sound at night without a named human hand on the rope, awake and intending the sound. A bell left on a timer, or a bell rung by a child asleep, or a bell rung by a person thinking about something else, gives a half sound that the whole valley hears as a mistake and cannot be explained.
8. **A flaw may be given rather than cut.** A founder may, once in a career, and in a bell that will be his own to ring, cast a flaw he did not cut — the flaw the metal wanted, described aloud by the pourer in the moment the metal comes out of the ladle. It is unpredictable, it cannot be planned, and it is looked on as a sort of madness. It happens about once in nine castings.
9. **Break the rules and the bell keeps the name, and the town keeps the name.** A bell rung for the wrong purpose does not fall silent; it produces its flaw out of place, and the flaw is understood. The whole valley knows within the hour that a rule was broken, and nobody can remember afterwards exactly what they heard, only that it was wrong. The same is true of a flaw removed from the book by a clerk: the bell goes on doing precisely what it was always going to do.
10. **A bell out of its headstock, in a yard, in the open air, is not a bell.** It is a heap of bronze. It cannot be heard at all. Bells only speak inside a frame, hung, and the frame is a promise somebody is standing in the rain keeping.

## Cast

**Nan Ilva**, 44, founder of the Gethin Bell foundry, the fourth woman to hold the job. *Want:* to cast a bell that will be hung in the town and be worth the water and coal it takes. *Flaw:* she reads every casting book that has ever been sent to her and cannot leave a line uncorrected, even a line that is not wrong, only old. *Detail:* burn-white crescents on both forearms, exactly the width of a ladle handle, and a habit of pushing her sleeves down before she answers a question. *Speech habit:* states the temperature first, always — "eighteen hundred, and falling" — and treats every topic as a heat problem.

**Ebba Roke**, 15, a bell-ringer and the youngest hand on the ropes in the Cloth Hall tower. *Want:* to be given the rope for the one bell in town she has never been allowed to ring. *Flaw:* she wants it so much that she will ring bells out of order to be near the ringing, and the valley can hear the order. *Detail:* a strip of grey wool wound round her right palm, permanently damp, worn over a rope burn she will not show anybody. *Speech habit:* narrates what she is hearing — "that is the fourth, that was the second" — and cannot stop herself doing it in her sleep.

**Verity Ash**, 39, the town surveyor and the parish's Sister-in-law, who measures the noise. *Want:* to have the valley's sound described by somebody other than the foundry, once, in a document. *Flaw:* she measures everything, including the arguments, and has learned to answer a question with a figure when the person asked wanted a yes. *Detail:* a waxed notebook on a lanyard, and a habit of putting two fingers flat on the sill of a window to feel the frame while she listens. *Speech habit:* gives distances and tolerances — "forty paces, give or take the wind" — and will not give an opinion while a gauge is in her hand.

**Aldous Crenn**, 58, casting clerk, forty-one years in the counting house, keeper of the leather book. *Want:* to reach retirement with the book in one unbroken sequence of legible hands. *Flaw:* he has never in his life written a word he did not have a precedent for, and he will not begin one now, and this is how the town ended up unable to record a decision. *Detail:* a green brass reading arm bolted to the desk at eye height, and a cataract-yellow left eye he reads around rather than with. *Speech habit:* quotes the previous entry — "the book says, in the hand of Ferrow—" and then stops, waiting, as if the sentence might finish itself.

**Odo Marrack**, 67, retired founder, thirty-one years at the ladle, and the only man in Gethin Bell who has taken the ninth rule seriously enough to attempt it twice. *Want:* to be asked whether it worked, and to be told the truth by somebody who was not in the shed. *Flaw:* he has told the story of his two given flaws to so many people that the story has grown a third one he never cast. *Detail:* a badly set left hand, two fingers short since 1971, and a black felt hat he wears indoors and out because his scalp is bald and he is not embarrassed. *Speech habit:* begins anecdotes with "I am not going to say this is advice, but" and then gives advice.

**Tamsin Lowe**, 22, an apprentice moulder with the lang-casting bench, cutting flaws into wet sand. *Want:* to be given the night pour, alone, with the town asleep and the mould his own. *Flaw:* she has begun cutting flaws she finds interesting rather than flaws the town needs, and the difference has not yet cost anybody anything, which is the dangerous part. *Detail:* the crescent of a sand-cake burn on the inside of her right wrist, and sand in her eyebrows that nobody ever tells her about. *Speech habit:* asks the question she already knows the answer to — "so which is it meant to be?" — and then waits through the silence.

## Places

**The Long Shed.** Two hundred and forty feet of low blackened timber and iron, three chimney stacks, a coke yard at one end and the casting pit at the other, lit entirely by the furnace mouth and by daylight through a roof of wired glass gone the colour of tea. The whole floor is a working geometry of moulds half-buried in sand, crucibles on barrows, tongs, and troughs of running water; the sound is a hundred kinds of hiss at once, and when the ladle comes out, every conversation in the building stops inside one second.

**The Casting Pit.** A two-storey brick throat cut into the hillside, entered by a stair, with a lifting gantry on a chain and a small furnace glowing at the bottom like an animal. It is the hottest place in four counties, it is cooled by a stream that arrives through a pipe in the wall, and it is where the pour happens: bell-metal at thirteen hundred degrees, in a ladle the size of a bathtub, going into a mould in a hundred and forty seconds. Everyone stands on the stair landing, which is the only place in the building where you can see the mould and stay out of the splash path.

**The Cloth Hall Bell Tower.** A square grey tower on the town's high point, with a louvred belfry, four bells on a modern wheel-and-rope system, and a stair that is cold even in August. The smallest bell is four hundredweight and is rung for the school and the curfew; the biggest is the fire bell, and its seam in the shoulder is three inches long and documented in four hands, and when it goes off the sound arrives in the valley as a scream that gets to you before the stroke does.

**The Counting House.** Two rooms over the foundry office, low-ceilinged, lit by one north window and a brass lamp on a swing arm, lined floor to ceiling with bound ledgers. The Casting Book lives on a lectern at the window because it is old enough to be handled by two people at once. The room is the quietest in Gethin Bell and the only place where the sound from outside has to be guessed at.

**The Lime Yard.** The market square: beaten lime and gravel, a ring of stone setts, a hundred years of notice-slate on a post, coal stacked in a wall, ingots stacked in a row, and three fiddle players who have never once in living memory played at the same time. The whole town comes here on Tuesday, and for two hours nobody can hear the bells over the market, which everybody has always assumed is a coincidence.

**The Scrap Field.** A sloping field of broken bells west of the foundry — hundreds of them, cracked, split, silent, sold for weight, lying in grass and puddles with their inscriptions upside down. The oldest are medieval and are in pieces. It is a place children are told not to play, and it is the only place in Gethin Bell where a bell has no voice at all and the silence is total.

## Visual Style

A hot-metal palette of wet slate grey and lime white, punctuated by furnace orange, bell-bronze gold, and one cold note of river green. Lighting is interior and low: a single roaring furnace mouth throwing hard orange across black timber, or a high wired-glass roof gone the colour of tea, with a cold blue-grey daylight at the far end of the shed where a door is open. Materials are coke dust, blackened iron, sand moulds, dripping bronze, sweating horses, wet slate, lead sheet, brass fittings, and wool soaked through with rain. The era reads late-industrial northern Europe, 1880s-1910s: chimney stacks, horse trams, a leather book on a lectern, no electricity, no fantasy armour, nothing shiny that is not bronze. The single recurring motif, reused across every cover in this world, is one bronze bell, mouth-down, with its crown stripped back and its flaw a visible seam of dull red, glowing under the furnace light.

## Motifs

1. **The seam in the shoulder** — a dull red line of exposed metal in the bronze, three inches long, filed visible by a founder who refused to hide it.
2. **The ladder of grey and green flames** on the mould, the pattern of a defect every moulder memorises and no passer-by can read.
3. **The ledger line** — a single ruled line on cream paper, one bell, one flaw, one word for the purpose, and nothing else.
4. **The four-tonne handwheel**, spun by a child in a wool coat, and the sound arriving in your back teeth before it arrives in your ears.
5. **The ladle**, the two-man crucible of hot bell-metal, swinging on a chain, and the four seconds in which a bell either takes its flaw or does not.

## Thematic Spine

All nine stories are about the difference between a fault and a decision, and about what happens to a community when that difference is quietly closed. The accessible band treats it as attention: a child who is not allowed to ring, a moulder who notices, a bell that will not stop, the ordinary discipline of hearing what a thing is actually telling you. The intermediate band treats it as jurisdiction and consequence: a bell that obliges people it was never recorded for, a record with deliberate gaps in it, a flaw that arrives without anybody choosing it. The advanced band treats it as authorship: who may write down that somebody chose, and what it costs a book — and a town — to hold a column called decisions.
