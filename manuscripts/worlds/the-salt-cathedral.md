# World: The Salt Cathedral

Four hundred feet under the Kaitole Flats stands a basilica that is no longer made of stone and has not yet finished becoming salt. It was cut from rock, then flooded with brine, then sung dry; its columns are now glassy white crystal that hums when the air moves, its aisles grow a half-inch a week at the cold ends, and its floor is under a fine drift of grit that squeaks like snapped sugar under every boot. Six singers live down there in brass-tipped choir stalls and keep the whole enormous quiet thing in the shape of a church by singing, one note each, without stopping for more than an hour at a time. The one thing that is wrong is that somebody has started singing a wrong note on purpose, so that a particular gallery will grow faster than the book can account for — because under that growth, in a room made entirely of crystal, there is a page.

## Setting

The Kaitole Flats are nine hundred square miles of white pan, hard as a table at noon and soft as a bruise at dusk, ringed by a low blue escarpment called the Rim. Rain almost never falls; the surface crust is a cracked polygon pattern forty feet across, and the wind over it makes a sound locals describe as a room being swept. Salt is harvested the old way, cut into blocks by hand with long hooked poles, and hauled up the freight cage at Kaitole Station, a corrugated-iron shed with a winch, a coal stove, and a wall of dated chalk tallies. The village of Verrick sits at the wellhead: forty-one houses, a bakery, a school, a chapel that is not the cathedral, and a pub called the Unwitnessed with a genuinely bad sign. Everything underground is quiet, and the quiet has a floor to it. Down in the nave, the temperature never moves from fifty-one degrees, the air tastes faintly of a dentist and of rain that has not happened, and candlelight on wet crystal throws more light than candles have any right to — soft, doubled, refracted a second time off every facet. A single brass lamp carried down the stairs throws a moving shadow that arrives in the crystal before the person does. Food is salt, always salt: sheep's cheese, cured fish, a dense flatbread called traybread, bitter tea with goat milk, and enormous quantities of pickled things in stone crocks. Market day in Verrick is a Friday, and it is a trade in labour rather than goods: the ledger of the day is the block tally, the shift rota, the water allocation, and the argument about the cage, which is always late. Transport down is the cage or a hundred and forty ladder rungs; transport sideways is a walking survey with a brass rule and a candle. Nobody goes down for pleasure. Everybody goes down eventually.

## The Magic

1. **Salt Remembers Shape.** Salt that has been near a thing keeps that thing. The basilica was a stone church for two hundred years, then was deliberately soaked in brine and sung dry, and now its salt is holding the shape of a church as a matter of fact rather than memory. Salt in this world is not inert: it is obedient, and it will hold any pattern put into it — a door, a stair, a name — provided the pattern is sung.

2. **Six Voices, Six Orders of Salt.** The choir is six and always six. Each voice carries one order of salt: **still** (bass, holds and calms), **drift** (tenor, moves and shapes), **plate** (alto, sets the hard surfaces flat and level), **spire** (soprano, sets the vertical — columns, arches, the lintel lines), **brine** (canto, keeps the water moving so it does not lock), and **silence** (the sixth, which does not sing a note but holds the room's own quiet in shape, keeping the air out of the other five). If a voice is absent, its order is not maintained, and its surface does its own thing.

3. **One Note per Column.** Each of the cathedral's 411 columns is a plate column and answers to exactly one note. Sung true, it sets: it stays hard, clear, and where it was. Sung badly, or not at all, it creeps, dulls, and grows soft white bloom at its base, and the growth is what eats the aisles.

4. **Witness and Entry.** A note is only sung if another living person hears it sung; and a column is only *paid* for if its note is entered in the **Ledger of Columns** in the Ledger Room, in ink, by hand, with a date. This is the whole law. Unwitnessed notes are heard by the salt and not by the book, and unwitnessed growth is a debt.

5. **The Cost of a Note.** A sustained note draws salt out of the singer: white crust on the lips, a sore dry throat, salt crescents under the nails, the sweat gone to brine. Sing hard and the cost comes out of the teeth. This is why the choir drinks constantly and why their hands are always in water, not cups.

6. **The Set Voice.** A singer who passes the limit does not die. They set: they become a clear upright pillar, still, blind, and silent to themselves, forever holding one perfect note that the rest of the choir can lean on. This is recorded as a column, not as a death. It is the worst thing that happens in this world and everyone treats it administratively, which is what makes it awful.

7. **The Outside Count.** The Ledger's totals are kept by hand and the book has a fixed number of ruled pages: 411. A column that is not entered has no page, so its growth is not in the count — it is a debt, and debts accrue. When the uncounted growth exceeds what the counted structure can hold, the salt begins taking back its own.

8. **The Closing.** The Ledger's remedy is written on the inside cover and is not secret: to keep the total true, the book cancels. A cancelled column is struck, its page is stamped CANCELLED, and the salt it was holding is released back to brine. Cancellations are published in order, one page at a time, and the schedule is what everything in this world is really about.

9. **Salt Does Not Lie. It Only Returns.** The growth is not malicious and never speaks. It is a filing system with an appetite: it grows around whatever was never written down, because a form with no entry is not a thing to a form. If anyone had stood beside it and sung the fact aloud and entered it, the salt would have set around it happily in a minute. The brine in the Wellmouth is not a monster under the floor; it is the only liquid left in nine hundred square miles, and it is warm.

## Cast

**Ilde Marrow**, 47, the Cantrix — she leads the six and has led them for twenty-two years, which is longer than anyone can hear of. *Wants:* to stop singing and be allowed to leave the cathedral alive and unrecorded. *Flaw:* she believes the choir is a debt the cathedral should have died out of, and has been quietly shortening her own sets and rationing the others to keep the ledger from noticing. *Physical detail:* a white moustache of salt crust above her upper lip, kept daily with a small three-inch file she carries in her breast pocket. *Speech habit:* she answers everything in the note she was last holding, so her replies are pitched, and strangers mistake her for strange until she drops to a speaking voice to be kind.

**Garrick Bole**, 31, ledger clerk and second tenor (drift). *Wants:* to be the first man in four generations to enter his own name in the book. *Flaw:* total literality — he will not round a figure, will not merge two entries belonging to one person, and cannot forgive anyone for tidying. *Physical detail:* a leather finger-cuff on his right forefinger, brass-shod and worn to a shine, used to steady his hand on a ruled page. *Speech habit:* quotes the ledger's exact wording and reaches constantly for the word "per" — *per the count, per the entry, per the schedule.*

**Nissa Ashkel**, 22, soprano (spire), great-granddaughter of one of the six names on the founding page. *Wants:* to find the single note that will stop the growing gallery without costing anyone a set voice. *Flaw:* she can hear the column that is growing, and cannot stop listening to it, and has been sleeping eleven hours a day from the effort of not answering. *Physical detail:* the front fall of her hair bleached bone-white in a hard fringe, singed by sleeping too close to the sunlit shaft at the crossing. *Speech habit:* repeats the last three words she heard before she answers, whatever they are.

**Ordin Brack**, 58, bass (still) and warden of the Wellmouth Cistern, where the brine tap is. *Wants:* to keep the tap open and the spring flowing, and to be left alone about it. *Flaw:* he is certain he is the only one who understands what is happening, and he answers every question about the book with an answer about the water. *Physical detail:* his beard braided once a year and bound with copper wire, and a permanent pale crust of dried pan-mud on both forearms from the cistern ladder. *Speech habit:* speaks so quietly that people must walk closer, saying only "Closer. The floor carries."

**Lida Trask**, 39, the Reader of the Ledger, forty years old in the book. *Wants:* to keep the ledger true. *Flaw:* she genuinely believes truth and kindness can be scheduled to be compatible, and she has been reordering the cancellation list for two years to spare people. *Physical detail:* a black mourning ribbon renewed every twelfth month on her left wrist for the one column she cancelled, on her own authority, in her twentieth year. *Speech habit:* speaks about cancelled columns in the present tense — *the gallery stands, it is not entered* — and expects others to do the same.

**Hesper Vail**, 17, the under-reader, the youngest person permitted in the cathedral, whose chore is reading the cancelled pages aloud to the others as a check. *Wants:* to be entered in the book as a working choir member, in ink, with a date. *Flaw:* she believes being recorded is the same as being real, and she is seventeen and has not yet found out otherwise. *Physical detail:* a brass-tipped hairpin holding back her hair, and salt-white crescents under both nails from handling damp pages. *Speech habit:* announces what she is about to do in the third person before she does it — *Hesper is closing the book now.*

## Places

**The Nave of Glass Salt.** Four hundred feet long, ninety across, thirty-nine columns down each side, and a temperature that never has been anything but fifty-one degrees. The crystal is not clear but milk-white with a green heart, and the light of forty candles comes back doubled, so the whole space glows softly from inside itself like something asleep. Move a candle and your shadow arrives ahead of you, standing in the aisle, waiting.

**The Growing Gallery.** A side aisle that was a sacristy two hundred years ago and is now a corridor of wet crystal pushing outward at roughly a half-inch a week, always in the same four directions at once. It is not beautiful; it is very smooth, faintly warm, and it hums a fifth above the choir, which is the reason the choir has not slept properly in a year. At its end, behind eleven feet of growth, there is a small room, and in the room there is a page.

**The Ledger Room.** Eight feet square, above the sacristy stair, deliberately too warm, with one candle, one brass-cornered lectern, and one stool nobody but Lida Trask has ever been allowed to sit on. It is the only room in the cathedral with no crystal in it, because a ledger kept in the damp grows a page a season and the book is 411 pages and there are no more.

**The Wellmouth Cistern.** A flooded chamber under the crossing where warm brine stands still and clear in a black pool, and a copper tap set into rock that someone has polished for two centuries. The light down here is amber off the copper and the water is the loudest thing in the cathedral. It smells faintly of a hospital and of eggs. The floor is wet and every footstep carries.

**The Six Stalls.** A railed loft above the crossing, six brass-tipped choir stalls set in a curve so the singers can see the floor and not each other, worn to a mirror shine at the mouth of each seat by four hundred years of one singer leaning forward to listen. Rungs, brackets, a chest for water, a chest for bread, and a coat nobody claims.

**Kaitole Flats and Verrick.** The surface: a white pan under a enormous sky, cracked into polygons, a corrugated iron station shed with a freight cage, a windmill, a hundred and forty ladder rungs going down a shaft cut in the rock, and the village of Verrick forty-one houses deep in the smell of the bakery. Fridays are market day and the market is a trade in shifts, block tallies, water allocation, and one argument about the cage.

## Visual Style

A cold neutral palette: salt white and bone, wet slate grey, brine green-black, chalk, and the warm amber of brass and candlelight as the only saturated colour in frame. Lighting is always low and always doubled — candlelight, lamp light, and the pale green inner glow of crystal that refracts it a second time, so every image has soft-edged shadows arriving ahead of the person casting them. Materials are glassy salt crystal with wet highlights, black wrought iron, unlacquered and verdigris brass, copper polished by hands, wool, brine-soaked rope, and pan-mud; the era feel is pre-industrial basilica crossed with nineteenth-century clerical bureaucracy, ledger paper, ink, stamps, wax seals, no electric light, everything hand-carried and hand-written. The recurring motif is a ruled page: parallel lines, or crystal facets that behave exactly like ruled lines, running through every composition.

## Motifs

1. **The Ledger of Columns** — a hand-bound book, brass-cornered, 411 ruled pages, one per column, in the Cantrix's shorthand; the interior of the front cover carries the cancelling schedule in smaller, neater writing.
2. **The half-inch a week** — growth measured with a brass rule held flat across the floor, and a date chalked beside the mark; the most repeated image in the world's stories.
3. **The six brass-tipped stalls**, mirror-worn at the mouth of each seat, with water and bread in the chests beneath.
4. **Salt in the body** — white crust on a moustache, crescents under fingernails, a dry voice, a heavy hand on the rail, a singer drinking constantly.
5. **A note arriving before the person does** — sound travelling through the stone and crystal ahead of footsteps and breath, the whole building slightly out of speaking distance with you.

## Thematic Spine

Underneath all nine stories is one question: what happens to the things nobody stood and witnessed, and does being unrecorded mean you were never real. Nobody in this world is a villain; the pressure comes from a correct, closable, dated institution that is only trying to make its total true, and every story turns on a person deciding whether to enter a fact, correct an entry, or quietly round a number. The accessible band takes the moral as kindness to unnamed things — measure it, name it, write it back, and the salt is glad. The intermediate band takes it as procedural honesty: the number is wrong, find out why, and do not tidy. The advanced band takes it as the cost of keeping any record at all, and asks what a person agrees to when they agree to be written down.
