# World: The Counterweight

Wyver Hold is a slate harbour town of two thousand four hundred people on a cold coast, and it is connected to the moor above it by a single cable railway: the Grip, one mile and one quarter, seventeen degrees, built in 1888 and rebuilt twice since. The Grip is not a lift. A lift is a machine you trust. The Grip is an argument between two iron cars hanging on opposite ends of the same cable, and the entire town is built on the fact that the uphill car can only rise if the downhill car is heavier than it, and the entire town is also built on the fact that this is a promise rather than a measurement. Every car that goes up is paid for by somebody standing on the balance pan at the bottom, on the other end, saying their name once. Wyver Hold does not weigh its passengers. It asks them.

## Setting

The town sits in the mouth of a slate river that runs down from the moor in winter and stands in a brown lake in summer. Below the harbour wall are the sheds, the loading stage, a fish market that smells of ice and diesel, and the lower station of the Grip with its iron-and-glass frontage facing the water. Above, four hundred and ten feet up, is Tannery Moss, where the slates are cut and the town keeps two hundred and sixty working houses, a school, a hospital with four beds, a mechanics' institute, and the moor. Between them: seventeen degrees of nothing that anyone has ever managed to build on.

The Grip is two cars of pressed steel on a single steel cable over twenty-two trestles, running on a water ballast tank at each end. The cable turns on a drum in the winding house at the bottom. The winding house is the heart of the town and the only building anybody argues about. There is a winding room, a balance room where the counterweight pans hang, an office with the consent book, a mess room, and a cabin above the drum where the grip-man sits through every hour of every journey.

The slate trade has been dying for thirty years. What is left is the harbour trade, a small woollen mill at the millpond, the ferry, tourism in the summer, and the Grip, which brings up the walkers, the surveyors, the doctors, and the occasional person who has come to see the view and does not understand that somebody is standing on a pan at the bottom of the hill holding the world in place for their benefit.

Weather is the town's clock. The Grip closes above twenty-eight knots of wind, stops in icing, and has been shut for a total of eleven days in a year. The town talks about wind the way other towns talk about money.

## The Magic

1. **The uphill car rises only if the balance car is heavier.** This is not a metaphor and there is no tolerance. If the loaded balance car does not exceed the loaded grip car by at least the weight of one adult, the cable will not move, and the grip-man cannot force it by any means short of breaking the drum.
2. **Balance is given, never taken.** No one is compelled. No employer may require it, no parent may assign it, and a balance given under any kind of threat does not count and will not appear to hold.
3. **Consent is one sentence and it must contain your own name.** You say it at the balance pan in the winding house, out loud, with the grip-man hearing it. `"I hold for the up."` is not consent. Neither is a nod, a signature, a mark, or a name written by a clerk. It is one spoken sentence containing your name.
4. **A name may be spoken once per year, by each person, and only for the line they live on.** A slate-quarryman may hold for the lower line and not for the mill. Holding for a line you do not live on does not balance.
5. **Holding is repaid in labour, never in coin.** The rule is that the balance is worked out over the year, and the town's custom is that everyone holds whether or not they ride. The unwritten part, which everybody obeys, is that the people who can least afford the hour are the people who give the most of it.
6. **The consent book is the law.** A name is in the book or it is not. There is no third place, and a name that is in the book and was not spoken is a forgery that the cable knows about. A name removed from the book stops holding at the moment of removal, wherever the person is.
7. **A car may not run empty.** An empty grip car going up requires the balance car to be heavier than the empty car by more than the weight of an adult, which is why the rule exists at all. The Grip cannot move without somebody's weight in the other end.
8. **The limit is the hour.** No holding may be given or taken while the cable is moving. A name spoken mid-journey does nothing, and a name crossed out mid-journey stops at the next brake. The grip-man pulls the brake rather than trust anybody's timing.
9. **The cost is the time, and the time is not recoverable.** A person who holds does not ride. That is the whole exchange, and it is why the best-known man in Wyver Hold is a man who has never been to the top of his own town.
10. **A balance broken mid-journey is not a fall.** If the balance is wrong, the cable simply stops, in whatever weather, in whatever position, and holds there until it is right. It has happened eleven times in thirty-one years. Nobody has ever been hurt. The town simply stops.

## Cast

**Ivo Trant**, 52, grip-man of the lower station, thirty-one years on the drum and the last man who can hold the house single-handed. *Want:* to be allowed to retire to the top of the town, where his daughter lives, having never once made the journey he has spent a career running. *Flaw:* he has never given an order in his life that he could not have given as a request, and at the top of a descent in a gale that is a thing that will kill somebody. *Detail:* a red woollen mitten on the left hand only, because the right is permanently white to the second joint, and a habit of counting aloud under his breath in fours. *Speech habit:* gives wind speeds and brake times before anything else — "gusting forty at the mast, she'll hold" — and treats every problem as something with a stopping distance.

**Nell Cardew**, 16, the youngest girl who is permitted on the balance pans, which is a job and not a privilege and pays eleven shillings. *Want:* to ride to the top of the moor. *Flaw:* she has never once admitted to anybody that she would not, and has built an entire personality out of not wanting it, and the architecture is starting to show. *Detail:* a wicker splash of boiled leather and a permanent tan line across her forehead, and chalk on her fingers from the consent book. *Detail second:* she eats her lunch standing up, always facing the drum. *Speech habit:* answers questions with numbers, and when she cannot, says a time instead.

**Gwilym Ore**, 61, winding mechanic, the last man who can strip a drum in a day and the only one who trusts the machine. *Want:* to be asked why the cable on the upper span was replaced in 1911 and never explained, because it was explained once and he was not in the room. *Flaw:* he believes the machine is honest and he cannot be made to consider that it might be merely accurate. *Detail:* a leather apron with a horseshoe burn across the front and a pencil behind his ear that he has forgotten is there. *Speech habit:* corrects weights and dates and never uses the word "because".

**Bronwen Vasey**, 38, harbourmaster's clerk, who holds the survey of the line, and who keeps the second book. *Want:* to have the arithmetic of the line audited by somebody from outside the town. *Flaw:* she has audited it eleven times herself, and every audit has cleared it, and she has begun to understand that an audit is a thing that is done by a person and not by a number. *Detail:* a ruled notebook, a tin tube, and spectacles on a cord that she removes to look at anything at distance. *Speech habit:* gives tolerances — "two stone on a car, that is the margin" — and cannot state a figure as exact.

**Aldith Marner**, 29, the schoolmistress, who teaches two hundred and six children the hour by the Grip and believes that this is the best arithmetic in Wales. *Want:* to have a pupil fail the consent rule in public, on purpose, so that they will know it can be refused. *Flaw:* she is frightened of the water and has never ridden the upper section, and has therefore never seen the view she describes to her class every Friday. *Detail:* chalk dust on the cuffs of a jacket that is much too good, and a habit of counting aloud under her breath before she answers. *Speech habit:* asks questions in the imperative and then waits, and the waiting is the entire method.

**Teofil Rownt**, 44, slate merchant, the largest single holder on the lower line, who has never ridden the Grip as a passenger in his life. *Want:* to be able to say that the line is run in his interest, which would be true, and which he has never been able to say out loud. *Flaw:* he has organised the holders into a rota that is so efficient that nobody now feels the weight of it, and a rule nobody feels is a rule that will not survive its first test. *Detail:* a brass token on a chain, worn through at the edge, and shoes too good for a slate town. *Speech habit:* offers terms — "for the year, then" — and cannot finish a sentence that has not been costed.

## Places

**The Winding House.** A long grey stone building at the bottom of the hill with a corrugated roof, a glazed frontage facing the harbour, and a drum room you can hear from the street. Inside: the drum, the balance room, the office, the mess room. The whole building is built around the fact that the drum must be attended, and there is a bed in the cabin above it that Ivo Trant has slept in four thousand nights.

**The Balance Room.** Six iron pans hanging on chains over a pit, two deep and one shallow, each one cut for a person to stand in and be counted. The floor is worn into a curve by eleven thousand pairs of boots. The pit grating below is the only place in Wyver Hold where you can see the full weight of what the town is doing.

**The Consent Book.** A single volume, calfskin, four hundred pages, begun in 1889, on the desk in the office. It is a list of names and dates and lines. It has been kept by eleven clerks. Its handwriting changes when the clerk changes and does not change back.

**The Upper Station.** Tannery Moss, slate roofs, a stone terminus with a wooden floor worn into grooves, a stove, and a view that people come from three counties to stand in the rain for. No one who works on the lower line has ever stood in it.

**The Harbour Wall.** A mile of wet granite with a crane at the end, and the town walking on it on a Sunday because it is the only flat ground and it is where the wind is least. The Grip's lower station glows across the water at night like a lantern.

**The Trestle Path.** The walk beside the cable from the bottom to the top, four miles of switchbacks, closed in winter, where you can stand level with the carriage and hear the drum through the ground.

## Visual Style

Cold north-coast light: wet slate grey and granite green under a low sea-light, with one warm interior note of oil-lamp orange and one sharp accent of signal red on a lamp, a coat, or a painted iron panel. Materials are pressed steel, galvanised wire, riveted plate, oiled hemp, wet timber, salt-stained granite, slate dust and tar. Figures are working people in wool and waxed cotton: a wind-reddened face, a red woollen mitten on one hand, chalk on the fingers. The cable and the drum are the recurring objects: a taut steel line running level across the top of the frame, and a drum turning at the bottom of it. The era reads 1880s-1910s: no electric light, no modern safety gear, horses and handcarts, and a signal lamp that is lit by hand.

## Motifs

1. **The balance pan** — an iron pan on a chain, one person standing in it, cut deep and worn to a curve.
2. **The consent book** — one open calfskin page, ruled in three columns, a name written in ink and the date beside it, nothing else.
3. **The drum** — a steel cylinder turning, and a man's hands flat on the brake lever.
4. **The red mitten** — worn on the left hand only, the right bare and white to the second joint.
5. **The cable line** running dead level across the upper third of the picture, with weather on both sides of it.

## Thematic Spine

All nine stories are about the difference between a measurement and a promise, and about what happens to a community when that difference is quietly closed. The accessible band treats it as weight: a child who cannot go up, a boy doing sums to prove he cannot go down, a car that will not move and the reason nobody will say out loud. The intermediate band treats it as obligation and record: a book that has grown a column, a gale that makes rule nine a trap, a single name crossed out and a town discovering what it depends on. The advanced band treats it as authorship: who may write down that somebody agreed, what it costs a book to hold a rota, and whether a promise can be altered by the people it is made of.
