# World: The House That Was Promised

Marl is a fen-edge village of brick makers where a house is not a building, it is a promise kept upright, and the three houses at the end of the furlong are the most litigated three structures in the county. The story everybody knows has a straw house, a stick house, a brick house, and a wolf, and it is told in Marl as a story about a mortgage. Because that is what it is. A house stands for as long as its promise is kept, it falls the moment the promise is broken, and a wolf is a licensed creditor with a book and a licence and a very specific set of powers and no others at all. The boy with the straw was not lazy. He could not afford brick, and he built what he could afford, and the thing he built had no standing, and the wolf came for it in nine days.

## Setting

Marl stands where the clay fen gives way to the dry land, on a low ridge of good brick clay, with three brickfields, a kiln, a weighbridge, and about nine hundred people who have built everything themselves since 1871. Brick is the material with standing in Marl: it does what it was asked, it does it in a hundred and fifty years, and it is the only building material in the county with a reputation. Stone is scarce, timber is scarcer, and there is no slate in four miles. This is why Marl has buildings and why Marl has no money, and both of those facts are the same fact.

The village is a single street of red brick, a chapel, a smithy, a weighbridge hut, and the three houses at the end of the furlong, which stand on the highest point because the land there is worst, which is where the poor are put. The trade is brick, and the season is April to October, and the winter is unemployment, and Marl's particular genius is that it has spent a hundred and fifty years building a legal and financial apparatus to manage a shortage of money that it caused itself by refusing to build anything out of anything else.

## The Magic

1. **A house is a promise.** It stands for as long as the promise is kept and it falls the moment the promise is broken, and it is rebuilt by whoever can pay for a new promise. A house that has fallen can be rebuilt by anybody at all. That is the whole of the relief in this story and it is not nothing.
2. **A material has a standing.** Brick is a standing material: it does what it was asked, for a hundred and fifty years. Timber is a standing material that will do what it was asked by somebody else. Straw is not a standing material, and a house of straw is a house whose promise nobody signed, and it stands only while the person who promised it is watching.
3. **A builder may promise anything. The cost of the promise is charged to the house, not to the builder,** and the house pays it whether or not anybody comes to blow, and the charge is entered in the house's own book and is visible on the walls to anybody who knows how to read a wall.
4. **A wolf is a creditor.** Any creditor may apply for a wolf, and the wolf is licensed, and the wolf's licence states exactly what it may take, and the wolf is a professional and is not permitted to want anything.
5. **A wolf may not take a thing the debtor has not promised.** A wolf that takes outside the promise is unlicensed, and an unlicensed wolf becomes a dog, permanently and irreversibly, and may be kept, and is often kept.
6. **The default of a house is resolved by whoever arrives first with a builder.** There is no court and there is no appeal. There is a licence, a queue, and a ledger at the smithy, and the queue is where the story spends most of its time.
7. **Blowing is not permitted. Blowing is an assessment,** and any licensed assessor may assess, of which the wolf is one, and a licensed assessor may blow, and the sound is a sound the walls are obliged to report on.
8. **A house may be remortgaged. A house that has been remortgaged twice may not be sold,** and the second creditor is the one who keeps it, and the second creditor is not permitted to sell it either, and this is the mechanism by which Marl has lost four houses in eighty years.
9. **A promise made to a child is not a promise until the child is grown, and a child may not be a creditor at all.** Every rule about children in Marl is one sentence long and every one of them is absolute.
10. **A house asked what it is made of and answered honestly is safe for a year. A house asked and lied to is not safe for a night,** and a house that has never once been asked is a house that nobody in the village can answer for, and the wind finds those first.

## Cast

**Tomas Peake**, 14, a brickfield boy, the youngest hand on the kiln gang and the only person at the end of the furlong who can read the wall. *Want:* to be given a builder's licence, which he cannot have for six years, and which he wants for reasons he will explain for a whole book. *Flaw:* he has been reading the three houses' walls for four years without being asked and knows more about the village's finances than anybody over thirty. *Detail:* a permanent white brick-dust line at the hairline, and a habit of touching a wall as he passes it. *Speech habit:* quotes a charge exactly, and cannot pass a wall without reading it.

**Aline Peake**, 38, Tomas's mother, who built the stick house in nine days in 1904 and has spent thirty years being a woman who did that. *Want:* to be asked, once, whether it was the right thing to do. *Flaw:* she has said no to work for thirty years to stay the woman who did that, and has never been paid for the nine days of building either. *Detail:* a burn scar inside one wrist from a bucket of lime, and a habit of counting the bricks in a course by touching them. *Speech habit:* answers in the future tense about a thing that happened, every time.

**Fennick Roke**, 58, the smith, keeper of the queue ledger, the man who decides who builds next, and the last four men at the end of the furlong have all been in his smithy being told no. *Want:* to be told that the queue is not his fault. *Flaw:* he has been running the queue for twenty-six years by deciding who had a builder and who did not, and he has never once written down a rule for it, and there is no rule. *Detail:* a hammer-callus on a right shoulder, and a habit of looking at a man's hands before his face. *Speech habit:* answers "not this year" and cannot say why, and it is the most honest thing he says.

**Wren Copse**, 12, a girl from the terrace who applied, in writing, to be assessed on the straw house, and whose assessment is the reason the story has a shape. *Want:* to be told she cannot, in a way that sticks. *Flaw:* she has already begun building, on the worst ground, out of what she can find, and has made a promise, and does not have a licence, and the wolf may come. *Detail:* a bricklayer's glove with no hand in it, carried on a string. *Speech habit:* asks for the figure before the permission, and always gets it.

**Obadiah Cressy**, 81, retired wolf-holder, thirty years with a licence, the last man in Marl to be granted one, and the only one to refuse a renewal in 1989. *Want:* to be asked whether it was right. *Flaw:* he is ashamed in a way that has never been spoken and has taught one son to be a wolf and one son to be an assessor and has never told either of them which. *Detail:* a jaw that clicks, and a habit of sitting with his back to a wall. *Speech habit:* apologises to the house before he speaks to the person.

**Mira Sedge**, 31, a widow with two creditors and a remortgaged house and no means, who is the only person in Marl to have been through the machine twice and come out with the house standing. *Want:* to sell, which is the one thing she may not do. *Flaw:* she has decided the system is right and is defending it against her own interest, and is right, and it is costing her everything. *Detail:* a second creditor's key on a chain, worn through the cloth. *Speech habit:* says "that is fair" and then gives a figure that proves it.

## Places

**The Three Houses.** At the end of the furlong on the worst ground: one of brick, standing and sound; one of timber, standing and mortgaged; one of straw, fallen. The middle one is the reason the village has a queue, and the third one is the reason the village has a law.

**The Smithy.** The queue ledger lives on the bench, a great flat book, and has done since 1889. The forge, the anvil, the bellows, four men waiting, and the only warm room at the end of the furlong. Where Marl's entire financial system is administered by one man with a book and no rules.

**The Brickfields.** Three works, six miles of clay ridge, a horse pump, a moulder's bench, and the gang of six who cut and carry. Marl's economy in one field, in April light, with a hundred and forty thousand bricks a week going out to a world that Marl itself will not build in.

**The Kiln.** A bottle kiln on the third field, forty feet high, lit for eleven days and cold for nine months. The centre of Marl's year, and the only place in the village where a person is paid by the hour and the hours are the whole point.

**The Weighbridge Hut.** A brick hut at the road end with a weighbridge and a rate book, where a house is weighed and valued, which in Marl is not a metaphor.

**The Furlong End.** The high bad ground, twelve feet higher than the rest of the village, where the three houses are and where the wind comes over without anything to stop it.

## Visual Style

A palette of Marl red brick, wet clay brown, and one very cold fen grey, lit by hard low sun off the ridge or by the orange of a kiln mouth at night, with a hard blue north light on all the other faces. Materials: new red brick, weathered Flemish bond, lime mortar pointing, white brick dust, unseasoned stack, a wet clay field, a bottle kiln of forty feet, tarred timber, and, in one recurring and slightly comic detail, straw. The era reads 1870s-1900s, pre-electric: horse pump, handcart, a bicycle, a stone jug. The recurring motif is the charge — a figure written in chalk on a brick wall in a mason's hand, still legible, still being added to.

## Motifs

1. **The charge on the wall** — a figure in chalk on a course of brick, a mason's hand, a date, a promise, and the running total a house has been charged for existing.
2. **The queue book on the anvil bench** — a great flat ledger, open, four names waiting beside it.
3. **Three houses on a ridge** — brick, timber, straw, side by side on the worst ground in the village, seen from the road.
4. **A bottle kiln at night** — forty feet of brick, an orange mouth, a gang of six in silhouette, eleven days of firing.
5. **A bricklayer's glove with no hand in it**, carried on a string by a twelve-year-old, which is the most legally significant object at the end of the furlong.

## Thematic Spine

All nine stories are about what a place owes when it runs out of money, and about whether a rule that has never been written down is a rule. The accessible band treats it as a wall: an assessment, nine days of building, a door. The intermediate band treats it as a licence: a reading, a mortgage, a wolf. The advanced band treats it as a book: an audit, an unasked question, and a default that nobody designed.
