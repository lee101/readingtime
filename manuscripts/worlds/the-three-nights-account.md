# World: The Three Nights' Account

Nether Combe is a flax mill town on a river that runs brown in summer, and the story everybody there tells is about a miller who was left ruined in a fortnight and a strange little man who spun gold out of a wheel with no wheel on it. What they leave out, in the telling, is the invoice. The strange little man was a lender and the gold was security; a true name, given freely, was the capital; and the third night was not a threat but a term. In Nether Combe a name is not a word, it is an instrument, and once it is out of your mouth and into a roll you are not a person any more, you are an account, and accounts in a mill town are the only thing anybody is really frightened of. The version where the miller guesses the name is the version where the miller does nothing at all. Here, he spends four months trying to find out, and every attempt he makes is itself a transaction.

## Setting

Nether Combe is a valley town of two thousand people built around one flax mill, the Combe Mill, which is the largest wooden-framed building in three counties and the only reason the river bends. The mill runs from October to April; the rest of the year the wheel is a fence-post and the river goes brown and quiet and the town empties of men. The trade is flax, tow, linen, and the seed. The mill's credit is the town's life: nobody in Nether Combe is paid until the mill is paid, and the mill is paid by a merchant in Bristol who pays on the first of the month if the cloth is good and does not pay at all if it is not.

The spinner's room is at the top of the mill, under the roof, one door, no window that opens, and it is not in the mill and it is not in the town and nobody in Nether Combe will go up there without an appointment. What is kept up there is a roll: a bound ledger of lent names, in order, in five different hands going back to 1791, with the date of the loan, the term, the shape of the call, and the outcome. The roll is the most valuable object in the valley and it is also, on the evidence of 1971, not complete.

## The Magic

1. **A name is a debt instrument.** To give your true name to a lender, freely, is to give them the right to call it in — once, at a time of their choosing, in their own good time, with no notice. A name lent is lent for the lender's convenience, not the borrower's.
2. **A lender may only spin with a name given freely.** A name taken, overheard, repeated from a rumour, written down from a parish register, or overheard and then written down is not given, and the spindle will not turn for it. It is the only protection a poor person in Nether Combe has, and it is absolute.
3. **A lent name is not gone, it is held,** and the holder is responsible for it in the meantime. Everything done in the town under that name that day is the holder's act, and the holder is accountable for it at the roll.
4. **A call must be in the form "I call &lt;name&gt;".** Any other form — a description, a nickname, a taunt, a description of where somebody lives — is not a call, and nothing whatever happens.
5. **A called name must answer or be struck off.** Silence does not free the name. Silence transfers the whole outstanding balance to the debtor's household, and a household with the roll's balance on it cannot sell, marry, or be buried in the churchyard.
6. **The third night is always the last permitted night.** There is no fourth. A fourth night offered to a borrower is a fraud, and a fraud is proved on the roll, and it is the only kind of proof this town has ever accepted.
7. **Full payment releases the name at the moment of payment,** and the lender must hand it back in a sealed envelope within the hour. A lender who keeps it is a thief, and the mill may take it, and the roll records the theft.
8. **Spun gold is the lender's gold while the debt stands.** The borrower may not spend it, may not melt it, may not put it anywhere; it is weighed at the mill, entered, and it is the collateral, and everybody knows it and nobody says so at breakfast.
9. **A name may be lent for a lesser purpose by somebody other than its owner,** and it will hold for a lesser time — an afternoon, a year, a lifetime — as agreed, and the agreement is what limits it. A name lent beyond the agreement does not hold at all and the lender is left with nothing.
10. **A lender who is unknown cannot be called.** Being found out is therefore the only thing a lender has to fear. The whole of Nether Combe's social machinery — the whole mill, the whole valley, the whole Sunday — exists to find out who the spinner is, and has failed for two hundred years.

## Cast

**Aldis Prowse**, 19, a miller's daughter who can read the roll and has read the 1971 page more times than she will admit, and who is the only person in the valley who has seen the spinner's face and survived the sighting. *Want:* to find out whether the name on page four hundred and six is her mother's. *Flaw:* she has begun lending small things — favours, attention, a place in a queue — against names she is not certain she owns, and is one entry short of a real lending. *Detail:* a burn mark on her thumb from the mill's ledger iron, and a habit of turning a page with two fingers, flat, as if it were hot. *Speech habit:* narrates the roll as she reads it, out loud, in order, and cannot stop mid-sentence.

**Corve Merrick**, 52, the mill's master, forty-one years in the counting house, a careful, unimaginative man whose whole craft is the weekly pay. *Want:* to be told the mill is solvent by somebody who can prove it. *Flaw:* he lent his own wife's name in 1998 to settle a bad quarter and has been paying the interest in silence ever since, and does not know that the interest is compounding. *Detail:* a green baize sleeve-half worn on the right arm only, and a habit of touching the mill's iron tally-board before he answers anything. *Speech habit:* states the week before the answer — "that was the week before Michaelmas" — and will not be drawn forward in time.

**Innett Sly**, 23, a spinster who has worked three years in the tow shed and can spin a hank finer than anybody under fifty, and who is therefore the person the valley most suspects. *Want:* to be left alone in the tow shed. *Flaw:* she is the mill's best hand and the mill's pride and the mill's evidence, and she has learned to be unavailable in exactly the way that makes people talk. *Detail:* a permanently mid-length cut of hair she keeps shorter than her sisters', and a habit of counting her own revolutions aloud. *Speech habit:* talks in quantities, always, and will not say a thing without saying how much of it there is.

**Jory Vasc**, 15, an apprentice, clever, frightened, and the first person in the valley to understand that a name lent is a name held, and the first to try to exploit that. *Want:* to be named as the mill's own man on the pay roll before he is sixteen. *Flaw:* he has put his own name down as a loan against a debt he does not think exists, and there is now an entry with his name in it and no lender. *Detail:* a very clean notebook of figures nobody asked him to keep, and a habit of writing on his own left hand. *Speech habit:* answers questions with a second question, at length, and is delighted when it works.

**Nanny Rowe**, 79, who was in service at the Combe when the 1971 night happened, and who was the only person in the house. *Want:* to be asked one question. *Flaw:* she has answered that question eleven times, to herself, in the dark, and got a different answer each time, and has decided the difficulty was hers. *Detail:* a black wool shawl indoors and out, and a habit of turning the lamp down before she starts talking. *Speech habit:* begins in the present tense and finishes in 1971, and is unaware of the join.

**Pell Harrow**, 34, the Bristol merchant's factor, polite, informed, and in possession of an 1841 list of every name the mill has ever lent, which he bought with a bolt of cloth and has never been able to use. *Want:* to prove the roll is incomplete, which would let him call in the whole valley at once. *Flaw:* he has decided this is the most powerful thing he owns and has never spent it, and is now too frightened to. *Detail:* a leather document case that is not dust, and a habit of touching it when a subject gets away from him. *Speech habit:* says "strictly" and "for my part" and means neither.

## Places

**The Counting House.** Over the mill office: low, ledger-walled, one window facing the yard, the weekly pay board, the iron tally, and the merchant's letters. The room where everything real in Nether Combe is decided, and where the only fire is the stove, because the mill's other fire is unheated for a hundred and forty years.

**The Spinner's Room.** At the top of the mill under the roof. One door, no opening window, a stool, a wheel with no drive, and the roll in a tin. It is four steps colder than the loft below it and everyone in the valley knows the number of steps.

**The Tow Shed.** A long low lean-to on the river where the flax is broken and the fibre pulled. Dark, dusty, hot with human work, forty women and girls, and the only place in the mill where conversation is not a form of accounting.

**The Sunday.** Not a building: the one day in the week the mill is silent and the entire valley walks the same road from the mill gate to the church and back. The social mechanism by which everybody sees everybody, once a week, for six hours, in order, and the reason the spinner's name is still unknown.

**The Wheel.** The mill wheel standing in the brown water, stopped in summer, four feet of elm, and the most-photographed object in the valley, which is why the spinner will not work near it.

**The Great Bindery.** Downriver, where the valley's linen is finished: an open shed, black with flax dust, forty presses, and the binder who has handled every name on the roll in one form or another for thirty years and cannot read half of them.

## Visual Style

A palette of flax cream, river brown, elm grey and one cold green, lit by low winter sun through mill dust and by a single hard oil lamp in the counting house. The light is always raking and always interrupted: loom beams, stair rails, sacks, and people's backs. Materials: braked flax, tow dust, tarred twine, oiled elm, a tin ledger, a black wool shawl, brown water, iron stamps, and the specific blue-white of a window in a shed. The era reads 1880s-1910s, water-powered, pre-electric: no motor, a mill wheel, horse carts, a bicycle, gas in one room. The recurring motif is the roll — a bound book opened flat, five hands, a column of dates, and a name in the third column that nobody will read aloud.

## Thematic Spine

All nine stories are about what a person becomes the moment their name stops being theirs, and about a town that has organised itself around a question it is not permitted to ask. The accessible band treats it as a bargain: three nights, gold that cannot be spent, a night that ends. The intermediate band treats it as an instrument: a form of words, an envelope, an afternoon. The advanced band treats it as a record: a page, a roll, and four months of looking.
