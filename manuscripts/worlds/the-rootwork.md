# World: The Rootwork

The Rootwork is a hundred and eleven acres of fruiting orchard planted directly over a buried city, in a river bend where the soil is dark to the depth and never quite dries. Daily life smells of wet bark and windfall apples going to cider-sour on the grass; you hear pruning-saw, the creak of the ladder, the pipe-gallery that sings faintly when the water table is high. The trees are tended the way churches are tended — pruned on schedule, railed against frost, mourned one by one with a written entry. What is wrong is that nothing here grows where it was planted: the roots are down in the ruins, drawing from streets and cellars and a flooded nave, so that every fruit is a location and every fruit can be read. Two families hold rival surveys of this ground, and both surveys are correct, because each has been corrected by the other for a century without either admitting it. And somebody has begun selling a graft that reaches somewhere neither family has ever surveyed.

## Setting

The Rootwork occupies a silted oxbow in a temperate river valley, forty miles from the sea, in a country that never wrote itself down. Land is flat, dark loam over gravel over brick, and the water sits forty feet down where you can hear it but never see it. Three plantings stand in the same ground: the Ward Orchard on the high west side, the Fenrows on the low east, and the old unlisted middles — trees that grew from seed spat or dropped and nobody knows whose. Winters are wet and mild; the blossoms come in a cold rain and the fruit colours in a long grey autumn, the last pick often done by lantern in fog.

Buildings are practical and low: a packing shed with a slate roof, stone cellars cut into the bank for cool storage, a survey house with a plan chest, a schoolroom, a chapel with a bell that is rung by the hour and not for services. There are no fences between the two families' blocks, only a drainage ditch and forty years of mutual grumbling. Transport is a handcart, a flatbed truck hired for pick day, and, on the rare occasion, a surveyor's theodolite carried up from the town by two people who will not speak.

Food is cider, cheese, pork in winter, a great deal of beans, and fruit eaten in states: windfalls under the tree, then pie, then a cellar barrel of pressed juice that is drunk cold and tastes of wood and cellar. Market day is Thursday at the packing shed: scales, a chalk board, the two families trading baskets of apples across the same table without ever agreeing on the price of the same variety, and the register's clerk under an awning with a bound volume on a crate, taking grafts and giving docket numbers.

## The Magic

1. **Nothing grows where it is planted.** A seed in this ground sends its taproot down past the soil line into whatever lies below — street, cellar, cistern, nave, the sealed floor of a warehouse — and the fruit that eventually appears is the shape of that thing's influence. The tree is a map. The fruit is a place.
2. **A fruit names what it is grown from.** Anyone holding a fruit of a Rootwork tree can, by stem, leaf, and taste, tell what it was fed on. This is true for everyone. It cannot be faked by shape alone; the taste carries the address.
3. **Grafting is surveying.** A graft joins the living map of one tree to another. Once a union takes, the two root systems are continuous and the grafted tree reads both locations at once — and, for as long as the union holds, so does every other tree sharing a rootstock with either parent.
4. **The register records parentage, not findings.** Every union is entered in the Grafting Register, a bound volume that follows a tree across generations. The register proves where a fruit came from. It has no column for what was found, because the register is a document of descent, not of content. This is the world's central blind spot and must appear in every story.
5. **The cost is the parent's memory of its own site.** A grafted tree, over its years, grows weaker at describing its own ground and stronger at describing the grafted ground. Graft enough and a tree stops yielding its own variety entirely: it becomes a pure function of where it was grafted to. Orchardists call this a *drifting* tree, and it is the permanent cost of every improvement anyone makes here.
6. **The limit is the register's custody.** Magic answers to a documented chain. A graft recorded in the register is stable for the life of the tree. A graft made, used, and unrecorded is a stranger's graft: it holds for a growing number of years and then comes apart in a single night, all of its held information going into the soil of that night, permanently. Nobody knows this, and everybody knows it, which is why nobody can explain why the unrecorded grafts in the district are so old.
7. **Breaking a rule takes a place out of the record.** Sever an unrecorded graft, or forge a parentage in the register, or graft across two families' stocks, and the ground loses that address permanently — not destroyed, but unreadable, a hole in the map that later trees will grow around. The result is a *blank*: a hollow in the orchard where the fruit is thin and sour and describes nothing.
8. **Selling a fruit is a way of telling a secret.** Because a fruit carries its site in its taste, a fruit given away is a location made public, and a fruit sold is a location published. The buyer knows where it came from. A gift is a courtesy; a sale is a disclosure, and the register's docket number is the paper trail of a disclosure.
9. **Neither survey can be wrong.** The Ward Survey and the Fenrow Survey disagree because they were begun from different ends of the site and corrected by each other for over a century. Rule: the two surveys are simultaneously correct, always; the conflict is a property of the ground, not an error. No story resolves it by one side simply being right.
10. **The buried city is not dead; it is unlisted.** Water moves in the ruins according to the water it always moved, and a graft deep enough will draw from a place that was on no plan because it was written down by nobody. Finding such a place is a *cold union*, and the register's silence about it is not oversight. Cold unions are the subject of all nine stories.

## Cast

**Marren Ashlock**, 41, head of the Ward Survey: the last person who will walk a whole ground row by row with a tape and a notebook before dark.
- **Want:** to finish the Ward Survey's hundredth year complete, and retire as the man who finished it.
- **Flaw:** he has never once in his life conceded a point in the field, including the six times he was wrong.
- **Physical detail:** a leather-cord notebook lashed to his left wrist with a brass buckle he has had punched twice, and a plumb bob he wears hung in his chest pocket like a bad tooth.
- **Verbal habit:** says "Noted" when he is about to lie, and "Noted, corrected" when he has just been shown he is wrong.

**Ottiline Vrey**, 38, keeper of the Grafting Register, registrar for the whole district.
- **Want:** to get the register surveyed, bound properly, and entered in the district record, so it survives her.
- **Flaw:** she believes a record is worth more than a person and is only ever half joking about it.
- **Physical detail:** a grey archive glove on her right hand only, always on, even in the orchard, and a warding pin of bone-white boxwood in her left ear.
- **Verbal habit:** answers questions with the number first — "Eleven, and who asked you to count?"

**Hollis Bray**, 14, pruner and ladder-holder, Millward's youngest.
- **Want:** to be told his tree-mapping is real work and not just walking with a stick.
- **Flaw:** he talks continuously, and cannot be made to stop for a question.
- **Physical detail:** a yellow-handled pruning saw worn in a leather belt-loop at the hip, and both palms permanently barked at the base of the fingers.
- **Verbal habit:** narrates everything out loud as he does it, so that when he is frightened the story he is telling simply speeds up.

**Sennet Bray**, 52, foreman of the Fenrow block and Hollis's mother, widow of the mapmaker Millward Bray.
- **Want:** to keep the Fenrow trees and her son on this land for the length of her back, and to never again sign anything.
- **Flaw:** she teaches Hollis that a tree answers to whoever holds the saw, which is true and which she half knows is a lie.
- **Physical detail:** a mitten with no thumb, permanently on her left hand, and a mouthful of iron keys on a bootlace she wears at her throat.
- **Verbal habit:** gives instructions as weather facts — "You will find it wet under the north row. It is always wet under the north row."

**Dellory Fenn**, 63, retired district analyst, last handwriting hand on the register before Ottiline.
- **Want:** to be asked to prove the thing he worked out, and not to be asked.
- **Flaw:** he has never submitted a finding, on the grounds that the finding might be wrong and the register cannot be wrong.
- **Physical detail:** a tin of drawing pins and a folding rule he never separates, and one lens of his spectacles wired to his ear with copper thread.
- **Verbal habit:** says "on the record?" as a whole sentence, and treats the answer as permission.

**Mirren Ostley**, 34, itinerant fruit-buyer and seller of the cold union, who came to the Rootwork two seasons ago and stayed.
- **Want:** to buy the unrepresented ground cheaply, through people who cannot read a docket, and put it somewhere it will be believed.
- **Flaw:** she is a better liar than she is a forger, so the graft she sells is genuine and the paperwork is the fraud.
- **Physical detail:** a canvas trade coat with the pocket mended inside-out and in a different blue, and a paper docket-book she never lets out of her left hand.
- **Verbal habit:** speaks to fruit sellers entirely in second person — "You and me, we both know what this tree is worth."

## Places

**The Packing Shed** — a long low slate-roofed shed at the top of the Ward block, doors open to the yard all Thursday, scales and a chalk price board and the smell of bruised fruit and dry shavings. Under the boards, and this is known to three people, the floor runs over a flagged cellar corridor that the surveyors have never mapped because there is nothing on the plans above them.

**The Register Room** — a low whitewashed room in the packing shed's east end, holding eleven tall ledgers on stands, chained by their spines but not their clasps. It smells of glue, foxing, and the cold mineral tang of a cellar, and it is the only room in the Rootwork where people lower their voices.

**The Fenrows** — the low east half of the orchard, planted in long straight rows on ground that was water meadow before it was orchard, so the roots run sideways and the trees lean. Wet grass in the wheel ruts most of the year, and in fog the far rows simply stop existing.

**Bellhouse Cellar** — a stone cellar cut into the bank behind the schoolroom, holding three hundred crates and a barrel of pressed juice that everyone in the district agrees tastes wrong, sour and faintly iron, and which is drunk anyway. Nobody has ever found a way of pressing the fruit in it twice.

**The Cold Graft** — a walled corner plot fenced in old orchard boards, containing one tree, a scion of unknown stock worked onto a root nobody can account for, kept in leaf but bearing nothing. Sellers bring fruit to its gate and it is never the seller who leaves disappointed.

**The Middle Orchard** — a dozen unlisted trees between the two families' blocks, of unknown age and nobody's parentage, growing out of a strip of ground where no seedling was ever put. Fence posts inside it are rotted through at the base, in a ring, at a regular spacing.

## Visual Style

A wet late-season English orchard photographed as a painting, not a fantasy: light is flat, grey and generous, coming through mist rather than beams, with colour carried by a small number of accents — bruised purple of windfalls, moss green of the north sides, and the red-brown wet of turned soil. Materials are bark, iron, sacking, tarpaulin, wet slate, and the foxed rag paper of ledgers; the smallest readable object in any image is a paper docket or a notebook page. The era feel is a working agricultural one, roughly 1890s machinery with no visible magic apparatus, so the strangeness reads as husbandry: grafting wax, label tags, a ladder laid flat in grass. The recurring motif is a fruit held open in two hands, always cut, always showing the wrong number of carpels for its variety.

## Motifs

- **A cut fruit held in two hands**, showing a wrong section of seed.
- **The docket book and the docket tag**, tiny paper, wrong handwriting, wrong parentage.
- **Iron keys on a bootlace**, carried by whoever has authority they did not ask for.
- **A ladder laid flat in wet grass**, with boots on the far side of it.
- **The plumb bob on its leather cord**, hung and swinging, waiting for the ground to settle.
- **A grafting knife with one blade edge deliberately blunted**, the tool of a hand that cannot cut cleanly.
- **Sacking sacks of windfalls** with the picker's private chalk tally, a map nobody else can read.

## Thematic Spine

All nine stories are quietly about the difference between what a record is for and what it happens to hold: the register is a document of descent that is constantly mistaken for a document of truth, and the work is learning to write down what you found without pretending the column exists for it. The three levels take three different moral angles — the accessible stories say that being noticed honestly is itself a contribution; the intermediate stories say that institutions are repairable by patient, unheroic, unsatisfying labour done before a deadline; the advanced stories say that the honest thing and the useful thing diverge, and you still have to file the honest one.
