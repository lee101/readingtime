# The War of the First Skies

![Cover art: The War of the First Skies](../art/advanced/24-the-war-of-the-first-skies.webp)

At sixteen, Tovan had three duties: braid the sun-hawk crest into his collar, check the copper vane, and never stare at an ice-sailer as if the sight were new.

He completed the first two before the eastern beacon turned white. The third he failed the moment Sera crossed the launch deck in an ice-sailer’s blue coat, salt-stiff gloves, and a red sash marking a licensed skyfarer.

The nations of Kesta and Iri had spent forty years calling the Whale Passage neutral while building cannon on either side of it. Every spring, sun-whales crossed between the warm sky seas above Kesta and the cold breeding grounds above Iri. Both fleets guarded the migration, both crews collected drift-salt for their cities, and both quarrels had grown sharp enough to cut.

Today the whale keeper had ordered every ship to anchor below the clouds.

Tovan touched the duty tablet at his belt. Its upper line read: DEFEND THE PASSAGE.

The beacon flashed three short pulses. Warning.

Squadron leader, ready your twelve.

Tovan was the youngest of the twelve. The others already had their braided crests polished. He tightened the blue safety sash at his waist, checked the release knot, and turned for the sixth hawk.

Sera caught his sleeve. “The long cloud,” she said. “Do you see it?”

Above the north horizon, a bar of silver cloud leaned into the sky. It was too straight, too sharp at both ends.

“Saltglass,” Tovan said.

“Wider than yesterday.”

Before he could reply, the beacon flashed again. A dull crack came from overhead. The fracture inside the long cloud opened a finger’s width, and every ship’s rigging began to sing toward it.

Sun-whales answered from far below.

Their calls rose through the hulls as low, branching pulses: bright voices from Kesta, deep voices from Iri, all calling their calves together. Tovan felt each call in his teeth. The whales were beneath the cloud sea, far below their fleet, climbing toward the silver cloud because it was the only opening wide enough for their migration.

One pulse came wrong. Short, rapid, repeating north.

Sera pulled out a listening slate. “False-storm signal. The calf has latched onto the fracture and is broadcasting wind warning across the whole passage.”

“Then the other whales will turn toward it.”

“If they do, they may strike the opening edge.”

Tovan watched a flock of copper kites break formation overhead. Their tiny sails snapped as the false signal pushed new wind across the deck.

“I can take hawk-six over the calf,” he said. “Pull it south while my squadron holds the eastern edge.”

“You cannot. Captain Dain has grounded the squadron. The fracture may split the cloud wall.”

The signal repeated. Tovan’s compass needle spun north. A dark seam appeared in the cloud ahead, broad as a ship, and the sun-whale calls changed from greetings to warnings.

Irsa Dain seized his shoulder. “Station, hawk-six.”

“That calf will bring the herd into the wall.”

“Then we hold the wall and pray the sky is merciful.”

“Mercy is not a tactic.”

Irsa’s eyes narrowed, but she released him. “All right. Take hawk-six. You are not to cross the black marker. You are not to chase the calf beyond the eastern buoy. You are to place a false signal where my squadron can copy it.”
Irsa pointed to an empty hoist line. “Use it as an ice-sailer distress relay.” Sera removed her red sash, clipped it to her coat, and hooked her skiff to the line. Then she launched below the deck.

Tovan followed on hawk-six. The moment he left the rail, wind tried to tear his crest loose. He leaned forward until the hawk’s beak pointed into the current. Below, the sun-whales moved like rivers of molten gold through cold cloud. Their young traveled beneath their elders, protected by broad fins and calls that pushed the water-sky ahead of them.

The fracture waited above them, a slash in the sky leaking white light.

At the eastern marker, Tovan released a ribbon of heat from the hawk’s sun-vane. It rose like a gold streamer, copied Sera’s relay, and curved south. To the herd, it showed a false copy of the caught calf leading them away from the dangerous wall.

The nearest elder turned south.

For one breath, Tovan thought the plan had worked. Then the fracture answered the false signal with a pulse of its own. Every particle in the false cloud gathered inward, making a whirlpool at the sky’s edge. The whale calves were pulled toward it.

“Signal canceled,” Tovan called. “Formation, wide fan. Do not cross the marker.”

Tovan banked toward the black marker and found Sera there.

Her skiff had gone still beside the relay line. One hook had frozen open because a piece of saltglass had caught around it. Below her, three calves circled the calf broadcasting the false warning, which was too young to climb above them.

Squadron Leader Dain curved the squadron into a crescent around the marker, drawing a bright bowl in the cloud. Sera cut away her lift cloth, dropped below Tovan, and caught the calf’s sweeping fin.

“Five seconds,” she shouted.

The fracture’s pull dragged against the hawk. His duty tablet sprang loose, and he caught it against the deck. The calf then pushed Sera’s skiff toward the silver opening. Tovan put his hawk overhead, using the sun-vane’s heat to tighten her ice sail into a barrier.

“Signal now!” Tovan called.

“Iri relay to Kesta relay: the calf broadcasting the false signal is caught on a pressure tongue. She is not leading; the tongue is pulling. We will make a false herd at the western edge, then let the true herd pass south. Confirm by lowering the outer vane.”

Irsa, far below on Sun-Seven, lowered her outer vane.

Sera pulled twice on the relay. The new message raced through saltglass toward every hooked line in the squadron. One by one, the sun-hawks banked west and spread their heat. A wall of gold appeared above the fracture’s western tip.

The pressure tongue struck the gold wall. White fragments flew across the hawks, but the wall held. Below, the calf dropped toward its herd, and Sera’s skiff swung free.

“Mother ship, request a rescue under the wall,” Tovan called.

Irsa answered through the relay. “Your hawk cannot cover itself while you lower a net.”

“Then let the hull crew cover us.”

“You are ordered to withdraw.”

The order carried Dain’s private mark. Tovan’s duty was the fleet. His life was the fleet. Every command he had known since boyhood had said the same thing.

Sera showed him a woven rescue net clipped to her belt. “Hold the calf for three breaths,” she called, “and the eldest whale can break its signal.”

Tovan turned hawk-six downward.

The dive began in screaming wind. Tovan folded the hawk’s wings, dropped like a burning stone, then opened them at the lowest safe mark.

He released the rescue net.

The calf felt the line tighten. It twisted, taking Sera’s skiff down with it. Tovan pulled hard on the control grip. The hawk banked instead of climbing. The net cords went taut across the calves’ path.

“Tovan, leave the line!” Dain called. “You are taking the hawk into the wall.”

The eldest whale turned above him, gold hide crossed by cloud-shadow. The calf clung beneath its belly, still broadcasting warnings.

The elder’s call was so low that Tovan felt it in his chest. The calf stopped. For three breaths, the sky went quiet.

Then it answered with the long, bright phrase young whales used when they had found their herd. Every elder turned south, and the calves streamed out from under the fracture.

Tovan released the net.

Sera’s skiff shot upward. Tovan caught the skiff’s bow line with a rescue hook and swung beneath it. Ice cracked along her sail. The blue skiff and sun-hawk hung side by side while the herd passed below.

A blast from the wall rolled across them.

The sky-hawk’s left wing folded. Tovan cut its lift cloth. The wing dropped. The hawk began to spin.

Sera cut away her own sail. With both hands on the steering bar, she brought her skiff beneath the hawk’s keel.

“Tie your line to mine,” she called.

“The ice will cut it.”

“Then face the wind.”

Tovan clipped his line to hers. Sera brought the skiff around until its straight keel met the hawk’s center. The spinning stopped. Tovan released the damaged wing, and the two craft fell together, side by side.

A gold fleet swept from below. Sun-hawks built a ramp on their heat sails while Iri ice-claws climbed beneath it with freezing veils that calmed the wind. Both fleets locked their vessels into a moving sling.

Tovan and Sera guided their damaged craft into it.

Dain’s voice reached them over the relay. “Iri rescue, report.”

Sera touched the blue line, then the gold line. “Two craft. One duty. Both accounted for.”

Below the fleet, the migrating whales curved around the south end of the fracture. Their young moved in the center. The rescued calf stayed near its mother. As the last whale passed, the pressure tongue thinned. The fracture’s white edge drew together until it became a narrow seam instead of an open wound.

The sea-sky repaired itself.

The war did not.

A hard bell rang from both flagships. One ring for breach of passage. Two for loss of a ship. Three for weapons drawn in the migration. Dain had signaled three.

Sun-hawks formed on the eastern sky. Iri ice-claws formed on the west. Their crews stared across an empty strip where the wall had stood. Several Iri gun crews had fired salt charges into the pressure tongue. Kesta’s outer batteries had answered with sunfire. Both nations could claim that the other had nearly widened the fracture.

When the fleet reached the neutral council buoy, the two ship masters came aboard under a white truce flag. Dain wore her gold sash. Ice Master Ona wore a blue one cut with silver.

Ona pointed at the cracked tablet. “Your pilot disobeyed.”

“He followed an Iri distress relay,” Dain said. “The distress belonged to every crew present.”

Tovan stood before them and set the tablet on the table. “I left formation and lowered a rescue net. If the fracture had widened, my squadron would have burned to keep your whales from striking the wall.”

“Confession does not change the order,” Ona said.

“The order preserved command,” Dain replied. “It saved no one.”

Sera stepped forward, her lost glove replaced by blue cloth stained with machine oil. “Iri crews caught the eastern calves when your wall turned west. Your freezing veils held Tovan’s damaged craft. Without your line, he could not have lowered that net.”

“Do you blame Kesta for the fracture?” Ona asked.

“No. I know where I was. I do not know what orders crossed the gap before us.”

Beyond the table, captured sunfire shells and Iri salt charges floated in separate evidence crates. Each nation had brought proofs of the other’s violation, but none showed the true storm.

The whale keeper came aboard in a plain flight coat, carrying a black horn and one shed golden tooth. “This was cast loose when the pressure tongue formed. Whale teeth keep the pattern of skies that made them.”

She turned the tooth. One old scar pointed toward Kesta. Another, newer and narrow, pointed toward Iri.

“Cloud pulls on whatever strikes it,” she said. “We do not know who struck first. But the second answer made the storm.”

Dain removed her captain’s sash and laid it beside the tooth. “Until the pattern is known, the Sun-hawk fleet will hold every breach charge.”

Ona did the same with her blue sash. “Iri crews will do the same.”

The whale keeper looked from one nation to the other. “And the passage?”

Dain lifted Tovan’s damaged tablet. “Open. Both fleets escort the herd. No banner leads. No captain gives an order across the other’s line.”

“If the fracture opens again?” Sera asked.

“Tovan’s method,” Dain said.

Dain and Ona placed their hands on the tooth. Sera stepped forward and added her wrapped hand. Tovan added his.

A call sounded below the council buoy. Low, branching, and bright. The herd had returned. The rescued calf answered with its long phrase, clear enough now to be understood by both fleets.

Tovan flew formation beside Sera that evening. Her skiff had been rebuilt around a cracked mast, and his hawk carried a borrowed sun-hawk net too large for its belly.

Neither vessel was made whole. Both were still needed.
