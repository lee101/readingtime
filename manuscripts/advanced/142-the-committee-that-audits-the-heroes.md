# The Committee That Audits the Heroes

![Cover art: The Committee That Audits the Heroes](../art/advanced/142-the-committee-that-audits-the-heroes.webp)

Halversgate has one committee that pays people for helping, and it is called the Able Service Committee, and it has nine members and a minute book and a blue folder for the accounts.

It pays nine pounds forty an hour to anybody who puts a name down and is fit and willing, and it pays on the fourth of the month, and it asks back for every hour it cannot show.

Fenn Lacey has been on the roll since the year the committee started. He is fifty-four, with grey hair cropped close to his head and a grey moustache, and he wears a green quilted jacket in every month of the year. He can do any job eight times quicker than a person should manage, and for every hour he saves he loses one day of his own past, and the days leave in the order they happened, oldest first, so he cannot tell what he has gone until somebody stands in front of him and tells him.

The committee meets in the upstairs room over the library on a Monday evening, at seven, and the room has a long table taken out of the old school and nine chairs that do not match and a barometer that has said CHANGE since the year it was put up.

The chair is Ines Verrall. She is forty-nine, with grey hair cut short at the front and longer at the back, and she wears a dark green coat over a grey jumper, and she carries a red folder under one arm and has carried a folder of some kind since she was thirty-one.

She is not a councillor and does not want to be. She took the chair in a bad February because nobody else would and because somebody had to do the sums, and she does the sums well.

There is a subcommittee on the cape. There has been one since the year the committee was made, it has met twice, and its only recorded decision is that the cape is to stay in the coat room case for the hanging of wet coats.

* * *

The form is one line per job: date, place, what was done, hours required, hours claimed.

For an ordinary volunteer the last two columns are the same number. In the small print on the back, which is the part that matters, it says that hours claimed must be shown to the satisfaction of the committee.

Fenn Lacey's claim for the year went in on the ninth of February. It was four hundred and twelve hours and one thousand and six jobs, and nobody thought anything of it, because four hundred and twelve hours from a man with no other work is a good half of what they had budgeted for.

* * *

Wenna Pyle is the clerk. She is twenty-three, with red hair braided over one shoulder, and she wears a navy anorak, and she has been the clerk for two years on a third of what the treasurer is paid.

She typed the claim onto the schedule and did the sum and put it in the folder, and in April a letter came back from the county with a query written across the top of it, because the money is paid through a county system and the county system has a habit of asking.

The query covered three entries and no others, which meant somebody had sat down with a schedule of a thousand and six jobs and picked three, and had picked them for a reason.

The eleventh of August, an attic at number fourteen Ferrier Row, four hours required, twenty-five minutes claimed.

The second of February, a shortwave set at a house in Tarnwick, which is a fishing village sixteen miles off and not in the district, four hours required, thirty minutes claimed.

The nineteenth of January, a field gate at Cove Bottom, two hours required, twelve minutes claimed.

* * *

Fenn Lacey came up to the library on the Wednesday and sat in the chair at the end of the table that nobody sits in, and he was not worried, and that was the thing about him the committee never got used to.

Eight times quicker is not a thing you can be caught at. He did not hurry and he did not show anybody and he was not in the habit of claiming more than he had done.

“The query is not saying you did not do them,” said Ines Verrall. “It is saying the sheet cannot carry them.”

“There was an attic,” said Fenn Lacey. “Number fourteen. There was a woman in it with a lamp and no way down that she could tell me about, and I went up and I cleared it.”

“How long did that take you.”

“Twenty-five minutes is what I put down.”

Ollin Bray is the treasurer. He is sixty-eight, with white whiskers and spectacles on a cord, and he has kept the accounts for eleven years in a hard black pencil. He put the pencil down and said, “The schedule says a clear-out is four hours.”

“It was four hours for a person,” said Fenn Lacey. “I did not want to be paid for the four.”

* * *

If he had claimed four hours the sheet would have balanced and the county would never have looked. He had claimed what he had actually spent, and there was no rule in the small print against a volunteer being quick, and a rule made to catch him now would only be that, and Ines Verrall would not put her name to it either.

What they were sitting round the table on Monday was not a suspicion. It was a column that would not carry the weight put on it.

* * *

In May, Wenna Pyle typed the notices for the roundup column of the Halversgate Advertiser, and the roundup is made out of whatever comes in on the backs of letters, three lines to a thing, and the letters come from everywhere because nobody in a small town has anywhere else to put a complaint.

Three of that week's items were from other towns.

Ninebridge: the bell from the old workhouse has been taken down and is to go into a pub garden, and a man who offered to polish it free of charge has been told the council will consider it.

Brinlow: Bram Vik, 10, mends the shortwave set behind the net shed, and has had it running on a car battery since the October gale took the aerial off, and has done all winter what he calls the boring part of it.

Falln: Iris, 11, reads the rain gauge above the mill every morning at seven and writes the number on the tally board in the school porch, and was in the porch at seven on Tuesday with the pencil, as she is on every school morning.

She typed all three because that is the column's whole job, and then she went down the screen again with her tea, and she read the Brinlow one three times.

* * *

A shortwave set at a house in Brinlow, on the second of February. Two hundred words away from it, in the same column, a boy of ten in the same village saying that he mends the set.

Nobody in Halversgate thought anything of that at the time. It was three lines about a boy, there were nine items in the column, and nobody reads a column of nine items.

Wenna Pyle telephoned Brinlow on the Thursday at four, on her own time, in eleven minutes, with a number written on a piece of paper.

* * *

Bram Vik is ten years old and small for it, with black hair cut square at the back, and he wears a yellow jersey with the sleeves turned back and he is not at all pleased to be telephoned.

She got his mother, and then she got him, and then she asked him the narrowest question she could think of, which was whether a man in a green jacket had come to his house on the second of February.

“He did,” said Bram Vik. “He came on the bike thing. He had the set on the bench with the lid off, and he said the valve had to be warm, and he warmed the tube with his hands for about a minute and a half, and then he said the lid could go back on.”

“Did he tell you how long he had?”

“He said he had to go.” Bram Vik said it flatly, the way ten says anything. “He said he had to go and remember something.”

* * *

Wenna Pyle wrote it down as near to his words as she could and then stopped, because four hundred words of column in her own head was not publishable and it was the only evidence anybody had.

She brought it to Ines Verrall before she went home and put it on the table face down.

“I telephoned a boy in Tarnwick about a job on our schedule. I have not written it up. I think it has to go in the hearing.”

“He said he had to go and remember something.”

“He did say it.”

“That is not evidence of anything,” said Ines Verrall. “If you put it in front of them with no rule to attach it to they will throw it out, and they will be right to.”

* * *

The hearing took two hours and eleven minutes on the eleventh of June, and the minute book has it in the flat hand the clerk uses for anything that is going to be read twice.

Nobody came to speak for the committee except Ines Verrall and Ollin Bray. Fenn Lacey sat in the chair at the end of the table that nobody sits in and answered nine questions in a calm voice.

The county observer was Adeyemi Rooke from the payments office, and he had come with a laptop and a great deal of good faith, and the good faith went out of him by half past eight.

The question was not whether Fenn Lacey had done the three jobs. Everybody in the room believed he had. The question was what a payment for them was built on.

* * *

“One line on the schedule is supported by the word of the person it was done for,” said Adeyemi Rooke, “or it is not supported. That is the whole of the audit rule and I did not write it.”

“Then the attic stands or falls on the woman at number fourteen,” said Ines Verrall.

“Does it?”

“Ines,” said Ollin Bray, “you know it does not. I have driven past number fourteen. It has been empty since the spring.”

“Then the attic is unverified,” said Ines Verrall, “and that is the finding, and it goes in the minute in those words, and it goes in the Advertiser in those words, because if we soften it in the Advertiser we are doing the thing this committee exists not to do.”

* * *

Fenn Lacey spoke once more, and what he said took eleven seconds and the clerk wrote all of it.

“If the attic is unverified then I have not been paid for it and I am not going to claim it.”

“Nobody said you had to claim it.”

“I know,” said Fenn Lacey. “I said it.”

And that was the whole of his contribution, and the committee voted three to two with two abstentions to allow the claim less the attic and to refer the Tarnwick item and the gate to November, and the minutes went out on the Friday to nine households and to the library.

* * *

Afterwards Ines Verrall sat at her own kitchen table and read the schedule from the bottom up, one thousand and six lines, two evenings of it, and stopped at the February line.

Then she went further down, to a roundup she had typed in May and filed, and read the Falln item again.

Falln: Harkin Yeale, 84, kept the rain tally board in the school porch for forty-one years, and taught the girl who keeps it now, and was in the porch at seven on Tuesday watching her do it.

She read it a great many times, and then she drove out to Falln on the Saturday and parked opposite the school.

* * *

Harkin Yeale is eighty-four years old, very thin, in a brown coat with the collar turned up, and he has white hair combed flat, and he is standing well back from the porch wall because the girl is at it.

The board is a sheet of roofing slate on the porch wall, ruled with a nail and a straightedge and re-chalked so many thousands of times that the slate is grey with it. It runs March to February down one side, the days of each month down the other, and beside each day there is a figure in millimetres and a mark where the figure is not to be believed.

It is the wettest year anybody in Falln can remember and it is the fourth driest since the board began.

“Seven o'clock,” said Harkin Yeale. “You will have come for the board.”

“I have.”

“Nobody comes for the board.”

* * *

She asked him what it was for, and he said it was for the next person who wanted to know what had fallen in March.

Forty-one years. One chalk line a day, in a porch, in public, with nothing owed to him, and every March the whole slate wiped and begun again. He had done it until his hands would not hold the chalk and then he had stood back from it and let an eleven-year-old take the pencil.

“It is not a record of me,” he said. “It is a record of the rain, and I am the hand it happened to be in.”

* * *

She wrote two pages over the Sunday and brought them on the Monday.

She said the committee could not verify a job by asking the volunteer, because the volunteer would say yes.
She said it could not verify a job by writing to the household, because the household had gone or was asleep or both.

She said it could not verify a job by requiring anybody to keep a book, because a man who has worked every day of his life without one is not going to be cured of it by a committee.

“Those are all the ways it does not work,” she said. “I have written them down so that we have them in front of us.”

* * *

“Then what do we do,” said Ollin Bray.

“We make the record belong to somebody else.”

She put her hand flat on the middle of the table, which she had never once done at that table.

“One line of the form. One name and one number from the person the work was done for, where there is a person. Not a book. One name on one line, the way somebody signs for a parcel. We keep those in the minute book and anybody can come and read them.”

“That is a great deal of telephoning.”

“It is,” said Ines Verrall. “Wenna will do most of it and she will be paid for it, and I will get the money from the subcommittee on the cape, which has fourteen pounds in it and no purpose.”

The subcommittee on the cape was abolished at the same meeting, in one line, and nobody voted against it.

* * *

Fenn Lacey began repeating the nine jobs on his own time in the last week of August.

That was the answer to the question nobody had asked out loud. The county wanted a second source, and the only way to get one was to do the job again.

He told Wenna Pyle on the Friday that he was not asking permission, he was telling them, and then he did the nine.

At four hours' worth of work done in half an hour, four hours saved, four days gone.

At two hours' worth of work done in twelve minutes, one and three quarter hours saved, one day and eighteen hours gone.

Nine jobs. Wenna Pyle did it on a sheet of paper in the end of August while he went on.

Twenty-nine hours and ten minutes of work saved. Twenty-nine days, four hours and ten minutes of his own life, taken from the front, oldest first.

* * *

What he no longer had, by the end of September, was the year before his daughter was born.

He did not find that out from a person. He found it out from a form. There was a job on the schedule in the May of the year his daughter was born, a boiler at a house in Weir Lane, one hour required, six minutes claimed, and when he read the line he put the page down and stood at the window.

His sister telephoned on the Tuesday and told him he had walked the length of Weir Lane singing, on his way home from that boiler, and that she had been behind the hedge for a quarter of an hour out of respect.

He believed her. He had believed her for eleven years. That was the arrangement he lived in, and nobody had ever asked him to describe it, and he had never tried.

* * *

The November hearing took forty minutes.

Bram Vik's letter came out of the file first, three lines in his mother's hand and three in his own: a man he had never seen before came on the second of February and warmed the valve with his hands for a minute and a half, and said he had to go and remember something, and the set had run every day since.

Adeyemi Rooke read it twice and said that a ten-year-old's recollection was not a document.

“No,” said Ines Verrall. “It is a person who was in a room with the work, saying what happened in the room, and it is the only kind of evidence this schedule has ever had for anything, including the four hundred and eight hours you signed off in June.”

“The shortwave item is verified,” said Adeyemi Rooke. “Fine. The gate?”

“The gate is verified as well. Cove Bottom is nine households and the man at the top wrote to us in the first week of October and asked why nobody had shut his field gate since January.”

* * *

The decision went in as nine lines and was posted on the library board on the Monday and printed in the Advertiser in four lines of the news column, unedited, with the numbers in.

The claim for the year was allowed in full at four hundred and twelve hours. The countersignature rule took effect from the first of January, every signature was to be kept in the minute book and not in the volunteer's house.

Two hours of the year remained unverified, and they were two hours in October at a lock-up on the coast road where there was no household and no person at all, and the minute says the committee proposes no action and pays the two hours anyway.

That was Ines Verrall's whole amendment, and she argued for it for nine minutes, and it was the only part of the evening she enjoyed.

* * *

On the fourth of December, at half past four, Fenn Lacey went to a house in Ferrier Row where a woman had a wardrobe across the landing and could not get it past the doorframe.

It took him eleven minutes and it should have taken an hour and a half. Thirty-five hours and twenty minutes, saved, of a day.

He went home along the front and stopped at the end of it, in the usual way, and looked for the thing that is always there afterwards and is never there.

The decision, the numbers, the three to two, the countersignature rule. All of it was where it had been.

He went home to an empty house and put his bag down and did not know what month it was.

The committee audited four hundred and twelve hours a year and never once saw a minute of the work, and every hour it could not account for was an hour somebody had given away for nothing. Halversgate put a form between itself and the work, and Falln put a slate on a porch wall, and the two arrangements have both run for forty years and neither has ever been audited. What sits on the library board now is nine lines saying that a boy in Brinlow watched a stranger warm a valve with his hands for a minute and a half, and that is the whole of what a committee of nine got, in a year, out of an impossible man.
