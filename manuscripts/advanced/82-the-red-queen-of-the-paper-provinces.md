# The Red Queen of the Paper Provinces

![Cover art: The Red Queen of the Paper Provinces](../art/advanced/82-the-red-queen-of-the-paper-provinces.webp)

The trust of Anhalt had occupied the second floor of a building in Wesel for two hundred years and had never in that time put a physical object anywhere that anybody had to fetch, which is why the first thing anyone noticed about Ilse Brandeis, on her first morning as ninth trustee, was the wax.

The estate’s only product was wax. It came out of the drawer in the shape of a woman’s head with a crown on it, and it went out of the drawer in a thin red disc with a queen in relief on it, and the disc went on to a document, and the document went into a file bound in red boards, and the file went into a press in the corridor, and the boards came back in the morning, and nothing had moved that a person could carry away except the queen.

“The province is not a place,” said the accountant, when she asked. He was thirty-four as well and had been in the office for nine years and had come from a firm in Rotterdam where the work was real. “It is a schedule. It is on the second page of the schedule and it says what it says, and everything else we do is arithmetic on top of that sentence.”

She had been a commercial lawyer for nine years and had read the trust deed over two nights in a hotel in Hanover, and had got to the second page of the schedule and stopped and read it again, and had then read it four more times, and had established that she could not find the boundary of the province anywhere in the document, and that the document was not the sort of document in which a boundary is accidentally left out.

Anhalt had been called the red province since the day of the seal, because the founder’s seal had been red and the founder had been particular about it, and the name had survived two changes of building and a war. The Queen was not a crowned head. She was a trustee with a seal, and the deed said that a trustee should be crowned once upon appointment and not twice, and the crowning took four minutes and was performed by a notary holding a piece of wax over a candle, and there was no crown anywhere in the building and never had been.

* * *

The schedule of the province had been drawn up in 1826 by a notary named Aldo Preyss and had never been amended, and it described Anhalt in words and not in acres, and the founder had given his reason on a separate leaf, in a memorandum that came with the deed and had been read, on the evidence of the circulation marks, eleven times in two hundred years.

A province is a thing that men say they live in, he wrote, and the moment a government puts a number on it the living of it stops. The trust is therefore to hold the peace of a name and not the rent of a field, so that the name may stay where it was.

The estate employed nine people, of whom four were clerks, and it had a pension scheme that was fully funded, because the trust’s liabilities were all of one kind and had been discharged on the day they fell due for two centuries. It had a bank account in Wesel whose balance moved between four thousand and eleven thousand marks according to the quarter. It had never had a surplus. It had never had a deficit. It had a firm of solicitors on the first floor and no Anhalt on any floor of it, and four senior clerks who spent their days certifying returns from provincial offices that had nothing to certify and producing, four times a year, a three-page instrument identical to the three pages before it except for the date and the seal.

Receipts had existed only since 1994, and existed because Sabin Ott had put them there. He had been a junior clerk then, and had been asked to make a form for the arrears account, which had been empty for its entire history, and he had made one out of a good instinct for a thing that had never been done, and forty years later a man was using it four times a year without knowing that it had been designed by somebody who did not expect to live long enough to see it used.

* * *

The founder’s clause was in the same hand and was the reason the whole thing had survived, and it was four words long: in trust for the peace of it. The trust had held the peace of Anhalt for two hundred years by never once disturbing anything in it. No rent had been demanded and none had been collected. No road had been made, no court established, no school founded, no boundary walked, no tree felled under the trust’s authority, and no poor person in the province had received a benefit from the trust or been refused one, because the trust had no officer in Anhalt and had never sent one.

The fifth trustee, in 1904, put the question in writing to the three senior clerks of the time in a memorandum of four pages and asked what the trust was for. The answer survives in the file, is dated the eleventh of April 1904, and is a single sentence. The trust is for the peace of the province, and the province is as it is described, and there is nothing further to be done.

“Who asked in 1904?” said Ilse.

“The fifth queen. Marianne of the second house. She wrote four pages and she was right, and the three clerks who answered her took eleven days to write one sentence, and I have read both documents.”

“Were they wrong?”

“They were not wrong,” said Sabin Ott. “That is the part I have never been able to put down anywhere. They were not wrong, and they answered the question she asked, and she did not ask the right one.”

The estate, being a charity, had been advised in 1831 that it could not hold what it did not own, and had spent four years and a small fortune proving it, and had been told in one line that the schedule is the schedule, and had then, for the next hundred and ninety-five years, found that the sentence could be made to do an enormous amount of quiet work.

* * *

The farmer came in on the eleventh of January, in the wrong queue, and said his name was Jost Vollmer and that he had come to pay rent.

He was forty-four and he farmed sixty acres with a barn that had a hoist in it, and forty of the acres were on the eastern side of the Aue, and he had been asked twice in his life why he paid rent, once by a bank manager and once by a woman from the paper, and he had told both of them the same thing, which was that his father had paid it and it was not a small amount.

“You take rent,” said Ilse, “for land you farm.”

“For land I farm. It is in the schedule.” He put the book wallet on the counter. It had forty booklets in it, one for each year since 1986, and each booklet held a receipt for four hundred marks, and the receipts were stamped and numbered in a consecutive series running from one, and the series had never had a gap in it. “My father paid the first one for me, in advance, before I was born, for the life of his son, and your office took it, and an office that takes a payment cannot afterwards say the payment was not due. Forty years. I have brought them because I was told to bring them.”

“Every year.”

“Every year. I ask for it at the counter and a man gives it to me and writes the number, and I go home, and I have never once been asked what it was for, and neither have I ever asked, because it was not mine to ask about.” He straightened the book wallet so the elastic sat square. “It is four hundred marks. That is not a small sum to hand over for forty years for a thing you cannot see.”

The clerk who took the money at the counter was called Kellner and had been doing it for four years, and stamped the receipt and wrote the number in the book with the same expression on her face each January, which Jost Vollmer had read, correctly, as respect. Nobody had ever asked her what the four hundred marks was for either. She had the file open in front of her every time, because the procedure required the file to be open, and the file was a folder, and the folder was thin.

* * *

Sabin Ott came into the corridor at four o’clock with the file for the province under his arm, and he had been senior clerk for twenty-six years and had served three queens and had drafted, in 1994, the instrument that first gave the trustees a schedule of rents and a form on which to receive them, and he was fifty-eight, and he was the last person living who had ever been in the counting-house at Wesel, where the founding instrument had been engrossed, because he had been born in the room above it and his mother had been housekeeper there and had carried the notaries their tea in and out of that room for nine years.

“Let me put the position plainly, your Grace,” he said. “The trust has one asset. It is an account of arrears that a man in a field has been paying into for forty years, and it is the only thing the trust owns, and if the trust is wound up tomorrow morning the account goes to him, and if it is not wound up the account goes on being what it is, which is a sum the trust has never asked anybody for and cannot now ask anybody for, because the limitation ran out in 1992 and the estate’s own solicitors advised in writing that no claim could be made, and the solicitor who advised it has been dead since 2001.”

“You advised that,” said Ilse.

“I initialled the file,” said Sabin Ott. “The advice is correct and I have never had cause to doubt it. The difficulty is that the advice is about what the trust may do and you are asking me about what the trust is, and those are different questions and only one of them has ever been answered in writing.”

She asked for the balance sheet of the trust on the Friday and was given one sheet with a single line under the heading of assets. The arrears of Anhalt: one hundred and sixty thousand marks, being the sum of forty annual payments of four hundred marks received and not applied. Under liabilities there was nothing, because a trust with no power to spend could not have one, and under the heading of net assets, for the first time in the history of the estate, there was a figure that was not zero. Sabin Ott put it down in front of her and said nothing at all, which was the most eloquent thing that had happened in that office in twenty-six years.

“Are you against it?”

“I am a clerk,” said Sabin Ott. “I have had no vote since 1994 and I am not eligible for one until 2029, and you may minute that if you wish. What I am against is the answer of 1904, and I have been against it for twenty-six years, and there is no column in the ledger for it.”

* * *

The court sat in a room with a red rug, which is a thing that happens and about which everybody in the building has an opinion. Ilse had come with a bundle and a proposition, and her proposition was that a trust exists for a purpose and ends when the purpose is discharged, and that the purpose was the peace of a province, and that two hundred years of not touching anything was an unusually good performance of a peace, and that a court which required a sealed trust to go on doing nothing, at a cost to nobody but its own fees, had misunderstood its own function.

The judge, Annelie Krast, was sixty-eight and had been on the bench nineteen years and had settled the estate’s 1831 case out of the records in eleven minutes, and she listened to all of it with a pen and then asked the trust’s solicitor what the alternative was.

“The trust continues,” said Master Wesche. “Quietly. As it has.”

“For how long?”

“As long as the purpose is undischarged, my lady.”

“And when is the purpose discharged?”

Nobody answered that. Two of the eight trustees before Ilse had believed the answer was in 1904 and had said so in writing and had been outvoted, and had accepted the division in the register without complaint, because a trustee who does not accept a division is removed at the next quarter, and both of them had children and neither of them was a fool. The judge adjourned for ninety minutes to consider, and during the adjournment Sabin Ott came and stood in the corridor outside the room with the file against his chest, and did not look at her, and said, “Your Grace, we have been the peace, and the peace is a thing you are supposed to be rather than a thing you are.”

* * *

The application was heard on a Thursday afternoon in the room with the red rug, and it took two hours and forty minutes, and the estate had never had an application before, because the estate had never before had anybody who wanted to wind it up.

Ilse did not argue about the peace at length, because the peace was not in dispute and had not been in dispute since 1826, and everybody in the room including the farmer knew it. She argued the arithmetic instead, in a document of nine pages which she had prepared in a night and a half, and the arithmetic was that the trust had collected four hundred marks a year for forty years from one man, had spent nothing upon him, had built nothing, maintained nothing, and employed nobody, and held at the end of it a balance sheet consisting of a debt and a shelf. A court that declined the application would be holding a piece of paper about a sum of money for the purpose of being able to go on holding a piece of paper about a sum of money.

“Is the man here?” said the judge.

“He is here, my lady.”

“Let him be asked whether he wants it.”

Vollmer stood up at the back and took a moment with his hands. “I have wanted it for forty years, and I would like it in the record that I have never once wanted it as a gift, because a gift can be refused, and this is not a gift. This is the other side of a receipt.”

“The trust cannot keep what it has collected,” she said. “It has no power to spend it. It has no power to hold land. It has no officer in the province and no prospect of an officer. The only lawful thing it can do with a hundred and sixty thousand marks is the one thing it has done for two centuries, which is to give it back, in the same form, at the counter, on a Tuesday, with a stamp.”

“The trust has no power to give it back,” said Master Wesche. “There is no clause.”

“There is no clause,” said Ilse Brandeis, “and there is a rule, and the rule is that a payment made by a person who did not know the law cannot be taken back from him, and the value of it enures to the person who made it, and I have never in eleven years seen a court apply that rule and I should not have thought it possible, and I am asking this court to do it, and I am aware what I am asking.”

“Your Grace,” said the judge, “I have sat in this court nineteen years and I have never applied that rule to an estate with a red rug in the name of it. I propose to apply it, and I propose that the paragraph be read into the record, and I propose that we all go home and that nobody mention it.”

The deed provided that a trustee should be crowned once upon appointment and should not thereafter be crowned twice, and at the close the judge asked whether the ninth seal was accounted for, and Ilse Brandeis took it out of the box on the table and broke it herself, in the room, into four pieces, and put the four pieces in her own pocket, and the minute records that the seal was produced, broken, and returned to the person who had made it. The farmer was given a receipt for one hundred and sixty thousand marks, stamped and numbered, in the same series, and put it in the book wallet with the other forty, and the elastic went on square.

The two who had voted in 1904’s favour had come to hear it and had brought their own copies of the memorandum, and sat at the back and did not speak and did not look surprised. Vollmer’s daughter had driven him and had made a table on her knee and had asked, twice, in the interval, what a receipt was worth, and had been told by her father that a receipt is worth the thing it is a receipt for, and had written that down, and had underlined it.

* * *

The clerk’s last day was the Friday and it was also his first, as a retired man, and he had not gone home. He stood in the corridor with a cardboard box and his own coat over his arm, and everybody in the office had come to say the thing that is said, and at half past four the room was empty and he sat down at the bench in the press room with the founding instrument, which had come out of the strongbox for the wind-up and was going back into it that evening, and which he had read perhaps four hundred times in his life and had never once read to the end.

The counting-house at Wesel had been a merchant’s counting-house until 1819 and had a plaster ceiling of fruit and a window blocked in 1841 for a reason nobody recorded, and it was the room in which the founding instrument had been engrossed over four days in 1826 by a notary who did the work himself, and Sabin Ott’s mother had carried tea into it eleven times a day for nine years, and had told him, when he was seven and asked her what the men were doing in there, that they were writing down what a place was, and that you only get to do it once.

He read it with a pencil, because that is what he did. He underlined each clause in the margin as he initialled it, in the same soft hand he had used since 1968, and he had underlined the whole of the trust two hundred times over the years, and he came to the last leaf and drew the pencil up in the air to make the first stroke, and stopped, because the leaf in his hand was the thirty-first and the engrossment in the counting-house had been written on thirty-two.

He turned it over. On the back, in a hand older than his own and slanted the other way, as though the man had been standing on the other side of the table, there were eleven words and a full stop.

And this trust shall end the day it is no longer needed.

He read them once, and then he put the pencil down flat on the bench, and made his initial in the margin beside them, and wrote the date under it, and wrote his own name, because that is what the margin was for.
