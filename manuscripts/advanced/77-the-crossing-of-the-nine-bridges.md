# The Crossing of the Nine Bridges

![Cover art: The Crossing of the Nine Bridges](../art/advanced/77-the-crossing-of-the-nine-bridges.webp)

The light went off the Coldrun all at once on the eleventh of October, the way it always did, like a lid coming down, and Odo trimmed the green-shaded lamp in the crossing house and set the two books on the counter where anybody coming off the slip could see them and be reassured by them. The first was the quarterly roll of the Nine Bridges, copied in by nine clerks from nine toll houses. The second was his.

The roll was the public record of the river and it was made of pieces, one for each bridge, and each piece was kept by the family that kept that bridge, and each bridge carried a different toll and a different clerk, so the roll agreed with itself about as well as nine households agree about a boundary. His book was the river's own count, kept in a single sequence, because the ferry was the only crossing on the Coldrun where a person who could not lawfully be passed over a bridge might still lawfully be put down on the far bank.

The law was the Pastoral Statute of 1704, ninth clause, and it read that no body whatsoever should be carried above the water more than twice in the term of its natural life, saving infants not yet answerable. At the foot of the same clause came the third crossing, and the third was given away four times over: for a marriage, for the call of a physician, for a body’s own passage to the grave of a parent, and for the return of a body to the ground that bore it.

The statute was a hundred and thirty-nine years old and had been written by graziers, in a dispute about sheep, and no one alive had been present at the writing of it. Men had been in that boat, by the ferryman’s reckoning, for two hundred and thirty years. The statute had merely arrived late and taken the credit.

Odo’s book had three ruled columns, which were the three the statute named: name, bank, passage. The fourth was not the statute’s. Odo had begun it in the spring of 1791, at nineteen years of age, in a hand that leaned to the left, and he had never told anybody what it was for, and he had told the three ferrymen after him, Fennick, then Ilde, then Ardan, that the column was older than the book. They had copied it up faithfully, as you copy a column into a book, because a column is a column and the man above you has been in the boat longer than you have been alive.

It ran to three hundred leaves of small marks, some of them crosses, some of them initials, some of them nothing at all but a pen lifted and put down twice, and it was much longer than any other column, and it was the only one anybody had ever written in.

He had counted nine hundred and eleven thousand crossings, which worked out at forty-seven a day if you averaged the year, and he knew each of them the way a man knows the creaks of a stair. He had also declined, by his own count, something over four hundred thirds.

Not one of those four hundred was in the book, and he had never written a reason for any of them, and there was a reason he could have written, which was that a reason would make a fifth column, and a fifth column would be a hearing, and a hearing is a court, and nothing sits above the Pastoral Statute but the King and the weather.

* * *

The slip creaked. A woman came up the boards with an oilskin parcel held against her chest like a bad tooth.

She was perhaps thirty-four and thin in the way of people who stand a long time. “I want the third,” she said. “I have the certificate. Ottoline Vass wrote it on Tuesday and I had it oiled against the wet, because I knew you would be particular about the wet.” She set the parcel on the counter and did not open it, which was a good sign and also a poor one. “My sister is dying at Kestrel and the physician says she is the physician’s call, and she has written it in the proper form, and I would like to be in the room when it happens, and I would like to be there for the part after it too, whatever that is.”

Odo took the parcel and unwrapped it. The paper was good, the hand was Vass’s, the seal was wax and not a thumbprint, and the wording was the wording: the bearer requires the third crossing under the ninth clause, for the physician’s call upon Ilse Cray of Kestrel, who is not expected to recover. He read it twice. Then he turned the book toward her and put his finger on the ruled space where an entry would have to go, and there was no such space, and the space that was there had a mark in it.

“That is not for you,” he said.

“That is a column.”

“It is not a column. It is a question answered in a mark, and questions are not facts, and a fact is what this book is for.” He kept his finger where it was, which was a thing he had learned to do with Fennick, who had been a good man and had frightened people. “The third crossing exists,” he said, “and there is nowhere in this book to put it.”

The woman looked at the book for a while. Outside, the river came at the pilings with the flat unhurried sound of a thing that has never once been argued with. Then she said, quite evenly, “Then it is not a law. It is a book.” And Odo said, “Yes.” She nodded, as if he had handed her something she had come for, and put the oilcloth back over the certificate, and took it away with her under her arm. At the door she stopped and asked how many people had stood where she was standing and been told the same sentence.

He said that he had stopped keeping that count in his head, and that by the way he kept it in his head it was rather over four hundred, and that it was not in the book either. “Why not?” she asked, and he said, “Because reasons get copied.”

* * *

The boy came on a Thursday, three weeks later, and he was nineteen and dressed in millwright’s oilskins with a leather tool-roll slung across him, and he asked for a passage to Kestrel for a day’s work and put eleven and four on the counter in coins. Odo found the name in the second volume, Ardis, Tam, in a fair clerkly hand, and stopped, because the name was twice in the book and both times it was eighteen years old or younger, and the first of them said, in a child’s own capitals, that it was done by the mother. Odo put the coins back on the counter and said, “You have none. You have had none since you were five.”

The boy said, at nineteen, standing very straight, that he had crossed the Coldrun twice in his life and remembered one of them, and that the one he remembered was at sixteen for the shop at Ashlade, and that a child carried to a town by its mother is not a passage, because the statute expressly does not count a body not yet answerable. He had the parish sheet folded in his shirt, and he put it on the counter, and Odo read the entry and the date and the signature at the foot, and it was true.

“That is a very good argument,” Odo said, “and it is not mine to hear. I can strike a line. I cannot add one.”

“The toll house can.” The boy said that Nyle Prew at the Ninth had looked at the parish sheet for him, and had agreed with him entirely, and had said that the ninth clause exempts a body not yet answerable and that the entry of 1803 was therefore nothing at all, and had stamped the sheet to say so.

He said that Prew had been extremely correct and had also said, standing in his toll house with the cold coming through the boards, that the sheet was worth nothing whatever, because the sheet was the parish’s business and the count was the ferry’s, and the ferry’s book could be altered by one officer only, and the officer was the man Odo was at that moment speaking to.

Odo looked at the boy for a moment. Then he took a pen, and ruled a line through the entry of 1803, and wrote the date in the margin, and wrote no reason, because the reason was in the book somewhere and he was not the man to explain it to a stranger. “You have one passage,” he said. “Use it like a man with a crown in his pocket, and not like a man with a farthing.” And the boy asked him, before he went, whether anyone had ever struck anything out, and Odo said that in a hundred and thirty-nine years there had been four strikes, and three of them had been made by him.

* * *

The inspector came on the first of November with a boy carrying a wicker basket and a schedule of columns in her own hand. Hester Mire was forty-one and had held the post for six years and had never yet had cause to look inside a register as old as this one, and she sat on the settle by the stove and read the headnote of the Pastoral Statute aloud twice to be sure of the exemptions. “I am required to schedule every column in the book,” she said. “Name, bank, passage. I can do those.”

“The fourth has no heading, no unit, and no denominator, and I cannot schedule a column I do not understand, and a register an officer cannot describe is a private book that a Crown officer has been keeping.” She had written it out plainly and she read it plainly, and she was not unkind, and she was right, and the river did not care.

Odo told her the column was of 1791. He gave her the spring, which was a thing he had never given anybody. She wrote it down, and asked what a mark in it denoted, and he said that a mark denoted a question, and she said that a question is not a column, and he agreed with her, at once, with no argument at all, and she said then it must be struck, and he said no. “Why not? It is irregular. It is an unlabelled column of questions kept in a Crown register for a hundred and fifty years. My office has no option but to recommend it be described or struck.”

“Then describe it,” he said, and she said, “I cannot describe what I do not know.” “I can,” he said. “Say that a column exists, that it is of 1791, that its unit is a mark, and that I will describe its unit before the quarter is done.”

She wrote for a while and then said, “Why does it matter to you what is in it?” and he said, “Because it is the only column anybody has ever written in, and a column nobody writes in gets struck for being empty, and a column people write in gets struck for being irregular, and either way it goes.” She sat with her hands in her lap and looked at him for longer than the question required. Then she said, “You have described it as a column you are obliged to destroy.”

“I have described it as a column I will describe,” he said, and she closed the schedule, and said that the Office would hold a fair copy of the book at the quarter’s end, and that if the column was not described by the last day of December she would recommend the appointment of a relieving officer, which was not a punishment, and which was how a person of seventy-one came to be relieved of a job he had held since he was nineteen.

* * *

He answered the Office on the eighth, in a fair hand, because he had answered the Office four times in fifty-two years and had a form of words. He reported that the register was complete, that no page had been removed, that the roll of the Nine Bridges was produced quarterly and compared, and that the fourth column would be described by the thirty-first of December.

The clerk who took the letter said that the description of a column of the fourth sort was the sort the Office most regretted to receive, and she said it kindly, and she stamped it, and it went up the same road the wicker basket had come down, and the whole of it was filed under Returns of Registers, Folio 214, where it stayed legible for a hundred and thirty years and was read by nobody at all.

* * *

The storm came in on the twenty-fourth and the millrace at Kestrel took the wheel-shaft pin out of the second wheel, and Orrin Ardis went into the race to clear it, and they got him out at the Coldrun Stone bridge at dawn, and the boy was in Ashlade at the shop and did not know until the Tuesday, when a letter came up the mail with a stamp that had been crossed out and put in again by somebody who had not known what the postage was.

He came to the crossing house at four in the afternoon with his face shut and the letter in his hand, and he did not ask for a passage, because he had only the one and he was saving it for a burial. Odo watched him not ask, and understood exactly what it cost him, and wrote nothing in the fourth column, because a man coming to say what a passage is for was not a man asking what the law was for, and he had always been scrupulous about the difference.

Afterwards the boy sat on the settle with his boots up and the fire going and asked the questions that people ask when they have nothing left to hold. “Who decided it? Not the king, I don’t mean the king. Who decided that it was twice?” And Odo told him: graziers, in 1704, in a quarrel about the summer pasture above the Fens, when nine families had put their flocks on the same hillside and the assizes had been asked to make a rule about it, and the rule they made had a body in it, because a body was the only thing in 1704 they could count. “They meant a sheep,” the boy said. “They wrote a man and got a man.”

“They wrote a beast,” Odo said, “and the beast had four legs and did not go to Ashlade to plead. Nobody at the assizes was against it, because the alternative was a fight in April.” He shut the book, which he did not usually do, and put his hand flat on the cover, and said the other thing, which he had said four hundred times and never once softened. “I have no authority over a third. There is no column, and there is no appeal, because the thing that would hear the appeal is the thing that does not exist, and if it existed I should be beneath it.” And the boy said, after a while, “And if somebody was stopped unfairly?”

And Odo said, “Then you have lost, and I have not done it, and there is nowhere to send it, and the river will be here tomorrow.” And the boy said, “Yes,” in the voice of a man putting a tool down, and neither of them said anything else, and both of them were right about their own reasons, which is the only kind of being right two people can manage at once.

* * *

On the twenty-ninth of November the boy came at half past seven in the morning, before the light properly came, with his father's tools in the roll on his back and a paper from the parish for the ground at Kestrel, and he said he wanted the passage and he said what it was for, out loud, in the ordinary voice a clerk would use, and Odo wrote his name in the second column and the bank and the passage, and the pen went on for the count, because the rule was that the count went on the line and no one ever read the number. And then the boy said, “What is it for?” Not the passage.

He put his thumb on the ruled columns, the three of them, and asked what the law was for, and Odo understood, at seventy-one, with his hand still on the pen, that this was the question, the whole question, the one that had been put to him in that room four hundred times and once in every mark in the fourth column, and he looked at the boy and gave him the only true thing he had.

“I have never known,” he said. “I have never once thought it was my business, and I would like to be relieved.” The boy nodded as if that had been a figure and not a sentence.

And Odo wrote under the date, in the fair small hand of a man who has ruled ten thousand lines, the description he had promised to the inspector of registers: that the fourth column bears, for each person who has asked him what the law is for, one mark, and that he has not recorded who, or when, or what was said, and that the column is longer than the other three together and that he can no longer say why he began it, only that he was nineteen and that he had a pen. Then he turned the book round and pushed it across the counter, and the boy wrote the date, and the last page of the register is the description at the top of it and the column below, going down the leaf and on to the facing one, three hundred leaves of small crosses and small initials and small lifted pens, in fifty-two years of his hand and a hundred and fifty-two years of the hands after his.
