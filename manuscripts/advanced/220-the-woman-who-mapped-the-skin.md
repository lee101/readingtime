# The Woman Who Mapped the Skin

![Cover art: The Woman Who Mapped the Skin](../art/advanced/220-the-woman-who-mapped-the-skin.webp)

The hand clinic is on the second floor of Auckland Hospital, at the north end of a corridor with a window at the end of it, and the room it uses has a couch in it with a towel over the arm of the couch, a trolley beside the couch, and a shelf above the trolley.

On the shelf there is a case that opens flat. Inside the case there are ten nylon filaments, each one about as long as a small finger, each one bent at a right angle in its own plastic holder, and stamped into the base of every holder is a number.

Wren Terei has worked in that room for nineteen years. She is forty-four. Her hair is salt and pepper and she keeps it in a low bun, and she wears a teal scrub top over a grey long-sleeved shirt with the sleeves pushed up past her elbows, because her work is hands and there is no reason to keep a sleeve in the way of a thumb.

She is there because of a man she saw when she was twenty-six, in 2008, who told her his thumb did not feel right, and whom she told to wait, and who waited eighteen months, and who came back with the nerve in his hand tied up in a knot at the place where it had been sewn, and a thumb that had no idea what it was for.

* * *

The numbers on the holders go from two and a half grams to three hundred grams. They are graded, not equal. The blue one, the 5.07, bends under four grams of force and a healthy fingertip feels it every time. The green one, the 5.11, needs sixteen grams. The yellow one, the 6.11, needs forty. Above that there is one for a hundred grams and one for three hundred, and a hand that cannot feel the hundred-gram filament cannot feel the oven.

Wren works down the case. She picks the lightest one the patient can feel, then the next one up, and she records the first filament the patient misses. That number is the reading. It goes on the sheet in the box marked sensory level, and after two years of doing it that way, every surgeon who sends a hand to her can read the box.

The case also has a sheet in the lid, and on the sheet there is a row for each filament and a column for the number of times it has been put on a person. Fifteen is the number. A filament is good for fifteen patients and then it is rubbish, because nylon takes a set, and once it has taken a set it bends before you are pushing as hard as you think you are.

Wren started that log in her second year because of a registrar named Tohu who asked her, in a corridor, how many patients she had tested with the blue one. She said she did not know. She went back to the room and started the log and has not missed a line since.

* * *

Wiremu Sola came to the clinic in March. He is thirty-one. He is a line cook in a hotel kitchen and he does the pass, which means he stands at a heat lamp for eight hours with a plate in one hand and his thumb on the underside of its rim.

In November he had put a knife through the base of his right thumb, palm side, on a wet board, going away from him. The wound was four centimetres. The surgeon at Auckland found two cut tendons and a cut median nerve, and did both in the same afternoon, and told him afterwards that the nerve in a hand comes back about a millimetre a day and that the nerve in a thumb is a hundred and three millimetres long.

Wiremu said the millimetres would have to do the work then, and the surgeon said yes, and said also that the nerve would come back and it would come back wrong, and that the clinic would have to keep the map.

The map is a sheet of paper with an outline of a hand printed on it, palm view, and Wren draws on it in pencil. She works at four sites, because the nerve has a pattern and the pattern is knowable. The pad of the thumb, the pad of the index finger, the ball of the thumb where the muscle sits, and the web between the thumb and the finger on the top side. She writes the filament number at each of the four, and where a filament can be felt somewhere and not somewhere else she draws a line between the two, and the lines on Wiremu's sheet in March were all of them at the top of his hand and not one of them on the palm side.

She also wrote a date, and she wrote the distance, and on the second visit she wrote a second date.

Twenty-two weeks apart.

She did not say anything about the two numbers. She has been careful about that for nineteen years, because the moment she says a rate out loud the rate stops being a measurement and becomes a promise.

* * *

By June the lines had come down the palm side of the thumb and stopped halfway. By September they were down to the pad and the readings were 5.11 at the thumb, 5.11 at the index, 6.11 at the ball of the thumb, and no reading at all at the web.

The patient had been told, in the ordinary way, that the nerve had come back. Wren had written on the form, in the box at the bottom that the insurer reads, protective sensation: partial. Nobody in the system had said what partial weighs.

Wiremu had been cleared for work with a cut-resistant glove on his right hand and he had asked for a lighter one, and he had been given a lighter one, and he had gone back to the pass in September and had stood at the heat lamp for four months without a problem.

* * *

Then, in the October, he said the thing that made the map a different object.

He said, “Your sheet says I cannot feel the middle of my hand, and I can feel the middle of my hand perfectly well.”

Wren did not argue with him. She asked him to show her. He put his hand palm up on the trolley and she put the blue filament on the middle of the ball of the thumb and he said, sharply, “There.”

She went to the shelf, took out the second blue filament, which was in the case and had been opened that morning, and she broke the seal on it in front of him and she put it on the same spot and he said nothing.

She went down the case. Green at the ball of the thumb. Yellow at the edge of the scar. Nothing at two centimetres out from the scar.

Then she opened the log in the lid of the case and she counted, on her fingers, in silence, with her eyes shut the way she counts when she is not proud of the answer.

Four hundred and six patients.

The blue filament in her hand had four hundred and six names above it in her own handwriting and it was still on the case and it still bent when she pushed it and she had pushed it, that afternoon, on her own thumbnail, and it had bent far too easily.

* * *

A worn filament is not a small error. It is an error in a fixed direction and it always pushes the same way. The fibre has lost stiffness, so it reaches full bend under less force than its number claims, so the hand feels it at a lighter grade than the hand can really tell, so the number written on the sheet is better than the hand. Every reading she had taken with that filament was a hand that was more sensitive than her sheet said.

Wren put the blue one in the bin and did not put the second one in the bin, because that one was new and had been on two patients.

She then did the harder thing. She took out every sheet from the last two years with a blue reading in it, which was most of the folder, and stuck a label on each one saying the reading had exceeded the use of the filament and needed repeating.

Then she told the surgeon, who was not angry, and asked the obvious question, which was how many hands carried that same reading. Wren said she did not know. He said he would need a number by Friday.

* * *

There is a second thing about a map like this and it does not show up as a number at all.

The brain has its own picture of the hand, and the picture does not get redrawn by the nerve arriving. The nerve in Wiremu's thumb had come back and joined the wrong endings in places, because one growing fibre branches, and where it branches it can reach two patches of skin, and the hand now has one patch sending its signal into a part of the brain that has been answering for a different patch since he was two years old.

On the sheet of paper the map is perfect. The map says where the filament can be felt and the map cannot say where he thought he felt it.

So on the November visit she stopped asking about numbers and asked a different question, and she wrote his answers in the notes column in his own words, in quotation marks, because that is a rule she made for herself after the first year.

“If I touch your thumb, tell me where it is.”

He said, “There. That is the middle of my palm.”

It was the pad of his thumb.

“If I touch the edge of your thumb by the nail, where is that?”

“Across the palm, down near my wrist.”

“Twenty-three millimetres is what you said. You were within one centimetre. That is not nothing. It is two and a half centimetres of nothing on either side.”

He thought about that. Then he said, “So it is all right.”

“It is not all right,” Wren said. “It is the right kind of not right. That one heals with practice. The other one does not.”

* * *

There is a third thing, and it is the one that had been on her form as a single box and had to become three.

The nerve comes back in the wrong order. Pain and temperature arrive before light touch, and they arrive out of order, so that the first thing a patient feels on the new skin is often not touch. It is a pin, a burning, a wasp that is not there. The clinicians call it mechanical hyperalgesia and Wren has never said the words out loud to a patient in her life, but she writes them in the notes when she means that the lightest filament on the scale came back as a thing the patient called sharp.

At the edge of Wiremu's scar, at two points, the lightest filament in the case read as a needle. He did not flinch much. He said it was like a hair on his arm, and he said it the way you say something you have decided not to make a fuss about.

The form had nowhere to put that. So she wrote it in the notes, and she drew a small second column on the sheet, and she wrote at the top of it: what he called it.

* * *

The burn happened in the February, at ten past eight in the evening, on the pass.

Wiremu had been on the pass for five months and working fast. The glove was a size small and it was cutting the tip off his thumb pad, so he had stopped wearing it for the last two hours of the shift, and at ten past eight he took a tray of a gratin out of the combi oven and took it by the rim, because his thumb was the only part of him the glove did not cover.

The rim of that tray was a hundred and forty degrees. He put his thumb pad on it. He did not feel it at the time. He has never been certain that he did not feel it, which is the part that frightens him, because what he has instead is the fact that the numb patch along the side of the thumb was gone afterwards, for about six hours, and then came back with the pain.

He finished the section. He finished the ticket. He went back to the fridge, and at half past nine he took the glove off and saw that the pad of his thumb was white and blistered in a patch about a centimetre and a half across, and it hurt in the specific way that a burn hurts when it goes past the first layer.

He told nobody for four hours, and he told his flatmate, who told the emergency department at two in the morning.

* * *

Superficial partial thickness, a centimetre and a half by two, over the ball of the thumb. The burns clinic dressing it was two floors below Wren's room, and the fellow who saw it wrote the word delayed in the box the register has for delayed, because a burn on a hand that was mapped as partly sensate is a different event from a burn on a hand that never was, and it costs the clinic a great deal more.

Five weeks of dressings. Eleven weeks off work in total. He went back on a reduced roster that did not include the pass, and at the end of it he had a scar on the ball of his thumb that he could not feel at all, and a skin graft donor site on the inside of his forearm that he could not feel either, and the two of them were different shapes.

The forearm is the part that should have stopped him and did not.

* * *

Wren went back to the map in the April. The burn scar read 6.65, which is the filament that bends under a hundred grams, which means the patch has no protective sensation at all, which means a hot tray, a kettle, a soldering iron and a stub of a cigarette are all the same object.

The pad of the thumb read 5.07. The number on the form was right. The number on the form had been right for eight months and a man had nearly lost a hand off it.

He had asked for the lighter glove and he had been given the lighter glove, and nobody had refused him anything, and every single piece of paper was correct.

* * *

What came after that was not a treatment. There is no treatment for a map.

Sensory re-education is teaching. It is done on a couch with the patient's eyes shut and it costs nothing and it has no instrument in it at all. You touch the skin once and they say where it was. You touch it twice and they say there were two and where they were. You go from two points to one, and from coarse to fine, and the patient sits with it while the brain builds a new picture, and it takes weeks and it takes almost nobody's patience but the patient's.

She gave him a card with the numbers one to nine on it, and she wrote a number on the palm of his hand with her finger while his eyes were shut, and he could feel every one of them and he got five out of ten.

He said, “That was a three.”

It was a three, upside down, written the way he used to write it in his first year at school before anyone taught him.

She wrote that on the sheet as well.

* * *

At thirty weeks he came back and she mapped him with a new blue, one that had been on four patients, and the numbers were 5.07 at the pad of the thumb, 5.11 at the pad of the index, 6.11 at the edge of the surgical scar, and 6.65 at the burn and at the graft donor site on his forearm, and there was no reading at all at the web on the top of the hand at all.

The first new thing on the form was a line printed under the reading. It said, this is a reading taken on the date at the top of this sheet, and it is a property of one nerve in one patch of skin, and it is not a property of the hand.

The second new thing was a column for the name of the filament and a column for how many patients it had been on, and a rule that the blue one went in the bin at fifteen, and a rule that a filament which bent too easily went in the bin at once whatever its count said.

The third new thing was that she made him write the time at the top of the sheet in the space for the date.

* * *

She did not retest everybody with a new filament in the last year. She could not, there were four hundred sheets. When the department asked what she wanted to do about it, she said she would start at the front of the folder, and would not tell anyone which hands had been tested with the old ones, because the moment she tells them the hands stop being hands and start being a column of liabilities.

So there is a plastic box in the store room with two hundred and eleven sheets in it, the top one labelled repeat with a fresh filament, the rest pending.

The last one Wiremu gave her was not 5.07 everywhere, and it was not 5.07 in the place that matters, and the difference between the two sheets is about four hundred patients.

The sheet went into the folder and will be looked at again in a year, when somebody needs to know whether he can hold a hot plate with the ball of his thumb, and the number at the top of it will still be the number he was cleared on. It will still be correct. It will still be wrong. Nobody who finds it in the folder will think about a tray at a hotel kitchen at ten past eight in the evening.
