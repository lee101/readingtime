# The Plague Year of the Bright Rooks

![Cover art: The Plague Year of the Bright Rooks](../art/advanced/68-the-plague-year-of-the-bright-rooks.webp)

The flock came over the Crag at dusk on the first Thursday of March, and Corvin Hale counted them aloud, because the rite is that the keeper counts and the clerk writes, and neither man alters the other’s figure.

The Marrow rooks are not like rooks elsewhere. Their feathers carry a pale bloom, the colour of wet slate in January, and no one in the valley has ever seen a dark one on the crag, and the four-hundred-year-old book records the flock’s colour in the same column as its number, between the ruled lines and the date, in an ink that is still legible because the page was folded away from the damp.

He reached four thousand two hundred and eleven at the point where the birds crossed the moon, which is where he had always reached it, and Pedr Dunn wrote it in the right-hand column of the Marrow Rite Book with a pencil worn down to a nail. Then Dunn stood up on the vestry step and read the left-hand column aloud, which is the whole of his office at a Reckoning, and he read it slowly, so that anybody standing in the churchyard could follow along if they wanted to.

The roll of the dead entered for the year was four thousand five hundred and eleven.

Corvin kept the count. Dunn kept the roll. That is the arrangement, and it has held since the year the book begins, and no one in the Marrow valley had ever seen the two figures part company. He looked up at the moon. The birds were already past it. There was nothing else in the sky, and the number was what it had always been, and it would be the same number at dawn.

“Three hundred short,” Dunn said. He wet the pencil, although there was nothing left to write. “The book does not have a column for that.”

The Rite Book had four thousand two hundred and eleven ruled lines, one for each bird, ruled in 1604 by a man who had counted the flock in his first spring and ruled what he found. A bird that died was replaced inside the hour, and the keeper entered the replacement against the same line, and the flock had never in four hundred years been anything but four thousand two hundred and eleven. There was no fifth column. There was no provision, because for four centuries there had been nothing to provide against.

Amos Hale had died on the second of February, and Corvin had entered him at number four thousand two hundred and thirty-eight, and the number was in the roll in Dunn’s careful upright hand, and there was no line in the book for it.

* * *

The council met nine days later in the upstairs room over the wool exchange, and Dr. Anwen Sarr came down from the county town with a satchel of case books and the particular stillness of a physician who has spent a year being reasonable.

“Two clerks,” she said, before the alderman had finished putting his hat down. “One at the vestry step, one at the mill. An interim provision, in writing, so that the roll is never unentered after dark. I am not asking the council to believe anything about birds. I am asking it to stop throwing away the only record this valley has of itself.”

The alderman was Dafydd Trethowan, who had been mayor eleven years and had a scheme. “The flock is being replaced,” he said. “Six hundred birds from the south coast, guaranteed to nest, guaranteed to come at dusk. A new rookery on the town side of the river, on ground we own, built by the town. The count will be a count of ordinary birds instead of a relic, and the valley will have a rookery it can repair.”

“Where in the valley does a new count leave the three hundred?” Corvin asked.

“Where it leaves everything else that no longer fits,” the alderman said. “Keeper, I have two thousand people in this town. I have a doctor telling me the number of the dead has outrun the number of the birds that carry them, and I have a council chamber full of people who would like to be told what is walking up the Crag at dusk, and I do not have that, and neither do you.”

Sarr put her satchel on the table. “I have watched one of them for two hours,” she said. “On the eleventh of February, at Bwlch-y-Garn, with a lamp. It stood on the wall by the lane and it did not move and it did not speak, and when I read out the names of the year to it, it went down. Four names. The fifth one it is still standing in the lane for, and I read it the name, and it went down as well, and the register for that house is closed, and it is the only time in my career that a name has worked.”

That was true, and it did not help, because the rite was not available for a lamp and a house, and the town council was not the vestry, and the roll was not a case book.

The alderman was not a fool and Corvin had never thought him one. That was fair. Corvin had walked the flight every evening for eleven years and had never once been asked a question he could not answer from the book, and the book had two columns, and the columns had always agreed, and he had no idea what the agreement had been keeping in the ground.

He said what his office was. “The flock is a living structure under the Amity of 1604, and the Amity schedules the rookery and not the birds, and the rookery cannot be burned while the rite is observed. The rite is the town’s. I keep the book. I do not decide what goes in it.”

“The book,” said the alderman, “is a hundred and forty pages of a dead language called numbers, and you are the only man in this valley who can read it, and you are telling me the book is not the council’s to change.”

“The book is the council’s,” Corvin said. “I am what the council has to make it legible.”

* * *

The boy was called Lowri Bevan and he was twelve, and in April of the year the plague began he had counted the flock for a school project, and he had done it in a fortnight, in the evenings, from the same stone Corvin used.

He came to the crag on a Tuesday with a sheet of ruled paper folded into eight and a stub of pencil, and he was very calm about it, in the way of a boy who has been told his work is unimportant and has begun to suspect otherwise.

“There were four thousand two hundred and eleven,” he said. “I counted them three times because I got twelve thousand something the first time and I knew that was wrong. And then the vestry book says the valley entered four thousand two hundred and eleven dead that year, and I copied that down too, because the mistress said a project should be checked against something.”

He unfolded the sheet. It was a list in a small upright hand, one to four thousand two hundred and eleven, with the number of the rook and what it was doing when he got to it, and beside the list, in a second column, the single figure four thousand two hundred and eleven.

“At the bottom I wrote both four thousand two hundred and eleven, and I underlined it, and the mistress said underlining was not a thing you do in a project.” He looked at the page and did not smile. “But it was true. The birds and the roll were the same number.”

Corvin looked at the sheet for a long time. “That is a year ago.”

“It is a year ago,” Lowri said. “You told me to bring it anyway.”

The boy was right to have come. The vestry books went back a hundred and forty years in unbroken runs, and every one of them closed at the same figure, four thousand two hundred and eleven, and the county returns put the Marrow valley’s deaths at between three thousand nine hundred and four thousand four hundred, and the flock had never once been anything but four thousand two hundred and eleven, and nobody had ever asked what a valley did that produced a dead count to the bird.

* * *

The five villages burned the rookery on the ninth of June, and Corvin was at the lower mill when the smoke went up, and he ran uphill in a coat he did not take off, and by the time he reached the crag there was nothing left of the terrace but a black ledge and the smell of a building that had been timber and lime for four centuries.

The birds were still in the air. That was the part nobody had expected. There were four thousand two hundred and eleven of them, circling a crag with nothing on it, and the villagers had not touched the flock, because the flock was protected and they knew it, and the rookery was a structure and the Amity scheduled structures.

Nobody in the Marrow valley burns rooks. That has never been the practice and it was not the practice that night.

He found the alderman’s clerk in the lane and asked for the warrant. The clerk told him there had been none, and told him honestly why: the meeting at Nant Fach had read out the one sentence of Dr. Sarr’s report that said the bright rooks were the only vector the county could not rule out, and the sentence before it had said the same birds had carried the valley’s dead for four hundred years without a single case traced to them, and the sentence after it recommended the rite be continued and adjusted, and the sentence after that recommended the rookery be inspected. Three sentences had been read. The one they had read aloud was the third.

“They were afraid,” the clerk said. “And they were right to be. We have had the walking since the second of February.”

Corvin went up to the ledge. The birds came in over his head and could not land, and went round again, and the count could not be read off them without the terrace, because the count is read as they come down onto the stone, one bird to a line, and the stone was gone. He stood there until it was fully dark and did the arithmetic in his head, and the arithmetic was not hard. The rite needs a floor.

* * *

In August the town installed the six hundred coastal birds, which were not bright, and a timber rookery on the town side of the river, and the count came back to four thousand two hundred and eleven every evening, and the valley settled, in the way a valley settles when the council has done something.

The plague got worse.

Dr. Sarr kept her registers by address, because addresses were the only thing she could not lose, and when Corvin sat in her surgery in the autumn and looked at them laid out along the shelf he saw a shape he had been looking at for eleven years and had not seen. No case had ever occurred in a house where a death had been entered on the roll and carried in the same dusk. Not one, in two years of registers, from Bwlch-y-Garn to Tre’rnant. The houses that had lost someone since the burning were the houses, and the walking went up the hill at dusk and stood on the crag until it was spoken to, and if you spoke to one and it was not on the roll then it walked off with you beside it, step for step, the way it had walked, and the houses filled like that.

“The quarantine was correct,” she said, when he put it to her. “I have read my own orders back and I would write them again. If we had not shut those doors, we would have had four hundred cases in a fortnight instead of ninety. I closed the doors and I kept the dead inside them, and the dead are the ones who need the flock, and after June the flock had nowhere to put them down.”

“That was before the burning,” Corvin said. “The walking started on the second of February.”

“Yes,” Sarr said. “And I have thought about that for a year and a half, and I can tell you what I have, and it is not a comfort. Nine walkers came up this hill before the ninth of June. Nine. I have their addresses and I have their dates and the last one was on the fourth of June, and the flock was working then, and nothing that I can see in the book accounts for them being on the hill at all, and that is the part of this I have not been able to put in a report.”

“Then we need the terrace back,” Corvin said.

“We need the terrace back by Tuesday,” she said, “and the council will not rebuild it, and if you build it yourself you will be finished, and I will not be able to say in front of a magistrate that I advised it.”

* * *

He began walking the flight alone in October, with a lantern and the book under his arm, and he read the roll aloud on the bare ledge in the dark, one name at a time, and the birds came over and could not land, and nothing happened, night after night, for five weeks.

Lowri came on the fourth night, and then every night, carrying a second lantern and the April sheet in a tin against the weather, and on the ninth night Corvin asked him to read the numbers while he read the names, because Corvin’s voice had begun to fail and the roll was eleven pages longer than it had ever been.

“It has to be the same,” the boy said, on the eleventh night, when the two of them had the book open between them on the ledge. “The birds are four thousand two hundred and eleven, and the roll is four thousand five hundred and eleven. Three hundred names are written in the roll and there is no line for them, because there is no line for anything past four thousand two hundred and eleven. That is not a mystery. That is a book that is too small.”

“It is a book that was ruled to the number it found.”

“It was ruled to the number it found once,” Lowri said, “and then it was never ruled again, and in four hundred years the valley never once had a bad year, and everybody agreed the two numbers matched on purpose.”

Corvin read the last page of the roll with the lantern on it. The three hundred extra names were in Dunn’s upright hand, entered between the eleventh of February and the fourteenth of March, all of them, and among them, at four thousand two hundred and thirty-eight, in the same hand, in the same ink, on the same afternoon, was his father.

“The bright rooks were never the plague,” he said. “They were what we carried our dead on, and the carrying stopped, and the plague is three hundred people walking because there was no bird to carry them home.”

The boy did not answer, and after a while he said, “Then we call them again.”

* * *

It took eleven nights. There is no hurry in the rite and there never was: a name is called, and the bird comes down, and the line is entered, and then the next name, and the dark is the only clock the valley has ever used. On the first night Corvin read four hundred and six names before his voice gave out. On the third he read eleven hundred. On the sixth a line of women from Cwm Ddu set up a lantern line along the road so the reading would not stop at the bottom of the crag, and carried water up to the ledge, and the valley understood by then what was being done and brought what it had.

The terrace came back in four days, because the alderman read the roll in the council chamber on the Thursday and understood it in the way of a man who has been eleven years an alderman, and put it in a schedule of works, and the clerk entered the works in the book, and the book is the town’s, and nobody on the council argued about the birds at all.

On the eleventh night the flock came back over the Marrow valley in a sound the whole valley could hear from every house.

It was not a call. It was four thousand two hundred and eleven birds coming down onto a ledge that had been rebuilt in four days, and the sound of them arriving was the sound the valley had been hearing every evening for four hundred years, in every room in every one of the five villages, and had stopped hearing in June, and heard now again at the same hour as it had always been.

The walking stopped that week, and the last of the three hundred went down on the Tuesday, and Pedr Dunn entered it in the roll as a death, which is what it was, and the column balanced, and the valley buried what it could name.

* * *

On the last page of the boy’s April list there is a space where four thousand two hundred and twelve should be, and another where four thousand two hundred and thirteen should be, and so on for three hundred, and the paper is ruled and the lines are there, because a school exercise book is ruled to four thousand six hundred, and Lowri Bevan had counted three times because he got twelve thousand something the first time and knew it was wrong.

Corvin had the sheet framed in the spring, and hung it in the crag office where the count is read, and the three hundred numbers are blank, and he has not filled them in. He could rule them. He has ruled lines before.

The flock is four thousand two hundred and eleven birds, and it will be four thousand two hundred and eleven tomorrow, and a number that has not been counted is not a number he is going to write down.
