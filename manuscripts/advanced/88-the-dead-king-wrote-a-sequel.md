# The Dead King Wrote a Sequel

![Cover art: The Dead King Wrote a Sequel](../art/advanced/88-the-dead-king-wrote-a-sequel.webp)

Tamsin Roeg was thirty-four years old and he read the dead king’s hand the way other men read weather, and it had taken him eleven years to earn that, because the late hand was not a difficulty of difficulty, it was a difficulty of eleven years, and before Tamsin nobody in the kingdom could read a page of it.

The king had died on the ninth of Frost, aged fifty-one, and had left four hundred and twelve pages, and they were not a testament, and they were not letters, and they were not the four years they appeared to be. They were a reign. The first page gave the year as the seventh after the famine year, which was wrong, because there was no seventh year after the famine year, because the famine was the last year, and the second page gave the number of the assize, and the number was a real number, and the number had been given to a real assize, and the assize had gone to real men in a real hall.

“It is the four years from the seventh year after the famine to the eleventh,” Tamsin said, to the empty reading room, on the fourth day. “It is four years in which the king fed the north, settled the Skell, and had his son judge in his own name, and none of it happened, and all of it is in his own hand, and the hand is the hand of a man writing at night, alone, with the lamp to his right.”

He read to the end of the seventh page, and then he read the last four lines of the seventh page again, and then he copied them into his working book, because the ink of the late hand faded in a way that took four centuries of storage to explain, and the copy was the only copy, and a man who is the only reader of a thing is not permitted to be its only keeper.

The difficulty of the late hand was not that it was illegible. It was that it was almost legible. The early hand was upright and economical, the hand of a man copying a clerk, and by the two hundredth page the letters leaned, and the crossbars had doubled wherever the pen had been dipped twice in haste, so that the same word written six inches apart meant one thing and, an inch away, another, and the difference between the two was sometimes a decision about a siege.

The Queen’s summons had come eleven days after the funeral, and it had not been a summons, because the Queen did not summon, she invited, and the word on the card was in her own hand, and the hand was nothing like the king’s.

* * *

Queen Maelis was sixty-one and had been the wife of a king for thirty years and a queen for none of them, and the chroniclers had settled on a phrase for her, and the phrase was that she was the Queen in Brackets, because in every roll, every warrant, every grant, every warrant of appointment, her name appeared between marks that meant she was present in the sense that a portrait is present, and she had lived inside those marks for so long that she had begun to set her own hand in them.

The brackets had a source. A disputed succession in the fourth king’s time had left two claimants and one crown office, and the marking was invented to record attendance without recording authority: the Queen Consort is named, and the naming is marked, and the marking declares that her presence is not a claim. It had been copied for a hundred and eleven years, and the copying had outlasted the reason, and she had watched four archivists enter it without a question.

Her reasons for the book were three, and she gave them to Tamsin in order, without ornament, in a room with a stove in it and no window that opened.

“First, the kingdom has had a hard reign, and it is entitled to know that its king spent the last four years of his life thinking well of it, and that is worth more to a hungry people than a famine is. Second, my husband was not a good king and I will not pretend it to him in his own hearing. Third, and I put it last because it is the reason and not the ground, my son is doing perfectly well, and a dead man’s book of what should have been done is the only thing in this palace that can make a well-governed reign look like a failure.”

“Which of the three do you want me to think is the reason?” Tamsin said.

“None of them,” she said. “I have three, because one is a reason and two are arguments, and a queen who gave her one reason would be describing herself.”

He gave her the manuscript in June, and in September she sent for him with the copy of the passages she had read, and there were eleven of them, and each had a leaf of her own paper clipped to it with a note, and the notes were not editorial, they were questions, and they were all the same question, written eleven times in eleven slightly different hands because she had written them over a year.

* * *

Prince Aldwyn was twenty-nine and had come in without knocking, which he did, and sat down, which he did, and asked for the fourth page, which he did, and read it standing, and did not sit down again until he had finished, which was the first thing about him that his mother had noticed and the last thing that she would have admitted to noticing.

He came into the world the year the king was thirty-one and frightened, which was a thing the Prince did not know and his mother had never told him, and the passage on the fourth page was the one his father had written at the greatest length, and it was about the eastern road and the granary, and it was four hundred words long, and it was the reason a city had rioted in a real year and eleven men had been hanged in a real square.

He had been educated after his father’s death by two men the kingdom chose out of eleven candidates, one of whom taught him the assizes and one of whom taught him nothing whatever, and he had spent a year doing both, and he had told the registrar, in writing, that the year had been the most useful of his life, which was the sort of thing he said the way other men said that the weather had been good.

“My father fed the north,” Aldwyn said. “In the book.”

“In the book.”

“I have opened the granary at Doul in the third month of every year since I was twenty-three, and I did it without a riot, and there was a famine in the Ash valley two years ago and we were the reason nobody in it died, and I have a list of the tonnage.” He gave the page back. “So I have read it, and it is well argued, and it is thirty years better than what we did, and I am not going to pretend to a room that the version that happened was not worth something, because I have been the one holding it.”

“You have told me you do not want it published.”

“I have told you I do not want to be corrected by a document,” he said. “If it is printed as a record of what my father thought of me, I will read it in one afternoon and I will put it in a drawer, and I will be furious for a year, and the drawer will be the drawer he put me in. He has had me in a drawer since I was eleven.” He straightened the page on the table, exactly the way his father had straightened pages. “If it is printed as what it is, I have no complaint, and I will not have one, and I have said that in writing to the registrar so that it is binding on me and not a courtesy.”

“Which is what?” Tamsin said.

“Whatever the truth of it turns out to be,” the Prince said, “and you are the only man in the kingdom who can tell me, so I would like to stop guessing and start reading.”

* * *

The forger came to the palace in the eleventh month and was given a room with a north window, and his name was Ferrier, and he was forty, and he was the best hand in the kingdom, and he was paid four hundred marks, and the fee was arranged in three parts, and he asked for none of them to be contingent on the result.

A forger’s first hour is spent on the paper and the second on the failures, because a true hand has a way of being wrong that can be learned and no way of being right that can be. Ferrier asked for four pages of the king’s ordinary correspondence and for the desk the king wrote at, with its worn patches, and he tested the imitation by writing, in the king’s voice, a sentence about the Prince that he knew the king had never once thought, and it came out of his hand perfectly, and he looked at it for a while, and then burned it, which was the only unprofessional act of his career and the only thing the Queen ever said she approved of.

He read the manuscript in nine days, which was faster than Tamsin had taken, and then he did not write for a month, and then he wrote two hundred and ninety pages in a hand that people who had seen the king’s late work for forty years could not separate from it, and the work was better than the king’s, and the work was the truth with the arguing taken out.

It was, in fact, a better book. There is no need to pretend otherwise. In Ferrier’s version the king opens the granary on the fourth day because the king is a man who opens granaries, and the Skell is settled in a fortnight because that is how the King of Corvain settles things, and the fourth year ends with the Prince in a black robe, judging, and the King in an ordinary chair, watching his son work, and the whole of it reads like a reign that happened, and would have happened, and ought to have happened, and any reader would take it for the will of a good man and hand it to a child.

Tamsin read both in the same week in the same room, and came to the Queen in the twelfth month and told her that Master Ferrier had done the work of a very good forger and that he would be, in the trade, without a rival south of the mountains.

“And it is not true,” the Queen said.

“No, my lady. It is not true in the way that matters and true in every other way.”

“Then we are finished,” she said, and Ferrier was brought in, and he stood in the middle of the floor with his hat in his hand, and she thanked him for the work and for the nine days and for the fee, and he said that he was glad of the commission, and then he waited, because he was a craftsman and he had spent a month of his life on it and the room was full of people.

“It is better writing than his and it is not what he wrote, and I have read what he wrote, and it is four hundred pages of a man arguing with himself, and I would rather have that in my house than your excellent invention,” she said, and Ferrier bowed and said that he was sorry, and meant it, and took his fee, and left the next morning without asking for the manuscript back, which he was entitled to do, and which the clerk had to write to him three times to explain.

* * *

The passage Tamsin marked for her was on the two hundred and eleventh page, and it was the one she had read before any of the others, and it was the one that had kept her awake in the February.

It was the eastern road. In the book, the King had wanted to open the Doul granary in the third month and had not, and had written four hundred words on why, and the writing was not an excuse, because an excuse asks the reader for a verdict and this asked for nothing. He wrote that the council had told him the eastern road was held, and that he had not sent a man to see, and that he had not sent a man to see because if he had, and the road was held, he would have had to admit that he had been told; and that he had not been ready to be a king who had been told; and that the eleven men were hanged in a square he could see from his own window, and that he had gone to the window on each of the eleven days, and that he had begun to go to the window on the days before, so that it was not only the hanging, it was the eleven days of knowing which eleven it would be, and that he had been thirty-one, and that he had a wife, and that the Queen had said a thing to him at dinner in the second month that he was not going to set down, and that this was the whole of the reason, and that he did not think less of himself for it, and that the city ate and he was not sorry, and that a man may be a good judge and a bad king and it had better be one or the other.

She read it in the first week of June, in the room with the stove and the window that did not open, and she was sixty-one and her husband had been dead seven months, and she read it twice, and then she sent for Tamsin at four in the afternoon and had him read it aloud to her, both hundred words, with the pauses he put in at the king’s own pauses.

“That is a man who did not do it and has told himself exactly why,” she said when he had finished, “and the reason is good, and it is good enough that I could not argue with it, and I have been trying for four months.”

“Yes, my lady.”

“It is not a plan,” she said. “That is what I had wrong. All this time I have been reading it as a plan, a programme, the intentions of a good king who was prevented, and if it were a programme it would be the finest thing in this kingdom and I would fight for it with the whole of the council and I would win, because my son is twenty-nine and I am sixty-one and the council would rather have a memory.”

“And it is not a programme.”

“It is an argument he lost with himself, and he wrote it down at night, in a room, with the lamp to his right,” she said, “and if I print it, my son will be corrected by a dead man. He will be corrected by a dead man in his own father’s hand, in his own language, with his own reasons, and the reasons will be good, and he will not be able to answer one word of it, because the man who wrote them will not be present. That is not history. That is an ambush, and it is the most elegant ambush I have ever read, and my husband built it himself, and I will not hand it to the archivists.”

* * *

The binding was done in nine weeks by the king’s own binder, who was eighty-four and who had bound three of the king’s books and who asked no questions, having said that a book’s binding is not a moral position, and who made the volume without royal arms on the spine, which was a small, quiet act of craft, and which the Queen noticed and did not comment on.

On the flyleaf she wrote a single sentence in her own hand, the hand that had spent thirty years setting itself between marks, and she wrote it large, and the ink was the ordinary royal ink, and there was no bracketing of any kind about it.

“We were both ashamed of the reasons, and mine were no worse than his, and I have had his set down entire under my own name, where anyone at all may come and read them.”

The title of the volume was The Four Years, and the attribution was to Maelis, queen dowager of Corvain IV, editor, and not to the king, and the catalogue in the royal archive, which has never contained an entry of any kind in which a queen was the author of a document concerning the king, recorded it that way in eleven words, and the registrar who made the entry said afterwards that it was the only entry he had ever been asked to make with such a long silence before it.

* * *

The reading room of the royal archive has four windows, a slate floor, and a rule on the wall by the door in the same hand as every other rule in the house: a reader may keep any volume in the room for as long as the light holds, and may come back to it on any later day, and there is no time set upon that which matters.

The Four Years lies on the long table under glass at the north end, open at the two hundred and eleventh page, because that is where a reader who has been reading for five hours has got to, and there is a small brass weight holding the leaves, and a card beside it with the attribution on it, and nothing else on the table.

There is one reader at the table. He is thirty-four, he is the only man in the kingdom who can read the late hand, and he has been in the room since the first light, and he is weeping, and he is not ashamed of it, and after a while he closes the volume, and puts his hand flat on the glass for a moment, and turns round, and the room is empty, and the window is long and grey, and he will come back tomorrow, because they let him.
