# The Court of Small Mirrors

![Cover art: The Court of Small Mirrors](../art/advanced/74-the-court-of-small-mirrors.webp)

The petition reached the clerk’s table on a Thursday morning, in an even hand the clerk took to be copied rather than composed. Four lines. The petitioner, Nessia Colm, twenty-seven, of the Foundling Line, complained that her father, Beren Colm, fifty-eight, of the same dwelling, had refused for eleven months to be presented for the recording of his appearance, and that she could not be married without a current record confirmed against one of his, and no such record could be made without him. She asked the court to compel her father to be looked at.

Ivet Serrat read it again in the anteroom, where the coats of eleven advocates hung on pegs drilled deeper in each succeeding decade. She was twenty-four and in her third year. The Court of Small Mirrors had heard petitions concerning faces, heights, ages, deformities, likenesses, and resemblance to dead relatives for six hundred years, and had never once had a case in which a person wanted to look like themselves.

That was not a policy. It was a fact of supply, and the court had never been unsettled by it enough to write a rule.

The chamber was thirty paces long and eleven wide, with a small mirror let into the panelling every four paces on either side, each in plain black wood, each at the height of a seated adult’s face. The founding instrument gave the reason, and every Marshal had repeated it: a small mirror judges a face honestly, because a person sees themselves in fragments. The Marshal’s bench was raised three steps at the far end, and behind it hung a mirror larger than the others by a foot and a half, with a shutter and a key.

Nessia Colm was brought in at nine and sat the way people sit who have not slept. She had brought an unsigned wedding licence eleven months out of date, her own appearance certificate, and a tenancy schedule naming her father as surety. “I did not bring him up,” she said, before Ivet had asked. “He is downstairs. He is downstairs most days. He has not climbed these stairs in four months, and I have stopped asking, and I cannot keep on doing this and also get married.”

The Marshal, Cressida Dane, sixty-one, came in at nine twenty and did not sit until the advocate of the day had finished turning a page she had already turned. She wore the plain grey wig of her office, cut with a roll at the nape. She read the papers standing and set them down. “Your father is named as subject,” she said. “Where is he.”

“In the public gallery,” said Nessia.

“Then he is not downstairs, and we will bring him up properly. Advocate Serrat, you will sit today. The court would like a hearing at large.”

* * *

Beren Colm came up with his hat in his hands, a solid, unhurried man with the hands of somebody whose work requires them to be exact. For thirty-one years he had struck the drop on the public scales at the Weighhouse, and his palms were broad and pale and unbroken, and when Ivet asked his occupation he gave it in the voice of a man reciting what he had recited to inspectors twice before.

“Scalesmith,” he said. “Thirty-one years at the Weighhouse, eleven of them head of the beam. My daughter will tell you I am reliable, and she has told you, which is a different matter.”

He had not been recorded since the spring eleven years ago, when an infection had taken the left side of his jaw and the bridge of his nose in eleven weeks, and the court had amended his entry in a single sitting, and he had looked into mirror three to do it, and had looked a long time. His daughter had been nine, her lease required a surety, and that record had let her keep her rooms.

“You understand why I was recorded that year,” he said. “Nothing has happened since. That record is the best record in this city, and the one I looked at longest, and it is my wife’s face and my daughter’s father and a man who can stand surety, and I would like it left exactly as it stands. This court is asking me to carry it down a corridor and hold it beside what I am now.”

* * *

“And this refusal,” said the Marshal, from the bench. “It is eleven months old. Tell the court when it began.”

“The day after my wife was buried,” said Beren Colm. “The record was made in the year before we married. My wife read the entry when we took the tenancy, because she read everything, and she said the man in the record was the man she had chosen, and she said it to my face, and she was not being kind to me. She was reporting a finding. I have had eleven years of being the man she chose and four years of her not being in the room. I would rather be the record than the other thing. I would rather be a surety’s ghost in my daughter’s lease than a man in this room.”

The Marshal let that stand for the length of a breath, then asked whether he would consider a private viewing. He said he had asked for one in March. The Marshal said the court had no procedure for it. He said there was one in Part Four, unused in her time. The Marshal said, without any change of voice, that she was aware of Part Four.

“Will you hold me to the schedule?” he said.

“No,” said the Marshal. “I will hold you to the hearing, and you may not leave it unanswered.”

* * *

The court entered its own witness, which it had not done in nineteen years, and the clerk announced him as the standing Comparison, and there was a small movement in the advocates’ rows, because the Comparison was not so much a man as a unit. Silas Vant was thirty-four. His face had been entered in the roll of standards at nineteen, and no assessor had ever judged any part of it to have altered, and the court had examined him four mornings a week for fifteen years without once ruling, because nothing was left to rule on.

Ivet asked him to describe, for the record, what a standard is. He said a face is a set of ratios, and a ratio needs a fixed point to be measured from, and the city had none until a nineteen-year-old with an unremarkable face agreed to become one. “If I am permitted to change,” he said, “then every record in which a distance is expressed against me must be compared again, and there are forty-one thousand of them, and the comparing is not arithmetic. It is sitting down with forty-one thousand people and explaining that the measuring stick has become a stick that bends.”

“Then you are a measurement,” said Ivet. “You are not a witness to anybody’s appearance. You are the ruler it is measured against.”

“Yes,” he said. “I have wanted that said aloud in a chamber for eleven years and nobody has needed it said, because the court knows it, and being right about it is the entire service I perform.”

Ivet asked what the standard had cost him, and he considered it with the care of a man handed it before by a clerk. “My daughter’s school record is cross-referenced to my entry, because a pupil’s surety may not be a standard in dispute. She was asked at an assembly to describe me. She was eleven, and good at it, and she described the entry: the roll number, the date, the finding that no feature had altered. It was the most accurate account of my face ever given in that school, and she has not looked at me since when she talks about my face.”

“Thank you,” said Ivet. The standard said she was welcome, and then told the court he was dying, and that it had held his physician’s note since the previous Michaelmas, and that the roll had not acted on it because a face cannot be transferred while its holder is breathing, and no provision for that case had ever been drafted.

* * *

The Advocate General’s office, which was not the court but which paid for it, sent a settlement to Ivet’s table at eleven. It was generous, competent, and produced by people who had thought about her case for a quarter of an hour. The city would pay. An assessor would be sent to the Foundling Line. The subject would be attended by two advocates of the court and by the Weighhouse, which had no statutory function in the matter but would send somebody to sit with him.

“And if he refuses inside the ninety days?” said Ivet.

“Then the young woman is unmarried for as long as he lives,” said the settlement’s clerk, “and the case remains a proper case, and you and I will both be doing this in twenty years, in this room, with a new subject and the same schedule.”

Ivet carried the settlement to the petitioner’s table and watched him read it, expecting the asking for a day, the beginning of a calendar. “I will do it,” he said, when he had finished. “Inside the ninety days.”

“And when?”

He considered it, and the consideration was real, and it was the first thing in that file that was not a document. “On a day I do not know,” he said. “You may write that down. You may tell my daughter her father intends to be looked at, and that his intention is sound, and that I would be obliged if nobody asked me again this year.”

* * *

In the annexe, with the roll-keeper’s ledger open in front of her, Ivet Serrat found the count. The standing roll of discrepancies held eleven million and four thousand persons. The court had reconciled four million one hundred and six thousand. The remainder stood at six million nine hundred and ninety-eight thousand, and the city held thirty-one million souls, and the roll-keeper, a patient man of fifty with a bad wrist, observed that the court had reconciled a seventh of the city and considered the remainder to be the population.

“A record of appearance is a legal personhood,” he said, before she had asked, because she had the face of somebody about to ask. “It is an identity the way a baptism is one, the way a marriage is one. She cannot marry without one, for a surety. You cannot hold a wage account without one. You cannot be named in a will without one, which is a kindness, in my view. The court decides nothing about what a person may look like. The court decides what a person may be recorded as, and the rest of the city reads that record and acts on it, and they act on it correctly, because it is correct.”

Her own entry sat in the ledger at the top of a column of six. Ivet Serrat, entered at nineteen at her first certification, amended twice since, both amendments clerical. She had not been recorded in five years. She was on the standing roll, with a standing number and a date of entry that made her a person who could not hold a wage account, or be named in a will, or be married, and she had spent five years in this building noticing none of it, because the only institution in the city that had ever asked to see her face had asked when she was seventeen, and had assumed that whatever it saw in the glass would still be standing there in five years.

She went back in and asked the Marshal a question that was not about the Colm petition, and the Marshal let her ask it.

“Your entry,” said Ivet. “Entered at twenty-eight, on your appointment. Never reconciled. You are on the standing roll.”

“I am,” said the Marshal.

“In thirty-three years you have ordered reconciliations by the thousand and you have not once been recorded yourself.”

“No,” said the Marshal.

“Will you say why?”

“Because I have not had cause,” said the Marshal, “which is the procedural answer, and the only one available to me in this chamber. Notice that I have answered you accurately, and that you are not satisfied.”

“I am not satisfied,” said Ivet, “because the four million came to this court. Nobody was fetched for any of them. Every one walked in and sat down and was seen, and that is what the number is, Marshal. It is not four million people who were processed. It is four million people who agreed to be looked at.”

“The remainder is made of people who have not come,” said the Marshal, “and among them are people who cannot come, and you were about to say the rest of that sentence very effectively, so I will finish it. There are people who cannot come. Yes. I know their number. This court does not publish it, because the court would be reported as saying a category of its citizens does not exist, and I have refused to permit that for thirty-three years, and I understand the refusal has been useful to me.”

* * *

Then she let the chamber be quiet, and when she spoke again she stood at the advocates’ table rather than on the bench, which no Marshal had done in living memory, and she said: “I have heard this case as a father who will not be documented, and the hearing has established that it is not a documentation matter. This court decides nothing about what a person must look like. It decides what a person may be recorded as, and a record of appearance is a legal identity, so our refusals decide not on beauty but on who is able to act, and we have decided that eleven million times without once putting the two halves of the question in the same room.”

She said that this court was the only institution in the city that required a person to look at themselves once a year on pain of nothing whatsoever, and that the four million on the reconciled roll were four million people who were seen, and that a man’s refusal to be seen was not a documentation problem but the whole case, and that the court had been mishearing it for six hundred years.

Nobody moved. The standard laid one flat hand on the arm of his chair, and the advocates’ pens went on moving for a few seconds after the sentence had finished, and then stopped, one after another, as though they had understood the point.

The advocate said she was not certain the Marshal was right. She said the account was untestable, that it could be checked against nothing, and that a court reorganising itself on an untestable account would be doing what every department in this city did at the end of a long argument, which was to decide it had been right all along and be congratulated about it. The Marshal agreed with all of it, and said the provision she meant to use had never been tested either, and that a court was not built to be right, it was built to be a place where being wrong was expensive and visible.

* * *

“Advocate Serrat,” said the Marshal. “You have prepared a motion. Do you move that the subject be compelled.”

“I do not,” said Ivet Serrat.

“You have prepared it.”

“I did. I withdrew it at the beginning of the fourth hour and did not tell the court, because I wished to see whether the court would notice.”

“Then there is no motion, the Colm petition is withdrawn by its own advocate, and no judgment will issue, because there is nothing before the court to judge. Does that satisfy you.”

“It satisfies the schedule,” said Ivet. “It does not satisfy the young woman.”

“Nothing in this building has ever satisfied the people upstairs. That has never been the office. Sit down, Advocate. I am going to rule on something older than both of us, and I am going to rule on it in the open, and you will want to write down where I found it, because in four years one of my successors will not believe I found it.”

* * *

The Marshal read the words herself, in the flat delivery of a person who has read a great many clauses, from Schedule Nine, Part Four, paragraph eleven, which permitted a subject to elect a private record of appearance, made annually, sealed, and unviewable by the court, by the city, by any employer, and by the subject. It carried no penalty and no schedule of inspection, and the roll-keeper explained that this was because the clause was drafted in a century when the court was considered a curiosity, and the only thing to be feared from it was curiosity.

She made the seal, and the first year of the new rule was a single page: four hundred and nine names in a hand nobody could read, entered by a clerk who did not ask, set into the roll where anybody could confirm that it existed and nobody could learn a thing from it. The Marshal was the first name on it, the standard the second, and the advocate the third, not having been asked, and not being asked now.

At half past six the clerks left, then the advocates, then the standard, who said at the threshold that he would not surrender the entry until the roll was abolished. She said she had noted the date of his physician’s note and would not have to look it up.

The chamber emptied the way chambers empty, which is not all at once. The Marshal stayed. She had done this for thirty-three years, and the practice had a name, and the name was hours.

She went up the three steps and stood before the Court’s own mirror, larger than the others by a foot and a half, with a shutter and a key, which the advocates of her generation had not known her to use. She took the key from the board beneath the bench, unlocked the shutter, and the last of the daylight came off the panelling and lay across the glass.

Then she took off the wig, which took some doing, because the pin at the nape had been set for thirty-three years by her own hands and had closed a little over them. She worked it loose with one forefinger and set the wig on the bench beside the seal, and turned to the glass, and looked at herself, which she had done at intervals of a year since she was twenty-eight, and which was on the roll, and which nobody had ever done for her.
