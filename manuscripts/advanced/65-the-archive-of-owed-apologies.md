# The Archive of Owed Apologies

![Cover art: The Archive of Owed Apologies](../art/advanced/65-the-archive-of-owed-apologies.webp)

The Registry of Acknowledgments opened on the first working day of each month in a long room with green-shaded lamps and a floor of grey tile worn into shallow dishes by four decades of standing clerks, and Wren Okonjo had been its chief clerk for nineteen of them.

The Hall held eleven million, four hundred and six thousand entries on eleven floors of open shelving, and every entry consisted of a number, a date, a name, and an amount. Those four were considered sufficient by everyone who had governed the place. Wren had a bad knee and an aunt who had got her the work by saying it was clean.

The machine ran on Form 1, which had not been redesigned since 1884 because Form 1 was the founding instrument and the charter forbade the alteration of any instrument that had been executed. A person who had done wrong came to a desk and acknowledged the fact in writing, and the schedule existed so that the acknowledged wrong could decay.

The interval was statutory. An acknowledgement had to be entered within ninety days of the act, and the act then decayed across seven years on a curve the clerks read off the wall. A wrong that decayed became a closed file. A wrong that did not stayed open, and an open file accrued maintenance daily, and the maintenance was the amount.

By the fourteenth the Hall took its figures from a board of thirty-one rotating drums along the north wall, one for each day of the month, each divided into the hours in which an acknowledgement could lawfully be entered. In Wren’s first full year the intake desk called out forty-four thousand one hundred and twelve entries. Last year, twenty-nine thousand three hundred and thirty-eight.

“A third down in four years,” her deputy said. Wren’s deputy was Aurelia Fenn, who had a way of putting a number in front of you and standing back. “A third in a generation is not a drift, chief. A third is a decision. Things do not lose a third of themselves unless something has been decided about them.”

“By whom,” Wren said, without looking up, because the answer was always a filing decision and she had spent nineteen years learning to make those before anybody asked. “You may as well say the Hall has lost its grip on the public. The public has simply stopped bringing us anything to acknowledge.”

“Or has stopped bringing it because it is not worth the walk,” Fenn said. Wren wrote 29,338 in the margin of her ledger, and the honest answer was that she had been making the same entry for four years and the number was not a mystery. It was a complaint.

* * *

The young man who came to the second-floor intake desk on the ninth of the month was twenty-two, thin through the jaw, and wearing the grey coat the Hall issued to anyone whose file exceeded three hundred entries. He set his papers on the counter in the order the instructions told him to, which Wren had never once seen a person do.

He had been convicted at nineteen of striking a tollkeeper named Baul at the Murrow Bridge tollhouse and breaking two bones in the man’s writing hand, and he had entered an acknowledgement every quarter since his release, and the number on his file was one thousand.

A thousand.

Wren looked at the nine folders the intake clerk had laid out for her, and then at the young man. “You understand that a thousand is not a figure anybody has ever reached.”

“I understand,” he said. “The notices said a thousand, and then a review.”

“The notices are issued automatically at the nine hundred and fiftieth. The review belongs to a different department.”

“The notices are the only thing anybody has ever said to me about this,” Ivo Sarko said. “I am not complaining, chief. Registering is my whole week. My employer sends me, and I come, and I pay the one mark, and I do not touch the rail on the way in.”

The intake clerk read the particulars back in the flat trained voice that the Hall put into nine hundred people. “Ivo Sarko, aged twenty-two, of Number Four Tannery Row, in respect of the striking of Baul on the fourteenth of Ash, third year past. Acknowledgement entered, number one thousand. The fee is one mark. Thank you.” And then he said, “Does anybody read them?”

The Hall was never silent, because the Hall could not afford to be, but the desk went quiet for the length of one drum, and Wren made him say it again.

“Does anybody read them,” he said. “Not the file. I know the file is read. The words. Somebody has to decide that I meant it, and I cannot see how anybody decides that without reading, and so I wanted to ask whether the reading is a thing that happens.”

The intake clerk, who was twenty-four and named Osric and had been at the Hall two years, said, “Section nine. Full text is not required to be read in the ordinary course.”

“Section nine of the 1907 amendment,” Wren said. “Full text is not required to be read. Nothing is required to be read. Thank you, Mr Sarko. Your file is updated.” The mark went into the box and Ivo Sarko went down the stairs past the queue, and Wren thought about the fact that she had told the truth.

* * *

Wren went down to the reading room on the ninth floor, the only room in the Hall with a lock. The acknowledged words of the city lay there in forty-one bound volumes from 1884, and then in loose batches, and then in summary form, and after 1971 in nothing that required a person to look at. Section nine had been drafted in 1907 by a committee that believed the words were sentiment, and rather than repeal the reading requirement they wrote exceptions.

And there was the sentence the whole building stood on, printed on the first leaf of the wall chart, taught on a clerk’s first day and examined on a clerk’s first year, above the seven-year curve and below the hours of the day. THE ACKNOWLEDGMENT IS ENTERED IN GOOD FAITH.

Wren had been looking at that sentence since she was thirty-two, and she had never found the sentence that came after it, because there is no sentence after it, because it is the entirety of the machine’s opinion of the world. The good in the city is computed from three facts: that a page was entered, that it was inside the ninety days, and how many pages there were.

Every clerk in the Hall understood the consequence of that sentence, and not one had ever said it in a room with a colleague in it, because saying it would have been the beginning of a proposal. The good in the city is published quarterly as the Decay Remission, and the Remission sets the Hall’s grant, and the grant sets the clerks.

Fenn came in without knocking, as she did, and set a quarter’s return on the table. “Remission, ninety-one point four,” she said. “Highest in the series. Intake down thirty-three per cent over four years. Headcount up nine.”

“They would not believe it,” Wren said. “They do not have to believe it,” Fenn said. “They have to sign it. You taught me that.”

* * *

The oldest bay in the building was Bay One, on the ground floor, where the shelves were oak and the air was held at sixty per cent by an apparatus that made a sound like a man clearing his throat. Wren went down with a conservation lamp, intending ten minutes, because a junior clerk had reported an illegible annotation in the first volume and she had signed it up herself.

The volume was a hundred and forty years old and had been rebound twice. The first entry was dated the fourteenth of February, 1884, and it was the entry the building was named for in every document it possessed. Wren read it twice, and then sat down on the low stool in the aisle.

The oldest entry in the collection was not an apology.

It was a registration made in 1884 by a woman who had been owed an apology and had come to the counter to register that she were owed one. Her name was Nell Ferrar, of Tannery Ward, and the first form the Hall had used was headed ACKNOWLEDGMENT OF FAULT OR OF DEBT, with a single column and two lines, for the party presenting and the party in respect of whom it was presented. The column did not distinguish between the party who owed an acknowledgement and the party who was owed it, and a woman who came in to say the city owed her an apology was recorded as owing one.

Nell Ferrar had stood as a debtor for a hundred and forty years. She had come in because the ward had been cleared for a sewer in 1881, eleven families put out in one night without a hearing, and the assayer who signed the order had been dismissed rather than acknowledge anything. She died in 1911, and her entry was never closed, because no procedure exists for closing an entry nobody has paid.

Wren did the arithmetic, because she was a clerk and it was in front of her. The principal was one acknowledgement, and the amount now stood at three million, nine hundred and six thousand, one hundred and forty-one marks and four sestertii, and it was the largest single item in the collection, and it was not money, because nobody had ever owed her money. It was the cost of keeping a record of a thing the city had not done, charged daily by clerks who had never met her.

Nobody’s fault, which was what made it hard to put down. No clerk had lied and no clerk had invented the debt. The form was ambiguous, the clerk on duty did the arithmetic the form allowed, and every chief clerk since had inherited a correct record of a thing that had never happened. Nobody could close it, because closing it would mean voiding four hundred and eleven thousand entries.

Wren wrote on the conservation desk’s slip, in pencil, because she had not yet earned anything better: a debt cannot be closed by the debtor and cannot be paid by the creditor and has been accruing for a hundred and forty years because the ledger was correct on the first morning and has been correct every morning since.

* * *

The Provost’s audit came on the twenty-ninth, and the Provost of the Bureau of Acknowledgments was Hilda Rask, who had held the post six years and had never made a decision she could not explain at a gate. She came with a schedule and sat in Wren’s office with her hat on her knee.

“Forty per cent of the quarter’s entries were filed by proxy,” Rask said. “Proxies are lawful since 1931. A person may be represented. There is nothing improper in it.”

“There is nothing improper in it,” Wren agreed. “There is a great deal in it, in arithmetic.” The tram authority had registered one thousand four hundred and two acknowledgements, one for each occasion on which a tram had stopped short and a passenger had fallen, and its counsel had settled on the reasonable ground that a body which harms eleven thousand people owes eleven thousand apologies.

The fourth proxy was the largest, and the oldest, and the one that made Fenn put her pen down.

“On the ninth of May 1968,” Rask said, “the Office of Wards and Assizes entered a single acknowledgement in respect of the evictions of the Tannery Ward. Section forty-one, mass entry. Two hundred and six open files closed in one morning.”

“They were the Tannery Ward files,” Wren said.

“They were,” Rask said. “The Council apologised. The apology was entered in good faith, as the form requires, and two hundred and six wrongs decayed on the seven-year curve, and the ward was not cleared again for forty years, which nobody in that office would call a bad outcome.”

Wren looked at the schedule. “Who read that acknowledgement.”

“Section forty-one does not require reading.”

“Who read it.”

“The Provost of the Council read it,” Rask said. “It is in the minutes. It is eleven hundred words. It names the eleven families. It says the Council was wrong. He signed it himself, and the Council has never disputed it.”

“Then it worked,” Wren said. “It worked,” Rask said. “It is the single most effective instrument in the collection.” And there it was, in good black ink on the ninety-first page of the quarterly return: a hundred and forty years of careful administration had taught one city exactly one thing, which was that saying the words is what closes the file. Not meaning them. Saying them.

Wren said, “The Remission is ninety-one point four per cent. It is a record. It is a record achieved while intake fell by a third, and those are the same fact.”

Rask frowned. “I do not follow.”

“They have not stopped apologising because they have stopped feeling sorry,” Wren said. “They have stopped apologising because they have noticed that nothing happens if they do not. And the reason nothing happens is not mercy, and it is not amnesty. It is that the machine must assume the apology is sincere, because nothing measures it.”

Fenn said, from the doorway, “The pages.” “The pages,” Wren said. “It is a good machine. It has decayed more wrongdoing than any court in this city and it has never once been wrong about a document. It has been quietly paying us by weight.” Rask said she would want the return to state why intake had fallen by a third.

* * *

The Hall tried the obvious things for nine days. Fenn proposed raising the fee from one mark to four, on the reasoning that a person who has paid four marks has been made to want the entry to be true, and the Committee rejected that on the ground that the fee measures means, not remorse.

And then Ivo Sarko came back for his thousand and first, and she was at the counter herself, which she had not been in eleven years. He had the same coat, the same upright hand, the same order of papers, and when she came to the number she stopped.

“One thousand and one,” she said. “One thousand and one.”

“The notices will not be issued again. The file is out of the ordinary series now. You are the only person in the city in that position and I have never seen the position before.”

“Then there is no review.”

“There is no review,” Wren said. “The file stays open, the maintenance accrues, the amount goes up every morning, and you will come here every quarter because you have always come here every quarter, and I have no power to tell you to stop.”

“Does anybody read them,” Ivo Sarko said. “That was my question. You never answered it, chief, because I asked it in the wrong words.”

“I answered it in the words the Act has.”

“Yes,” he said. “That is what I have been thinking about for four months. It is not that anybody has stopped reading what I write. Nobody ever read what I write. But I can tell that it made a difference, and I want to know why I have been paying a mark a quarter for forty months in order to make a difference nobody can check.”

“Because the schedule requires an entry each quarter,” Wren said, and heard herself, and stopped. “Yes,” he said. “Because the schedule requires it. Chief, is the schedule the reason, or is the schedule the reason we have got?”

She signed his entry, and he went down the stairs, and she understood that the two thousand and six files of the ward clearances of 1881 were still open upstairs, and that they were open because a clerk had been handed a form with one column instead of two.

* * *

On the sixth of the month Wren Okonjo filed a report of eleven pages with the Bureau of Acknowledgments, and the eleven pages recommended that the Archive be closed.

The recommendation occupied one page, on the fourth, and read: That the Registry of Acknowledgments be wound up; that the schedule of maintenance be suspended on all open files; that the entries be transferred to the Public Record without further accrual; and that the collection thereafter be maintained as a historical matter.

The other seven pages were the working. She set out the founding assumption and the schedule of exceptions and the 1907 amendment and paragraph 41 of 1968 and the volume series with its four-year fall, and she set out, in a table, the fact that had undone her: the curve was a function of the entry, the interval, and the count, and not of the person.

The fifth page was the one that made the report hard to sign, and she wrote it last, in nine lines, and left it as it stood. It said that the Registry had been founded in 1884 to convert remorse into a schedulable quantity, and that the conversion had a condition never written into any instrument, which was that the remorse be real, and that no reading could test it.

On the sixth page, in three lines, she entered the Bay One reference, and stated only that the oldest entry in the collection was made under an ambiguous form, and that the ambiguity cannot be corrected without voiding four hundred and eleven thousand entries, and that no living person is a party to it, and recommended no action, because there was no action.

She signed it and took it down to the intake desk at half past four, because the Act requires that a report of the Chief Clerk be entered in the collection on the day it is made. It does not say under which heading, because the Act was drafted in 1884 and there was one heading.

“Section one,” Osric said, and he had Form 1 in front of him, because that is what is in front of a clerk at the end of the day. “An acknowledgement presented. Acknowledgment of fault or of debt, by whom, and in respect of whom.” And Wren listened to a young man read her report back to her in the flat trained voice of the Hall, and heard the thing she had known would happen, and had written the report anyway.

“By the Chief Clerk of this Registry,” Osric said, “in respect of the Registry.”

Wren said, “That is correct.” “Yes,” Osric said, and looked up, and she saw that he had understood it, and that he was twenty-four and had not yet learned the trick of not understanding things. “Do you want the number spoken. It is customary.”

“Enter the next number,” Wren said. “That is the next number.” So the clerk wrote eleven million, four hundred and six thousand and one, and the folder that came off the press that evening was grey card, and the amount entered against the Registry itself was one acknowledgement, unsatisfied.

The last warden of the day was a man named Tam, who had carried folders for twenty-six years and who took them up without reading them, as his office required. He pressed seven, and the folder went into Bay Twelve, in the aisle where the collection keeps the entries that have no acquittal, and it was the only one there whose amount was a single mark.

On the ground floor the humidity apparatus cleared its throat, and in Bay One, in the aisle at the left-hand end, a folder that had been standing since 1884 stood open at its first page, where a Form 1 of the first printing carries one column, and the entry is in a brown upright hand gone soft along the folds, and the amount is in the same brown ink, and it is three million, nine hundred and six thousand, one hundred and forty-one marks and four sestertii, and the woman who wrote it died in the ward cottage in 1911, and it has not been settled.
