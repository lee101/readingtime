# The Clockwork Cathedral of Small Beginnings

![Cover art: The Clockwork Cathedral of Small Beginnings](../art/advanced/13-the-clockwork-cathedral-of-small-beginnings.webp)

On the morning the bells forgot how to end, Lio found a brass automaton speaking in his own voice.

It perched on the highest ledge of the cathedral tower, wings folded against the rain while the carillons below rang backward for seven minutes. One clunk, then a pause, then two, as if the city were trying to remember a sound it had lost.

Lio dropped his oilcan, which rolled across the slate and stopped at the edge. Below him, every civic clock had stopped at seventeen minutes past five, leaving shopkeepers in their doorways and a baker holding bread in midair, while beyond the square a cart wheel gave one slow creak.

The automaton clicked again, saying, “Find the missing tooth,” as the tower bell sounded once so hard that rain shook from the eaves.

Master Tavi found him three minutes later, climbing with a lantern in one hand and an unfamiliar key in the other. His silver hair was flat with rain, and his right sleeve was pinned above a hand that had not moved since Lio was six.

“Do not touch the bird,” Tavi said, as Lio raised a hand toward its copper feathers. Lio replied, “It touched me first,” while the bird tilted its head, and Tavi looked up, his expression theatrical despite the danger. “Gears are not birds,” he added, and then declared, “Then it is a very bad bird.”

They descended through a trapdoor beneath the bell rope, where the air smelled of oil, wet stone, and hot copper. The Great Wheel filled the round chamber with thirteen brass circles surrounding a black wooden axle, each lined with fine teeth, while a slender current of blue light ran through the axle and vanished into a sealed door.

For three years, Lio had mastered polishing, counting, and managing those wheels, but he had never seen the blue river move. Tavi set down the lantern and placed his metal hand near the empty socket, its surface warm despite the underground damp.

“The thirteenth tooth is gone,” Tavi said, and when Lio asked whether they could make another, Tavi answered, “Not from brass.”

The word *not* fell heavily between them, and above, the backward bells resumed their broken count. Lio heard seven clunks vanish into the rain.

“Does the Wheel do that?” he asked.

“It remembers,” Tavi said, touching a ring beside the empty socket. The cathedral was built as a covenant, allowing a person to return to the instant before the decision that made their life turn, for one attempt with recollection intact, so returning would not mean escaping.

When Lio asked who had made those choices, Tavi did not answer. The silence acquired the weight of a confession he could not yet speak.

The great door opened, admitting Councilor Marrow and two wardens in blue coats. Marrow was broad and calm, a man who made furniture appear smaller, and his eyes moved from the empty socket to Lio.

“Master Tavi,” Marrow said, “I expected you to understand that some machines are better left quiet.”

Tavi replied that the Wheel had stopped. Marrow blamed a city that had forgotten how to choose, promising to repair the west gates by noon because the river would reach the lower market in three days and the council had to decide by morning.

Lio watched the blue river pulse and said, “The Wheel can return the delegates before they read the report.”

“After you restore it, it can return anyone,” Marrow answered.

He moved closer, the wardens silent, and told Lio to bring the missing tooth from the lower archive by nightfall. “Your master may be old enough to forget what he knows, but you are not.”

He left, and Tavi waited until the heavy door closed. He explained that Marrow wanted the first minute: the one before every other choice, which the founders had believed a city could use wisely.

The first thing they found was a rubbing of the tooth, hidden in the tool cupboard before Marrow arrived. Sera Venn, a junior mapmaker, had slipped it there while looking for a chart of the old water channels, and she was twelve, with ink on one cheek and a roll of vellum under her arm.

“From the archive,” she whispered, spreading the rubbing across the floor. A tooth had been pressed into the paper so hard that it tore, and she added, “My mother made this while she worked in the Quiet Hands office.”

When Tavi asked about the office, Sera met his eyes and said, “The office that corrected decisions. You knew.”

Beneath the tooth, the vellum carried a list of places and dates—Eastgate School, spring lottery; Ferry Landing, last winter; Lantern Ward, sixth bell. The hand trembled at the ends, suggesting that the writer had understood the danger and written anyway, before Lio asked what it was and Sera called it the records of the first minute.

They found the tooth in the lower archive, inside a box labeled with thirteen blank circles. It rested on a cushion of black cloth, small and warm, beside an order from Marrow: *Do not return it to the Wheel. Some beginnings are too useful to be repeated.*

“He knew exactly where it was,” Sera said, picking up the order, and Tavi replied, “He put it there. He wanted us to find it.”

Only then did Lio notice the scratches inside the box, thin and impatient rather than neat like a locksmith's cuts. Someone had handled the box every day and resented every mark.

When Lio lifted the tooth, blue light brightened between the rings, and the chamber filled with images that appeared in the spaces between the gears like palm-sized mirrors. A woman moved a red bead in a school lottery, a man carried a message to a ferry landing an hour late, and a clerk slid a warning beneath tax papers. In another scene, a child opened a gate to admit a stranger, and neighbors quietly paid for the stranger's food.

The scenes offered no judgment, only hands and consequences, as if the mechanism had learned to distinguish intention from effect without pretending either was simple.

Sera leaned toward the school image and said, “That was forty years ago.”

“My grandmother taught here,” Tavi said, and he added that she won a place for her brother.


Another image appeared: a storm-warning bell removed from its bracket and hung in an orchard where no one would hear it. Next, a hand set a cup of water beside a sleeping child, who woke, found it, and left without waking the person who had placed it there.

Lio felt as if the cathedral had opened many subtle doors at once, each hiding a different version of responsibility. When Tavi explained that every choice leaves a path and changing a path at its beginning can send someone toward a different end without revealing that the road itself changed, Lio understood why the histories felt dangerous.

Marrow appeared beside the empty socket, his arrival silent, and declared, “That is why no one may use the Wheel.” When Sera accused him of wanting it restored, he explained that a machine permitting another attempt also permitted patient arrangement: the rich moved a bead, the powerful moved a warning, and the lonely received a cup, so all it took was a small beginning.

He gestured at the images and said Bellwether survived because those beginnings were directed. Floods were prevented, wars delayed, children fed, and the council made the city possible, though Sera called it lying, Marrow called it arranging what could be arranged, and Lio answered, “By taking choices away.”

Marrow's eyes sharpened as he said, “You are eleven,” but Lio answered that he was old enough to know a lock is not a kindness. Tavi placed a hand on Lio's shoulder, not to silence him but to steady himself, and admitted, “You are right. I was guided too.”

The Wheel turned a fraction on its own, and a new image formed: Tavi at seventeen, standing at a harbor with a small bag over his shoulder. A ship waited beyond the pier, a cartographer handed him a message, and Tavi read it, looked toward the sea, then turned back toward the city.

Lio watched the scene repeat as the cartographer handed him the same message and Tavi made the same turn.

“The map was wrong,” Tavi said, explaining that the Blue Reach road was open and that he had discovered the truth three days later. When Lio pointed out that he had stayed, Tavi said someone had told him his mother needed him, although she did not, adding, “I was useful, which was enough for a frightened boy,” as his metal hand shook against his coat, followed by, “A falsified map made me afraid to leave, so I became a watchmaker and called fear loyalty.”

Marrow smiled without warmth and said, “Now you understand the value of direction,” but Tavi answered, “I understand theft.” Marrow replied, “Everything is theft if you count the beginning.”

The blue light flared, and the thirteenth tooth began turning in its cushion though no one touched it. When Marrow moved toward it, Sera slammed the archive lid hard enough to stop the movement.

“At dawn tomorrow,” she said, “the council votes on the west floodgates. You were going to put the delegates into the Wheel and return them to before they read the report.”

Marrow did not deny it, describing a clean room with no accusations or frightened rumors, only the map, the river, and the choice.

Lio stepped between him and the box and accused him of already knowing what choice he wanted the delegates to make.

Marrow closed his hand around a small iron key at his belt and said, “A city is not a child, apprentice. It cannot afford to start over whenever it dislikes the result.”

Behind him, the wardens drew their keys of office. Tavi moved between them and the wheel as Lio told Marrow to start with himself and return to the moment before he took the tooth.

Marrow stared at him and said, “The Wheel cannot make a person good,” but Lio answered, “It doesn't have to; it only gives you another chance to be responsible.” When Marrow asked whether he could return without knowing the cost, Lio said, “You've always known.”

The blue light flickered, and somewhere below the bells began counting forward. Lio placed the tooth into the empty socket.

The Wheel woke with the sound of a held breath released. Rain ran upward along the walls, the images around the rings blurred and sharpened, and Marrow lunged for the axle before Tavi caught his metal hand. The chamber filled with the city hearing itself at once as Sera pulled the councilor's iron key free and opened the lower door.

They found Marrow on the floor beside the archive, clutching the black box. Nothing in the room had changed, but the tooth was back in his hand while Tavi and Sera stood at the door exactly where they had been. Marrow opened his eyes and declared, “You cannot keep me from my own beginning.”

When Marrow reached toward the archive, Lio pulled the door shut between them, and one of the wardens lowered his key.

“He already returned once,” Lio said, and when he added that Marrow had chosen the same thing, no one moved. Marrow hammered the door and declared, “You think one night makes you clever,” before Sera asked what they should do now.

Lio thought of the harbor, the ship, and the salt smell he had never seen. He thought of his small beginning, the morning he had chosen to stay and learn the Wheel because Tavi promised him a place in the workshop, and although it had been a choice, circumstance had shaped its edges and a lie had waited nearby.

He took the silver thread from his belt, the apprentice's mark tied when he had been accepted. Tavi had given it to him, and Lio had worn it every day without asking what it meant.

When Sera asked if it could work, Tavi said, “It is a beginning,” and Lio pressed the thread into the blue river. Instead of a sound, a small image opened above the floor, showing Lio at seventeen, boarding the ship, one hand resting on the rail, and for one breath he could imagine leaving.

Then the image faded, and Lio said, “No; I choose this,” stepping back and leaving the thread in the river, burning like a tiny star. Marrow's hammering stopped, and he whispered that he was wasting the only mercy the Wheel offers, but Lio answered, “The Wheel is not mercy. It is a chance to be responsible.”

The wardens opened the archive door without seizing Marrow, reading the orders in his box, the altered reports, and the dates stamped on the thirteen teeth. Sera gave them the cartographer's records, while Tavi confessed the harbor, the falsified map, and the years he had mistaken manipulation for duty.

By dawn, the council had gathered in the nave beneath the slowly turning Wheel, whose thirteen teeth displayed the west gate, the school lottery, the ferry landing, the warning bell, and beginnings. A clerk wrote each one on a public slate, and every request to return a person had to be spoken before the whole assembly with its reason and cost named aloud.

Marrow sat beneath the arch with his wardens beside him. The floodwater was still coming, but the delegates could see the river's height on maps as they debated the west gate, the eastern gardens, and the lower market, with some arguing for opening the gate, others for waiting, and no one hiding behind a bell or a moved bead.

At sunrise, the western delegates voted to open the floodgates, the garden ward voted to hold the eastern wall, and the market ward sent volunteers to carry supplies uphill. The decision belonged to all of them, and its cost was written beside it.

Lio stood beneath the Wheel until the last vote was counted, then Tavi handed him the harbor pass and said, “Your ship leaves at noon.”

Lio took the harbor pass and asked whether Tavi would come, but his master said, “I have a Wheel to maintain.” When Lio asked about after he sailed, Tavi looked at the blue river in the axle and answered, “It will still be here, and so will the responsibility of using it well.”

Lio climbed the tower at dawn, where the brass bird waited on the highest ledge with its feathers washed clean. “What now?” he asked, and the bird tilted its head before answering, “The road is longer when no one owns it.”

Below him, the bells rang forward at last, their voices crossing the city one clear note after another. Lio packed his small bag, stepped through the cathedral doors, and went to meet the ship, while behind him the thirteenth tooth turned in the open light, recording no secret and asking no one to forget.
