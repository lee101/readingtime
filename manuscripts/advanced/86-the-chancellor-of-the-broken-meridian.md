# The Chancellor of the Broken Meridian

![Cover art: The Chancellor of the Broken Meridian](../art/advanced/86-the-chancellor-of-the-broken-meridian.webp)

On the morning the Meridian was finally made straight, Chancellor Aurel Vessel read the two provinces’ grievances before she drank anything, because the grievances arrived at dawn and the tea arrived later, and the empire had survived nine centuries by getting that order right. The grievances that morning concerned a millrace, a widow’s stove, and a dog shot in two provinces on the same afternoon, which had happened before.

Her clerk set the survey down with both hands, the way people set down something that has cost them more than money. It was a board of seasoned oak sheathed in brass at the corners, a chained survey nine years old at the joinery and still square.

“Chord of the Broken Meridian,” said Surveyor Ilsa Berrit, thirty-four, standing very straight. “It closes within nine millimetres across the whole length. I would like it read in the correct order, because it is a document, and documents are read in the order they were written.”

Vessel turned the board so the chord lay before her and did not lift the chain, because she had read the eleven reports that preceded it and knew what she was looking at. It was a chord, struck from the datum stone at the abbey of Stennick to the sea-cliff at Ord. It was straight, and it was correct, and every sentence of its argument was true, including the sentences that would not save it.

“Nine millimetres,” Vessel said. “In nine hundred years the Line has never held to a hand’s breadth anywhere in the Lower Provinces.”

“It has never had a chain,” Berrit said. “It has had a rope, a boy, and a great deal of good weather. Weather is not a boundary. It is a rumour with rain in it.”

Vessel read the inventory of instruments, the tolerances allowed by the Board of Weights, and the schedule for the removal of four hundred and six pegs. She read at the speed of a woman who could hear a false sentence arrive before the page was turned, and in twenty minutes she found the whole case, honestly founded, competently argued, and wrong in the one respect that mattered.

“Objections may be lodged for sixty days,” she said. “I will walk the Line on the first of the month. Choose the place where the Line has the worst of your reasons, be there at dawn with your instruments, and I will bring a clerk and no opinions.”

“Chancellor,” Berrit said, “I have no reasons. That is the difficulty. If I had reasons, I would not be asking you to walk it.”

* * *

Hallow Bridge Street is one street with two customs houses on it, one at the river end and one at the mill end, and it has been that since before either province admitted the other existed. Vessel walked it at dawn, and by the time the light came off the water there were eleven people waiting to explain to her why the arrangement worked.

The first was a chandler, whose salt came by the eastern road and whose glass came by the western, and who paid for the road he never used.

“I use the one my wife’s cousin delivers on,” the chandler said, “because the other bridge shuts at dusk, and I am not loading glass in the dark and blaming the border.”

Vessel asked what the crookedness cost him. A hundred and eleven marks a year. He had priced the alternative: a sealed customs house, a licence, a bond, a warehouse, a bonded clerk. “I am not against the Line,” he said. “The Line is the reason my glass arrives wet. But if it comes straight, the road I use becomes the road I pay for, and I am ruined, and I am ruined correctly.”

The second was a schoolmaster from the interval, thirty-six, holding the register of forty children. The hall stood where the Line doubled back on itself, so that no province could tax a school it had not admitted was in the building. Thirty-one of the forty had parents of one province and grandparents of the other.

“A child of the interval is apprenticed at fourteen or not at all,” he said. “That is the only reason I have nine apprentices instead of none. If your line comes through this hall it will come through straight, and a straight line is made of decisions, and decisions are made out of the people who are not there to object.”

The third was a woman with a basket and a goose, waiting to cross. The Line ran down the middle of that street because in 1744 it had been pegged that way, and she lived on the west side and her mother was buried on the east, and each crossing cost her a penny and a quarter of an hour. She said she would like, once in her life, to be permitted to stand in one place without deciding which province she was standing in.

Vessel wrote it all down, because that was the work. A chancellor’s hands are cold instruments and you keep them warm. She wrote for six hours on a bench at the mill end, and at the end of it she had a document that said: the customs regime on Hallow Bridge Street is not an abuse. It is a two-regime structure with no corruption in it, no fraud, and no dishonest officer anywhere in it, and every person caught inside it is worse off than one honest line would make them.

That was the whole of her opposition, and it was a method, and she wrote its name in her own hand at the foot of the page. The line was not the question. The reasons were the question.

* * *

Orm Ferrow was sixty-one and farmed eleven acres above the Stennick water, where the Line came down the hill in a loop the old survey sheets called the Sorrow and the villagers called nothing at all, because to name it would be a claim.

His house stood on the eastern side of the loop. His wife’s grave stood on the western side, eleven paces away, in a churchyard belonging to the other province. He had walked to it every Sunday for thirty-one years and paid a penny a year for the right to stand in the churchyard, which was the arrangement, which everybody had made, which had a name: a walking fee.

He was waiting at the gate with a spade in one hand and a folded licence in the other, and he did not bow, because his wife had taught him that bowing to officers is a habit that outlives the need for it and does more damage than the rudeness ever did.

“You will want the deed,” he said. “The deed says the house. The deed does not say the woman. She is in the western book and I am in the eastern book, and every Sunday I buy a penny of western ground in order to stand on her, and the penny is not a tax, it is a licence, and a licence can be refused, and it was refused once, in the year of the flood.”

Vessel read the document. It was a licence for standing, issued in 1794, amended in 1841 to require the ground to be dry, and the clause about the ground being dry was the reason the whole empire admired this valley.

“A parish clerk,” Orm Ferrow said. “Oduya Fen. He added it because the ground had been wet.”

“Was it wet?”

“It was wet for a fortnight. I buried a dog in it.”

She asked how long he had walked to the churchyard. Thirty-one years. She asked what would happen if the Line came through straight, and he took a long time to answer, not because he was thinking but because he had answered it before, to a tithe inspector, to a bishop, to a man from the Salt Roads, and it had never improved.

“Then she is on the wrong side and I am on the right,” he said, “and between her and me there is a customs line, and a customs line between a man and his wife’s grave is a thing that can be done, because it has a form, and the form has a name, and the name is an entry of goods, and I would be entered as importing a man into the western province, and the man would be me, and the import would be a Sunday.”

“Send me the licence,” Vessel said. “And bring the amendment. I would like to see what a clerk wrote about a wet fortnight.”

* * *

The camp at Ord was nine tents and a shed, and the shed stood on a concrete plinth, because instruments are not permitted to stand on grass, and the surveyors had poured it themselves in the fourth year out of a local conviction that it was wasteful.

Ilsa Berrit had been awake since three. She had eleven months left of the term and no intention of spending them being careful in a way that added nothing, so she had spent them being careful in a way that added everything. The tent at dawn was the cleanest place in the eastern province: plates wiped, screws in their cloth rolls, records under a weighted board.

Vessel arrived at seven and found the surveyor’s hands, which were the argument. Two nails short, a cracked nail bed, a thumb with the second joint locked for good from a screw-jack, a small burn on the wrist from a reading lamp. The chancellor’s hands were worse.

“You have held a line for thirty years,” Berrit said. “I can see that you have argued with paper.”

“Paper wins every argument once,” Vessel said. “Give me a fortnight with a document and no responsibility and I will sign it, and sign it well.”

Berrit laughed, put her hand flat on the plinth, and did not touch the instrument, because she had asked that nobody touch the instrument in her presence since the third year, when a deputy had rested his hat on it.

“Four months ago the western commission sent us their chain and asked for a comparison,” Berrit said. “Eleven millimetres at Ord. We sent back four pages and no opinion, because the report was true and the opinion would have been theirs.”

“Four pages,” Vessel said. “You had an opinion.”

“One page of my own, in the box, with the rest of the paper,” Berrit said. “Read it if you swear nothing, and if you swear nothing I will tell you what it is.”

Vessel swore nothing. There was a form for it, and the form was used so rarely that the ink still had its shine, and the clerk had to fetch the bottle from the shelf.

The opinion was a single sentence, and Berrit read it out in the shed with the door open, so that the wind could hear it too.

“I have a good chain, a good glass, a good datum, and a good correction for the curvature of the earth, and I can hold this line to nine millimetres for two hundred miles, and I do not know one single reason why it should be straight.”

Vessel asked her what she thought the reason was. Berrit said she had eleven thousand pages of the Line’s own history in the western archive, and that the Line was a record of arguments, and that every deviation had a name and a year and usually a dead man attached.

“Show me an overlap,” Vessel said.

“There are two hundred and eleven of them,” Berrit said. “Where the Line doubles back, the ground belongs to both, and the both is a legal person, and the both is called the interval, and the interval pays no customs, and the interval is where every hedged field, every unattested death, and every marriage two provinces disagreed about has happened for nine hundred years, and no province can close it, because to close it you must say which of the two is right, and the Line is crooked so that nobody has to say.”

* * *

They laid the two boards side by side in the hearing chamber, the chord on the left and the Line on the right, and the clerk lit both lamps, and it was the first time in nineteen years that both had been in the same room.

Vessel looked at it for eleven minutes without speaking, because she was reading the overlap, and the overlap was not a border disagreement, and nothing in the archives described it, because no province had ever drawn it, because to draw it you must accept that the ground is not yours.

She had expected a scandal of inconvenience: a farm cut in two, a road crossed, a house made a stranger in its own courtyard. It was worse, and it was duller. The chord crossed the interval at four hundred and six points, and at each of them it entered ground that had been answering to two law courts, two sets of weights, two bell-ringers, and two clerks’ hands for longer than either archive had been kept.

And the effect was arithmetic rather than romantic. Where a chord leaves an overlap, it enters a belonging. The interval held a census of its own, and its people stood on neither provincial roll, and if the chord came through, the province outside the loop would have to count them and the province inside would have to release them, and such a release was a transfer of persons, and the empire transferred persons by weight.

“Two million,” Berrit said. “Not two million people who lose a market. Two million people moved, administratively, out of the jurisdiction that has always held them into the jurisdiction that has never heard of them, with their taxes and their marriages and their dead going with them. I put it in the appendix at folio nine, Chancellor, because it is not an argument. It is a count.”

Vessel said nothing, because she had been the best administrator in the empire for a long time, and she was looking at a better map, and she was becoming aware of a thing she had known for thirty years without ever having to hold it at once.

“The broken meridian is not a mistake,” she said. “It is the accumulated total of four hundred years of what is actually there, and a straight line would divide the empire into two equal halves, and the two halves are not equal, and the crookedness is the record of what is actually there, and straightening it would put two million people in the wrong jurisdiction, including, on your own map, one woman who has been in the correct graveyard for sixty-one years.”

Berrit did not answer. She had made the number, and the number was the end of her case, and she had a professional’s respect for a sentence that took a case out of a person’s hands and put it into a statute.

“One more thing, since you have asked me for nothing,” she said. “Straightening the Line empties four hundred and six custom houses, weighbridges, and toll clerks, and the saving is one hundred and nine thousand marks a year. It is in the appendix. It is real, and it is also the entire reason it has never been done. Every time a person here has wanted a straight thing, they have found the money for it in the wrong place, and the wrong place has a man in it, and the man has said no, and has been right.”

* * *

The Line was straightened by statute: sixty days for objection, and the objections were filed, and they were filed competently, by the western province, by the Salt Roads, by the Office of Weights, by the four hundred and six householders, by the bishops of Stennick and Oll Harrow, and by eleven people who filed alone, gave no reasons, and asked for no costs.

Vessel heard all of them and decided nothing for eleven days, which was longer than the last chancellor had taken on any question. The clerks noticed, and were right to notice, because a chancellor who hesitates is read as a chancellor who means to yield, and such a chancellor attracts a different kind of petitioner.

The western province made the strongest case, and its argument was about money. The two-regime structure cost the west four times what it cost the east, the west had never been compensated, and the west was not sentimental, it was a creditor. The Salt Roads made the second strongest, and it concerned a bridge, and the bridge was a real hazard, and two eastern commissioners agreed with the hazard and not with the conclusion.

Vessel ruled on the eleventh day. She upheld the objection of the interval on the ground that the interval was not represented, and the finding was procedurally correct, and everyone in that chamber knew it for a piece of luck, because on any other ground there would have been no inquiry at all, and the inquiry was what she wanted.

“I am not ruling against the survey,” she told the chamber. “I am ruling that a survey which has not counted the ground it is about to become the boundary of is not a survey. Miss Berrit counted it. She counted it in the eighth year, at her own expense, in winter, against advice, and the appendix is in front of you. The commissioners have six months.”

That was the whole of her opposition, and the chancellor knew what it was worth, and she said so to the only man in the chamber she trusted, which was the clerk who had written down her words for thirty years.

“It is a delay,” she said. “The chord will survive six months. It is a good chord. And when it survives I will have to decide it on something other than procedure, and I have decided nothing on a policy in thirty years, and there is no procedure for what I am about to do.”

* * *

The parallel survey was commissioned in the ninth month, and it was not a body and had no title, because a thing with a title becomes a department, and a department becomes a policy, and a policy can be replaced at the next change of government. Vessel kept it in her own office and gave it a schedule, which was the only instrument she ever built that was designed to survive without her: a survey of the Line, nine hundred years deep, of every reason the Line is where it is, the reasons entered in the order they were made, the whole to be bound as one instrument and read twice a year.

Eleven thousand pages was the estimate, and it was correct. The work went the way the empire went: slowly, in ink, in the cold, by people who were not clever. The petition of 1387. The boundary rolls. The bargains of the flax reeve. The rent book of the abbey. The letter about the two dead men in the water-meadow, and the water-meadow itself. Nineteen years, and Vessel did not see the end of it, and the three chancellors after her each tried to fold the work into a department, and were defeated by the schedule.

* * *

The Meridian Proper ran to nine hundred years and eleven thousand pages, bound in the leather the western abbey had been saving since 1412, and it was signed into law in the ninety-fourth year of the working, by a chancellor of seventy-three who had inherited the schedule and obeyed it.

The straight line had been built. The straight line was in use: nine millimetres closed, nine years in the making, the tariff equalised on both sides, the two custom houses at Hallow Bridge gone, the four hundred and six toll houses gone, the four hundred and six households that had never known what the money was for paid for in full, and nobody could remember a year that had gone better.

No one living had ever seen the Line broken. The child of the interval was apprenticed at fourteen, because the interval was now a region with a name and a schedule and a school and a border, and it was a straight place, and the straight place was very good, and it had no jokes in it.

The last page of the Meridian Proper was a plate. On it were two small line portraits, drawn for the record in the third year of the working, when both of them had still been alive and arguing about a drainage ditch, and both of them died before the bound.

On the left, Aurel Vessel, Chancellor, aged fifty-five, in the plain collar of her office, with the oak and brass chain of the survey on the table in front of her, not smiling, and not looking at the surveyor.

On the right, Ilsa Berrit, Surveyor of the Meridian, aged thirty-four, both hands on the brass, sleeves rolled, the burn on the wrist, the second joint of the thumb locked for good.

Beneath the portraits there was one line in the first chancellor’s hand, and it was the finding of the whole instrument, and the chamber had it read without comment, because there is nothing to comment on.

“The line is where the reasons are,” the chancellor read, “and where the reasons end there is a straight line, and it is there because we have finished arguing, and that is a good reason, and it is the only one of the two that is honest.”
