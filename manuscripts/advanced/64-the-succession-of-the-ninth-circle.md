# The Succession of the Ninth Circle

![Cover art: The Succession of the Ninth Circle](../art/advanced/64-the-succession-of-the-ninth-circle.webp)

The Registry of the Compact occupied the third gallery of the Circlehouse and had four windows, two of which opened, and on the morning the eight-house instrument was to be certified the notary was forty-four years old and had been in the chair for eleven years, which was four years longer than the last notary had managed, and the reason was not competence. Isolde Ashe had simply declined, twice, to be assessed for the chair, in a way that the Assessors had taken as a form of modesty, and then the Chair had died in the winter and the Assessors had appointed her, and the appointment had been ratified, and nobody had ever asked her whether she wanted it, because asking would have required a reason.

The instrument to be certified was the Fourth Extension of the Compact, an instrument of about nine pages that recited the nine houses, and then said eight, in a table, in a column headed HOUSE, and then provided for the administration of the city-state for a further nine years. The recited nine was in the preamble and had been in the preamble since 1871. The table said eight because the ninth house had walked out of the Compact in a single night eleven years ago and taken the founding schedule with it.

Isolde read the nine pages twice, which was her habit and not her doubt, and then she took the seal down from its bracket, a wheel of nine points on a handle of cornel wood, and pressed it into the wax, and the ninth point went into the wax exactly as it had for a hundred and fifty-one years, because nobody had re-cut the seal, because re-cutting a seal is an amendment, and the Compact had not been amended, and there was no longer a mechanism for amending it.

* * *

On the floor of the Circlehouse the argument took four hours and was conducted entirely in the second person plural. Delegate Boaz Ostrov of the second house had a table, because he was of the second house, which was the house of the salt road, and the second house had kept records since before the Compact existed. He had three columns of figures and he put them on the board in his own hand, because the clerks had gone for coffee, and he did not blame them for it.

The first column was the number of houses with living members, which was eight. The second was the average number of years a house took to produce a successor, which was nineteen, and which meant that a rotation of nine required a reserve of roughly one and a half houses at all times, and Corvaine had been running on its reserve for a decade.

The third column was the number of sittings of the Circlehouse since the ninth house left, which was two hundred and eleven, and Ostrov pointed at it and said that two hundred and eleven sittings had produced a functioning city-state, with its water, its bridges, its granaries, and its very good system for burying people, and that he was not aware of anyone in Corvaine who had gone without water in that time, and that the ninth house had gone without a seat.

“An eight-house rotation is a rotation,” he said. “A compact that can lose a house is a compact that can lose two, and one that can lose two can lose four, and at that point you are not governing a city-state, you are adjudicating a civil war with a table at it.”

Nobody contradicted him. That was the thing about the floor of the Circlehouse, and it was the thing Isolde had noticed in her fourth year and had never mentioned to anyone. The advocates of eight were never accused, because no one accused them. They were eight serious people with a table and a case, and the case was good, and the advocates of nine were nine serious people who would have to say the word vacancy out loud, and the word had not been said in public in Corvaine for eleven years.

“You are all waiting,” said the Speaker of the fourth house, who was Isolde’s house and who had been Speaker for six years, “for somebody to say the word, and nobody is going to, because the notary is sitting there with the seal in her hand, and the notary does not vote.”

* * *

The ninth house had left on the ninth of Ashfall, and the departure had been noticed at the morning sitting and not at the evening one, and there had been no scene in the Circlehouse at all. House Nine was the house of the ferrymen and the water-clerks, and it had held a seat since 1871, and what it had taken with it was not a seat but a document: the founding schedule, the engrossed original, the one that made the rotation a rotation instead of a sequence, kept in a case in the ninth house’s archive since the year the Compact was made, and not in the Registry, because the Registry held a transcript and a transcript is not an instrument.

Isolde had been thirty-three and second in the Registry then, and she had been in the gallery that night, and she had watched nine people leave a room carrying a case, and she had written it up. The minute she wrote, and the minute that stands in the book, says: the Compact notes House Nine’s absence. It does not say that the schedule is missing, though everybody in the gallery knew, and it does not say that a rotation without its schedule is a sequence of eight-year terms, though that too was known within a week.

Eleven years had produced four extensions, two attempts to recover the schedule by requisition, one circular to the other eighty-one city-states asking whether a copy existed, and a set of minutes in which the question of the schedule was raised in nineteen sittings and answered in none. In the fourth year a committee of three had been appointed to consider the matter and had met twice, and had drafted an invitation to House Nine, and the draft had gone to the Assessors, and the Assessors had returned it with a minute attached, and the minute said that the Compact had no standing to correspond with a house that had withdrawn, and the draft had gone into a file, and the file had not been opened since.

Yannik Roos was twenty-seven and a junior counsel with the Registry and had been in the gallery four months, and he came to Isolde’s desk on the morning after the extension was certified and put a sheaf of paper on it, and said that he had found something while indexing for the Fourth Extension, and that he had checked it twice because of what it was.

* * *

It was Article Nineteen of the founding schedule, and it was called Of a Vacancy, and it ran to thirty pages, and it had never been invoked, because there had never been a vacancy, and it was in the certified transcript made in 1871 and kept in the third gallery of the Circlehouse in a box marked TRANSCRIPT, ORIGINAL, SCHEDULES, 1 of 3, which had been opened perhaps twice a century.

Isolde read the operative words aloud, twice, and then a third time, sitting quite still, with the transcript open in front of her, in a room with two working windows.

“The Compact shall invite the house to return,” Yannik read. “Not restore. Invite. The Compact shall not treat a vacancy as filled, nor the absence of a house as a consent, nor the silence of a house as a refusal, for any purpose, at any time, or in any instrument. Where a house has withdrawn, the Compact shall send one invitation, in its own hand, by its own notary, and shall record the answer whatever it is, and the record of the answer shall be part of the instrument.”

“It says one invitation,” Isolde said.

“It says one invitation. One. And it does not say the invitation may be withheld, and it does not provide for a committee, and it does not permit the question to be treated as a matter of correspondence with a house that has withdrawn, which is what the Assessors said in the fourth year, and they were wrong, and they were wrong in a minute, and minutes are what the Assessors are for.”

“Then it has been breached.”

“It has not been breached,” Yannik said. “It has never been operated. There is a difference and it is the whole of the case, and it is why nobody has ever been in trouble. If you breach a clause somebody can be cited. If you never operate a clause, there is nothing to enforce, and nobody has done anything wrong, and the Compact goes on.”

That was the shape of it, and it had been the shape of it for eleven years, and Isolde had known the shape of it since the night of the ninth of Ashfall, and she had a certificate of good conduct from her own father’s hands on the wall of the gallery to the effect that she was a careful officer of the Compact, careful in the matter of the founding schedule, in the matter of the vacancy, in the matter of all instruments committed to her charge.

* * *

The Compact’s failure had not been a secret. It had been a series of competent decisions by careful people, and Isolde had made some of them, and the record of her own share was in the books in a hand like her father’s.

The first year, the absence was a vacancy, and the notary’s duty was to enter it as a vacancy, and she entered it as a vacancy. The second year, the Assessors advised that the Compact was in continuity, and continuity was the doctrine, and continuity was reasonable, because House Nine had walked out and a house that has walked out is not a house that has been extinguished, and so the Compact went on and the seat stayed empty, which is a thing that can be done in law and looks, in a minute book, exactly like anything else.

The third year a delegate asked, in a full sitting, for the Compact to comply with Article Nineteen, and the Speaker ruled it out of order on the ground that the question had been answered, because the Compact’s answer was that it had complied, and the Compact had complied, because the minute recording the invitation’s absence and the minute recording the Compact’s compliance were the same minute, and nobody could show which was which. The delegate was not a fool and did not press the point, and he sat down, and Isolde wrote the ruling in the book, and the ruling is good law.

The fourth year a committee of three was appointed, and drafted the invitation, and the draft was seven lines, and it began: the Compact of the Nine Houses of Corvaine presents its compliments to the Ninth House and requests. The Assessors returned it with the minute about standing. Isolde had drafted the covering note, and the covering note was three sentences long, and it was careful, and it was true, and it did not mention Article Nineteen, and the omission was hers, and no register of the omission existed, and that was also deliberate.

* * *

A rotation is a promise that nobody is permanent, and a promise nobody is permanent is a promise that anybody can leave, and Corvaine had made it in 1871 to nine houses, of which the ninth had been the smallest and the poorest and the one whose members had never lived inside the walls, and the making of that promise had been, in its way, the act of a city-state that was trying to be decent, and eleven years of not breaking it had produced the most livable government in the region.

Isolde believed this. She believed the eight-house Compact, and she believed it as a belief and not a pose, and she had said so to nobody, and the belief showed in the way she certified instruments, which was exactly as the Compact of Nine Houses would have required, and would have been, in fact, identical to the way her predecessor had certified them, which is what a rotation is for.

“Somebody has to say it,” she said to Yannik. “The floor will not say it, and the Assessors will not say it, because the Assessors are appointed by the seats, and the empty seat appoints nobody, and so the Assessors are eight people who must be re-elected, and no one of them will be the one who says the word.”

“Then the notary says it,” Yannik said.

“The notary does not vote.”

“The notary is not being asked to vote.” He put his finger on the page, on the words that were not underlined and were not meant to be. “The Compact shall send one invitation, in its own hand, by its own notary, and shall record the answer whatever it is. The answer will be a refusal. It will certainly be a refusal. Nothing has changed in eleven years, and I do not expect anything to have changed. And if the answer is a refusal, then the refusal is in the book, and the Compact has complied, and the vacancy is a vacancy the Compact has dealt with, and the fifth extension is the extension of an instrument that has done what its founding schedule requires of it.”

Isolde looked at the transcript for a while. Then she said: “Sending it and being refused is a different thing from not sending it.”

“Yes,” said Yannik Roos. “It is the only thing.”

* * *

She did it on her own time, which meant on a Sunday, at her own desk, in her own hand, and the invitation was eleven lines, and it was the fourth draft, and it did not contain the words request or compliance or vacancy, and it did not cite Article Nineteen, and it asked the Ninth House of Corvaine to attend a sitting of the Circlehouse, and it gave a date eleven weeks out, and it was signed with the cornel-handled seal and with her name, and the ninth point of the wheel went into the wax.

The answer came in nine days, and it was two paragraphs, and it came on the same paper stock the Ninth House had used since 1871, and it said that the House thanked the Compact for its invitation, that it had considered the invitation in full session, and that the House declined. It added, in a final sentence that Isolde read four times, that the House would be glad to receive the founding schedule, which was the Ninth House’s own and had never been the Compact’s, and that the House would resume its seat on its return.

On the day appointed, six delegates of the Ninth House came in through the river door, and one of them asked, before sitting, whether the Compact would table the schedule at the same sitting, and Isolde said that the Compact would table whatever the House tabled, and they sat, and the sitting lasted ninety minutes, and nothing else was discussed, and at the end of it the Speaker of the Ninth House put the case on the table, and asked that the founding schedule be returned to the house that had kept it since 1871, and the motion carried without a division.

* * *

Isolde resigned on the fourth of Var, by letter, in two paragraphs, the first of which gave notice that she would vacate the office of notary at the end of the term and the second of which thanked the Assessors, and both of which were correct, and neither of which mentioned the fourth year, or a covering note of three sentences, or a certificate in a careful hand, and she wrote them both herself at her own desk in the third gallery and sealed them and gave them to the clerk, who was twenty-three and named Pell, and who would remember the morning only as the morning the notary resigned, which is a thing that happens every few years.

Pell took the transcript down afterwards, because Article Nineteen had been lifted from the box and the box had to be re-collated, and the founding schedule was required in duplicate for the House’s archive and for the Registry, and the Registry copy was the first that had ever been made, because the Registry had never held an instrument of the schedule and had only ever held a transcript of it.

So the clerk copied the founding schedule of the Compact of the Nine Houses of Corvaine, in a fair hand, on Registry paper, at a quarter past two on a Wednesday, and stamped each page as he went, and when he came to the article on a vacancy he stopped and asked whether he was to include it, and the notary told him to include everything, and he included everything, and the copy ran to two hundred and forty pages, and it was the eighth copy of the schedule in the history of the document, and Pell regarded it as a filing chore, and filed it.
