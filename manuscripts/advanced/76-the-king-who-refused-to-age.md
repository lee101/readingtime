# The King Who Refused to Age

![Cover art: The King Who Refused to Age](../art/advanced/76-the-king-who-refused-to-age.webp)

The King’s study was on the north side, above the orchard wall, and the light came off the river in the afternoon and went by four. He was forty-one. He had been forty-one for sixty years, and everyone in the kingdom had a copy of that figure, and every copy agreed.

Perrin had been a page of the second house for seven months and was fifteen, and the post was carrying a stick, holding a door, and sitting on a stool from three until the light went. On the fourth Tuesday she found the King alone with a day-book open in front of him, and stood in the doorway, and he said, “Put the candle in.”

“Your Majesty,” she said, “I have been in the kingdom three years.”

“Longer than that,” said the King. “But I have been shorter. Put the candle in, and sit down, and I will tell you why I said that, and you will want to be sitting down.”

He put the candle in and went back to the day-book, and she sat on the stool, and nobody had ever explained to her that there was a subject a person could not ask about, and so she asked it, in the ordinary voice, the way you would ask the price of a horse.

“Your Majesty, are you forty-one?”

He stopped writing. He put the pen in the inkwell. He looked at her for long enough that she thought she had been rude, and then he said, “I do not know.”

The girl believed him. It was the plainest thing he had ever said to her, and she took it as you take a fact about a river, and said, “Very well, Your Majesty,” and he said thank you, and she sat on the stool until the light went.

* * *

“Every year,” said Lord Chancellor Rell, “on the ninth of Marren, the clerks go down to the vault with a fair copy of the coronation instrument, read the schedule aloud, and write the year against it. It takes forty minutes. It is the oldest thing in the treasury.”

He was sixty-eight, and had been Lord Chancellor nineteen years, and he had a way of setting a document down as though the document had done something.

“The coronation instrument schedules the kingdom,” he said. “Not the King’s age in the first instance. The kingdom. It establishes the day from which the kingdom’s obligations are counted, and the count derives from the sovereign’s ratified age, which is forty-one, and so the kingdom is a year old, and the schedule has been a year old for sixty years, and every fiscal instrument drawn since has been drawn against it.”

“The kingdom is a year old,” said Perrin.

“The kingdom is a year old,” said the Chancellor, “and the debt roll was priced on a census taken at the coronation, and a realm one year old has no births recorded in it, so the census is short by a year’s worth of people, and the treasury has been compounding that shortage against itself for sixty years. The kingdom is solvent, in the most extraordinary fiscal position in the west, and it is there because of a filing error made by a competent man on a bad afternoon, and correcting it tomorrow would be a catastrophe with a date on it.”

“Do you know how it happened?”

“I have read the account,” said the Chancellor. “He was a clerk of the rolls called Edrin, he was twenty-three. He copied the schedule from the instrument and the King’s age from the household book, and the household book for a coronation day is the day before, and the King had had a fever, and the fever had made him a year older in the book than in the instrument. The two pages were identical except for a figure, and the figure was the only thing checked.”

* * *

“There is a statute,” he said, “that permits a second coronation. The sovereign is re-ratified in his own person, the schedule is re-examined and re-entered, and the fiscal apparatus of the kingdom is re-fixed to the true day. It has been repealed four times.”

“Forty years ago. Reason given: a second coronation would be an assertion that the first had been defective, and the crown’s whole claim is continuity. Ottavia Renn drafted it, because the alternative was worse, and she said so in the margin.”

“Twenty-seven years ago. Reason given: a rumour had reached the wine market in two cities, and a rumour corrected is a rumour confirmed. A repeal costs nothing and makes no admission. My teacher drafted it. He was dying, and it is the best piece of work he ever did.”

“Eighteen years ago, in the ninth year of the Darian war, when there was no money for a coronation and no appetite for one. The repeal named the King as a party, so he had to assent, and it was put in front of him at dinner, and he signed what he was given. The instrument is correctly drawn and the assent correctly given, and the man who brought it apologised and meant it.”

“Nine years ago. A lawyer in the municipal library found the statute, wrote to the Crown, and was answered with an instrument revoking it in terms so closely modelled on the original that he wrote back to say he was satisfied. It is the best work of my career, and I could not have done it at forty-four.”

“And each of those four men was protecting the kingdom.”

“Each of those four men was protecting the kingdom,” said the Chancellor, “and in my opinion three were protecting themselves, and the fourth was protecting you, and the fourth was me, and I would not have drafted it if you had not sent for me and said the shape of the page was wrong.”

* * *

“Then resign,” said Perrin.

The Chancellor did not answer at once, and the King, listening from the window with his back to the room, said, “She is right that you have thought about it.”

“I have thought about it for nineteen years,” said the Chancellor, “and never been able to make the question sound like anything other than a catastrophe, and I am going to try once, and you may stop me.”

“A fresh coronation is a fresh instrument,” he said. “And a fresh instrument cannot cite the schedule established by a previous one, and a man reading the two sees the discrepancy in a quarter of an hour, as four men have. So the new instrument states the age at accession. Not the ratified one, which is proved wrong. The true one, which is whatever I am.”

The room was quiet. Down in the yard a page was calling a ladder about.

“Which is a hundred and one,” said the King.

“Yes.”

“So I would remain forty-one in every record in the kingdom, and the day a successor is crowned the day-book gets a new instrument in it, and the instrument says a hundred and one, and then it is a fact.”

“Yes,” said the Chancellor. “And there is a phrase in the second schedule that says the age of the sovereign at the day of his accession, and there is no day of his accession anywhere in the kingdom, because the coronation had a date, not a day, and the distinction has never been tested. I have not asked anyone to test it. Nor have you.”

“No,” said the King. “I have not.”

“Your Majesty,” said the Chancellor, “if you were to resign, I would want that tested in a fortnight, by counsel, in a room with no fire in it. Shall I put it in that order.”

“Put it in the order you like,” said the King. “It is the only piece of administration in this business that I have ever wanted done badly.”

* * *

Perrin believed him, and went back to the stool, and the King has never been asked a direct question in the twenty years since he stopped volunteering one; the four people who asked him before her each worked it out in about eleven minutes, and each decided not to say it, and the reasons were four different, and the fourth was his.

He told her about them in the second year, not in order, and not as confession. He told her the way a man reads out a list of things he has put off, in an afternoon, to a girl who was nineteen and had a very good hand for figures and was the only person in the household permitted to write in the day-book.

The first had been a physician, forty years ago, at three in the morning, in a house in the lower town with a fever in it. Aurel had been forty-one for twenty years. Doctor Iven Sarr took the King’s wrist, counted, let go, and said, “You are sixty-one.”

“Eleven minutes,” the King said. “I had got up twice on the way. He was the first person in my life to have the thought and I watched him have it in the street, and in the doorway, and twice on the stairs.”

“And he said nothing.”

“He wrote forty-one in the chart,” said the King, “and nothing else that night. The fever broke, and eleven years afterwards, in a street, he was very glad to see me, and neither of us said a word. I understood at the time that he had not said it because I was forty-one and standing at the foot of the bed, and a man cannot do that to you on a Tuesday for no reason at all.”

* * *

The second had been the Darian ambassador, thirty-one years ago, at a dinner of ninety people in a windowless hall. Teodor Rusk had been posted here nine years and had spent them learning what the kingdom’s paper was worth.

“We had been talking about the debt roll for an hour before you sat down,” said the King. “He explained what a term meant, and he was patient, and he was doing it to show me the shape of the thing that held his master’s money. Eleven minutes from the moment you sat down. You had a roll in your head, I could see it, you always did at dinner, and then you put the roll down.”

“I put the roll down,” the King said, “and I said, and I have thought about the sentence for thirty-one years and I still have not worked out whether it was clever. I said: does the term stand if the count moves?”

The Chancellor had looked up from his plate. The Darian said, “That is a technical question.”

“It is the only question there is,” said the King. “And he said that if the count moved the term would stand, and the count was a matter for the sovereign’s own instrument, and the Darian treasury had taken no view on the age of any sovereign, and a man was not obliged to hold a view on things outside his province. And he filled my glass.”

“And you were pleased,” said Perrin.

“I was so pleased,” said the King, “that I have thought about it every year since, and the year I was most pleased was the year his granddaughter’s loan came due. Nothing whatever was said to me in that hall except four sentences about a term, and I have been living on them.”

* * *

The third had been the Keeper of the Rolls of the Year, twenty years ago, in the long room under the west chapel, where twenty-two thousand days of annals were kept, one to a line, in nine hands.

“Anselm Vey was the best man of letters I ever met, and he came because the King had never been in his long room in forty years, and he wanted to be able to say that he had.”

“He got as far as the first line,” said the King. “Eleven minutes. He was a very slow reader. I watched him reach my grandfather’s reign and see the day at the beginning of it, and then read the whole reign in a different voice, quickly, the way a man reads a tide table when he has understood the tide.”

“And then?”

“Then he closed the book, thanked me for the visit, and walked me out through the vestry, and at the door he said, may I ask a thing that is not about the annals. I said ask. He said whether the sovereign had ever asked to see the day-book. I said no. He said, nor should you. I have turned that over for twenty years and I do not know what it was for.”

“He wrote a note,” said the Chancellor, from the window.

“He wrote a note,” said the King. “In the year following. It went into the rolls, and it is not in the annals, and it is in the wrong place, because the roll-keeper was told to put it in quarantine, and the roll-keeper was me, and I did not tell him where quarantine was. Quarantine is a drawer with a broken hasp in the long room, and it is still there.”

* * *

The fourth had been twenty-two years ago, and she had been Ottavia Renn, and she was the first of the four to get it in the wrong order, which meant she had it before the King had finished his soup.

He put the spoon down. “She was fifty-six and a fortnight dead, and the estate came to me because there was nobody else, and among the papers was her own repeal, the amendment of forty years ago. I read it because I was the King and could not think of anything else to do with an evening.”

“The margin,” said the Chancellor. “You told me the margin.”

“She had written in the margin of her own repeal, in the same hand, nothing for a reader, because a reader was not who she was writing to. She wrote that the sovereign’s age was an entry, an entry could be corrected, and the difficulty was not the correction. It was that a corrected sovereign was a man who had been sixty for an hour, and a kingdom that had counted from a mistake for forty years had no way of holding a corrected number.”

“That is a very good piece of analysis,” said the Chancellor.

“It is a very good piece of analysis,” said the King, “and it is why I have not had it tested in nineteen years, and I have not been entirely honest with either of you, and I am going to try to be now.”

He looked at the girl on the stool, who had her hand on the day-book, copying the month, unfinished.

“Thirty-one years ago a man sat at this table and I asked him a question that made him put down his roll, and I said nothing to him. I want to explain that, because I have never explained it to anybody, and I have had nineteen years and two Lord Chancellors, and nobody asked me.”

“That is what it is like to be a mistake. It is not a scandal. Nothing has been taken. Nobody has been hurt. It is that you are a thing that was entered wrongly, and the only way anybody finds out is if somebody asks a direct question, and then the whole thing has to be a fact. Then I am a man of a hundred and one instead of a man of forty-one, and the treasury falls in, and the four thousand people in this city whose mortgages are indexed to the kingdom’s age lose their houses.”

“And then I am a very interesting item in four countries’ gazettes by the following spring,” he said, “and inside two years there is a different man in this room, who will be a better king than I have been, and I will have done all of it to protect a filing error.”

Silence in the study, and the light going off the river.

“So you said nothing to the Darian,” said Perrin.

“I said nothing to the Darian because for four years after that dinner I did not know, and by the time I knew, it was a thing you could say. And Ottavia knew first, and I did not tell her, and she died without my telling her, and I have not been able to get round that.”

“The fourth reason is the best one and it is the one I have. She asked me not to. On her last afternoon she said, do not tell anyone, and I said I would not, and she said why, and I told her, and she said that was the first sensible thing you have said to me in eleven years. She died at four the next morning, and the Chancellor has been very careful for twenty-two years never to raise it, and I have noticed the care.”

* * *

The King died in the fourth year of the reign’s true reckoning, at a hundred and one, and that reckoning was a thing invented by a page of the second house with a good hand for figures, on the fourth Tuesday, out of a day-book and a debt roll and a phrase in a second schedule.

She had asked for the reading to be made a duty of the office. He refused for two years, and agreed in the third, on a condition: that she read it as an entry and not as an accusation, and that when she came to the days that were his own he might say so. She said yes without hesitating, and he never forgot that she had not.

She read it in the north study, from the first of the corpus, on the first of the month, at ten in the morning, and the room learned to be quiet. She read the coronation, and the census, and the short year, and then the sixty years, day by day, in order, without comment. Where the day-book recorded a visit or a signature she gave the day and nothing more, and where it recorded nothing she gave the day and nothing more. It took four years, because the book was being kept as it was read, and it did not end until the man it was about had finished, and it ended in the third week of a wet February.

The clerks struck the totals on the last page, and the accounts were solvent to the day, which was the fourth great and ordinary miracle of the reign. They wrote the day-book line that no day-book line had ever needed, giving the true age, in the fair hand, and entered it in the annals in the ninth hand, at the place where the reign begins, which is where Anselm Vey had said it went and had not been listened to.

On the last morning he turned his head and asked her what the date was, and she read it to him, and he said that it was correct. Perrin Ashlow, who had asked the first question and believed the answer and had then spent four years making the answer unnecessary, read the rest of that day out loud in the same voice. The last line of the true reckoning is the day, and beneath it, in the hand of the man who put it in quarantine in a drawer with a broken hasp, is the word that stood in the margin of the first repeal, and the word is: entered.
