# The Last Bard of the Unfinished World

![Cover art: The Last Bard of the Unfinished World](../art/advanced/37-the-last-bard-of-the-unfinished-world.webp)

At dusk, the village of Bellwether began to lose its name.

Ilyra Vey saw it happen from the crest of the pilgrim road. First the boundary stones went pale, their carved letters thawing into wet chalk. Then the smell of woodsmoke weakened, followed by the evening bells that had guided travelers for two hundred years. Each chime sounded farther away.

The village sat in a fold of green hills, terraced with gardens and red-roofed houses. While Ilyra watched, one roof became a line of red rain on the grass. A woman near the well continued pumping water into a bucket, though the well's stone rim was already mist.

Ilyra set down her battered lute case. Its brass clasps had gone green, and one tuning peg was missing. She had wrapped the empty socket in red thread.

“Something has taken the ending,” she said.

The woman at the well looked at her. Water spilled over the bucket unnoticed.

“It did not take it,” she said. “We forgot it.”

At fifty-seven, Ilyra had retired twice. The first lasted until a duke paid her in warm bread to sing at his mother's funeral. The second lasted nine years, until travelers began speaking of songs that kept cities from vanishing. She carried two blue coats, a black hat buttoned beneath her chin, a walking staff, and a voice scraped thin by decades of nightly singing.

She crossed the thinning road. Underfoot, it felt less like stone than flour.

In Bellwether's square stood a fountain shaped as an open book. Water poured from its chalk-white pages, but the carved lines were smooth.

“It was about two sisters,” an old mason said. “One kept the bees. The other kept the bell tower. They quarreled before the winter market. Then everyone went inside for supper, and afterward…”

He pressed both hands against his head.

“Afterward was where the story stopped.”

Ilyra unpacked her cracked lute. The mason remembered bees humming like rain. The fisher remembered a red kerchief on the flood bank. An old bootmaker recalled a funeral bell beneath black water. Each memory was bright, but none contained its reason.

Near dusk, a stranger arrived in a plain coach. She was very old, with silver hair pinned by a bone clasp.

“I am Avel, keeper of the vanished,” she said. “Everyone knows the woman who left the Ballad of First and Last unfinished.”

Avel raised one hand. The fountain pages shivered. A thin black shape with no face peeled from the stone and hovered between them. It listened like a held breath.

Ilyra sang the first scrap she had collected. The fountain supplied four bright notes over a low drone. She shaped them around the bees, the red kerchief, and the bell under water. The black shape stretched, delighted. The houses stopped retreating.

“Will you remember the final line?”

The question opened a hollow place inside Ilyra. She had sung before armies, crowned children, mourned kings, and storms that mistook thunder for percussion. But a final line could not be borrowed. It had to be true enough for everyone who had lived it.

“I will find it.”

“Then come. There are fewer than you think.”

* * *

The world beyond Bellwether was coming loose at its seams.

Roads ended in fields they had not crossed before. Rivers ran uphill beneath a violet moon. In one green valley, a windmill turned without a tower, its white sails combing the grass.

Avel drove while Ilyra walked beside the coach. Every settlement endured a world and the story that made it memorable. Stone, grain, and people persisted only while they remained part of a tale. When an ending was removed, details fell away. Names vanished. Buildings blurred. Finally, no one recalled why the place mattered enough to exist.

“The Quiet College took every ending,” Avel said. “Happy, humble, cruel. They said final words were too powerful: kings to thrones, lovers to vows, ghosts to their deaths. They believed every tale should remain unfinished.”

Ilyra watched a haystack lose its shape. “Now entire places disappear.”

“Yes.”

“Worse.”

For years, Ilyra had avoided unfinished songs. On certain nights, she could still hear the unfinished song that had ended her first career.

Four roads met at the Hall of Nine Names. First Well and Last Mill quarreled over a river reed. A treaty gave the river to First Well. Its judges declared Last Mill the first village destroyed; the millers declared First Well the last village to deserve destruction. The words took effect. Doors failed. People forgot their children's names. The dead walked home, unable to leave.

Ilyra had sung a counter-decree: both villages were sacred, neither first nor last, the living and dead were free. It had worked. It had also broken a treaty signed in blood, killed a boundary keeper, and erased a festival older than either kingdom.

Then she had walked away before the final line.

The College completed a hostile ending in her absence. A hero cut the bell rope, ended the feud, and made the boundary keeper the villain. The story closed with peace. Children were punished for singing it.

A voice in Ilyra's own register said, “The ending cannot be taken until someone living agrees that it has happened.”

The black listener unfolded beside the coach. Avel drew it into her sleeve. Through it, Ilyra glimpsed thrones holding figures frozen in final gestures: a king at a locked gate, a mother at a window, someone carving her name on a familiar wall.

“Do not show me that.”

“Why not?”

“Because I know what I will see.”

* * *

The Quiet College occupied a mountain of unworked stone. Its circular hall had no windows. Inside, young archivists in gray coats sat at round tables, each holding the final line of a story. Whenever a line was read, someone erased it. The words sank into blotting paper, leaving a black stain shaped like a door.

The College's head waited beside a vast blank book. She was broad-shouldered and middle-aged, with a shaved head, green velvet coat, and ink-dark hands.

“Late as ever, Ilyra,” she said. “You sang over my cradle when your company passed the western gate. You gave me this missing thumb when I stole it from a sleeping giant.”

A page named Tanno, wearing one gray glove, brightened with recognition.

“The Ilyra Vey?”

“The same. Too old to be dangerous.”

The woman's smile vanished. “So you are the Curator.”

“And you opened Bellwether's well.”

The Curator’s smile vanished. “Avel said you came to bargain.”

“I came to ask why.”

The answer was grief.

Three centuries earlier, a plague swept the mountain kingdoms. Stories called the dead back to relive their final days, and the plague followed them into the living world. The first College of Endings tried to repair those tales. Each repair forced a soul to continue as the story required.

“The Quiet College cut the endings free,” the Curator said. “We thought liberation was kinder than correction. The dead remained dead. The living were free.”

“You only made forgetting possible.”

“Forgetting is how it ended.”

“Then the plague taught you to fear endings, and you mutilated the tales instead of healing them.”

The Curator bowed her head. “Yes.”

The book before them had no spine, boards, or visible pages. When the Curator laid her ink-dark hand on it, a chorus breathed beneath the mountain.

“The book gathers every ending you restore,” she said. “Offer your unfinished song as the key. Bellwether will hold. Perhaps other villages will follow.”

“Perhaps?”

“Then we will see what may safely be returned.”

Ilyra studied the missing thumb. “Or I can keep everyone unfinished.”

“No. Whole armies would step into the present. The plague would walk again. A village where every child was called into a river might fill this chamber.”

Ilyra had no answer.

That evening, the page Tanno brought Ilyra barley soup and said, “My mother used to tell my ending. ‘Tanno will grow tall and disappoint every person who says she is too small for a great deed.’ Then she would laugh.”

“Was she right?”

“I am tall.”

“So she was.”

The mountain shook. A crack crossed the stone floor and stopped at the blank book. In Bellwether, the fountain spilled black water. Children were vanishing one street at a time.

* * *

Ilyra did not take the key.

She sent Avel back to Bellwether. She sent Tanno to the river towns to recruit cooks, shepherds, boatmen, and anyone who knew a local tale. Archivists carried paper to vanished villages. Ilyra kept the mountain.

For three days, the blank book leaked ash. The suspended endings struck its cover from within. Beside the Curator, Ilyra opened narrow paths through the plagued stories. They unwrote poison cures that bound the dying to illness and loosened hungry ghosts from the habit of seeking. They did not return the endings. Not yet.

On the third night, travelers arrived.

They were not heroes. A flour-covered baker carried her grandmother's sentence: “We kept the river by learning the names of every reed.” A child recited a death song. A queen in mourning gray said, “An ending is not forever. It is a door left unlocked.” Shepherds brought the story of a windmill whose youngest son let the sails fall rather than shame his father. The mill rose around them, stone tower and all.

Avel returned last with a jar of black water. Bellwether had rebuilt its fountain. Its two sisters, it turned out, had guarded different halves of one bee nest. The old keeper lowered the jar.

“They know it was a lie.”

“They know it ended the way they needed to,” Ilyra said. “Lies can mend. Truth only reveals the wound.”

“The key, Ilyra.”

“No. A key should be held by more than one hand.”

Ilyra took her lute and began a song of joining. She sang one ending for each traveler. The baker drummed on her flour sack. The queen struck an anvil with her signet. Tanno beat time on the stone floor. When every tale found its final line, the chorus lifted the words together.

A completed ending did not seal anyone in place. It settled what a story owed and released what it no longer needed. Dead souls found the door. Living people kept their freedom. The plague-struck tales bent around their cruel first command until even it admitted it had done enough harm.

The book filled with light. The mountain's false windows opened onto real sky.

* * *

The last ending was Ilyra's own.

It appeared at the bottom of the blank book, a white page no larger than her two hands. Avel stood nearby. The Curator bent her head. Tanno and hundreds of witnesses waited beyond the hall.

A door opened in the page. On its other side stood Ilyra at twenty-three, before the court at the Hall of Nine Names, her black hair braided like a crown. Dead villagers crowded behind her. A peace commissioner waited in red.

“Sing,” the young Ilyra said, “and we can go home.”

The older woman recognized the moment. She had held it for thirty-four years because every ending she could invent was false. The hero had not saved the villages. The treaty had not repaired them. Even her song had chosen a different history for some people and erased another for the rest.

There was no triumphant final line.

Ilyra drew red thread from her lute's empty socket. She used it to tie the loose peg of a long-ago lute to this one, as she had once tied a snapped string during a storm. It would not restore perfect pitch. It would only keep the instrument from splitting.

Then she sang.

“I left before the court could call my song finished, and it did not save me from growing old, or losing my voice, or admitting the harm I caused. But I kept the road behind me open, so others could pass. This is the end of my ballad. I am no answer. I am only the sound that comes after.”

The page held still. No city rose. No dead returned. No verdict changed.

Her final note emerged low and weathered, cracking where breath met broken tone. Her throat felt loose and painless. She tried once to shape a hum. No sound came.

For one moment, panic rose. Then she remembered the road behind her, crowded with people who had needed no perfect ending to continue. She lowered the lute.

The Curator whispered, “You have surrendered your voice.”

“For a while,” Ilyra said. Her whisper reached every corner. “Bards have hands.”

Outside, dawn entered the mountain. Bellwether's bells rang, then those of a dozen villages, then far-off settlements. Some rang in triumph. Some with grief. One sounded like a cracked cooking pot. The sounds drifted across the waking world, imperfect and alive.

Ilyra Vey joined them on foot.
