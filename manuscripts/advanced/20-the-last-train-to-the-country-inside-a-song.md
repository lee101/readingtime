# The Last Train to the Country Inside a Song

![Cover art: The Last Train to the Country Inside a Song](../art/advanced/20-the-last-train-to-the-country-inside-a-song.webp)

The last train entered Bellwether three minutes before the end of the word Bellwether.

At the platform, Iona Vey saw the last part of the station name fray above the clock. Its hands kept marking ordinary minutes, although no one in the carriage trusted them; outside, chimneys blurred at the edges, brick walls turned thin, and a dog crossed the square to bark at paws that were fading.

Brake wire screamed beneath Iona’s hands as the engine stopped between two notes that had not joined. Below, a porter waved a sheet of music, and the wind tore it until only one line remained: AND ALL I ASKED WAS—

“Your predecessor promised this train would come back,” the porter shouted when he reached the engine. “I’m the conductor,” Iona replied from the cab.

“I know,” the porter said, “which is why we are still here.”

The air smelled of hot metal and rain that had not fallen. When the porter pressed the ribbon into Iona’s gloved hand, he said Bellwether’s closing words had failed because no one could agree how the town ended.

“The blank has reached the station,” he said, while people across the square carried chairs, cradles, market baskets, and one elderly goat toward the railway.

The note under the engines was fading, so if it stopped, the train would lose its road. If Iona left without the town, whatever remained of Bellwether would be unable to change.

Iona put the unfinished line in her verse bag and ordered everyone aboard. The furniture could stay unless it could walk, and the goat would have to stop arguing.

“Then the town?” the porter asked.

“The town is coming,” Iona said, pointing to the thin blue line in the west. A place was still a place, she implied, when the conductor arrived late.

The porter stared at her, then blew his station whistle. The note tore from the tower and followed everyone up the steps, while passengers held scraps of paper, cloth, ribbon, or birch bark for things they had left unsaid.

A widowed courier wept into a sealed envelope, while two inspectors clung to one umbrella. A doctor carried a wedding program with the names scratched away, and all three looked afraid in the way unfinished things often did.

The station master came last with half his badge missing and said Chancellor Vey had sealed the town’s closing words himself. When Iona told him the chancellor was her brother, he replied, “That may be why.”

The rear brake released while a second silence swallowed the place where a junction should have been, and Iona rang for departure before taking the train west on rails that rang like struck wire.

* * *

Beyond the orchard line, pear trees bore paper fruit stamped with promises. The first assigned station stayed in sight for an hour, then vanished until the train’s headlamp found it again.

Nessa Pike met them at the station, a broad-shouldered keeper with ink stains on both wrists. She hung a red signal lantern on the engine and helped the passengers aboard.

“I took the transfer because you were late,” Nessa said. “You promised the last train would leave after the final chorus, before that chorus lost its last note.”

Iona glanced toward the verse bag and asked Nessa, “You collected that from Bellwether?” Then Iona told her, “I collect what the country abandons.”

From behind Nessa, a hard voice said, “You collect property belonging to the Crown.” Its owner was Auditor Sable Rook, dressed in black despite the heat, while two clerks carried cases for seized verses.

“No passenger may give up a verse on this line,” Iona said, keeping one hand on the brake. Under the Endings Act, words left unfinished on public ground belonged to the Crown, but Nessa took down a paper lantern and said her own verse would remain hers.

Sable said the last train could cross only named stations, but no one was allowed to name its stops now. Black paper rolled across the departure board, dimming the train until its lamps resembled drowned stars.

Inside the lantern, where a golden thread burned instead of a flame, Nessa said she had kept Quill Crossing for nineteen years. She had written its morning stops, storm delays, five funerals, and the morning a crooked bridge learned to stand upright, so her station had a name whether Sable agreed or not.

The place-name window on Iona’s console had gone blank, although the track ahead still shone faintly. That happened when a town remembered itself more strongly than a record did, so she ordered the guard to feed all paper lights into the main lamp.

Nessa’s thread jumped to the engine and rattled the clerks’ cases, until Sable caught the lantern before it tore free. Iona told her to leave the new rules to Orin, but Sable said he was trying to stop the country from being eaten, and Iona replied that he had misunderstood the appetite.

Iona pulled the throttle, and the station boards strained like black wings. The train struck the west rail, sent Quill Crossing away with a crack of bells, and reflected a rose-colored morning the town had never known.

“You are coming with us,” Iona called.

Nessa folded the lantern closed and said she was not, because she had a westbound train to catch; when Iona reminded her that this was the last westbound train, Nessa replied, “That is my objection,” before remaining on the platform with the thread tucked into her coat and the red lantern swinging once in her hand.

* * *

In the postal car, passengers compared their abandoned lines by candlelight. The courier’s envelope held SHE DIED BETTER and part of an account of courage she expected Iona to fix before delivery.

The bride had written the groom’s name four times without writing the event that separated them. One inspector admitted that the last check was done from memory, while the goat carried a line beneath its collar: TELL HER I FORGOT THE WORTH IT COST ME.

Some scraps were cries, some were deals, and one said only, I was wrong about the sea. When Iona opened the bag to count the passengers, she found a dozen pieces although she had taken just one.

The empty cars were filling with words whose owners lived far away. At each stop, the country seemed to press its unsaid life into the last train, which moved between what people said and what they left alone.

Near the second hour, the radio tubes began to whisper, and a woman’s voice said, “Do not let her finish it.” When Iona reached for the dial, the static moved first and claimed that her mother had stopped because she knew the cost of the ending.

That was false, or false in the way Orin’s official endings were false: they were made from good material and put where a heart could not refuse them. When the radio said there was no road past the Chorus House, the horn cried again and Iona shut it off.

Through the glass, a valley opened in blue and white, with shanty towns climbing the slopes on songs that had never found a second line. No official road served them, so the railway was their only link, and its rails ended at a signal mast among flagstones.

The signal showed one word: NO. Iona rang the bell and stopped the train, finding a woman in a red conductor’s coat beside the mast, with tarnished buttons, white hair, and a violin case in one hand.

“Do not touch that,” Iona said.

“I was going to give it to you,” the woman said. When Iona replied that this was worse, she opened the case, which held a short length of rail bright as a drawn breath instead of strings.

“Juniper Vale,” Iona said, recognizing her mother’s last editor. Juniper had once pushed the national draft across a table and declared that a country must know where it ended.

“The blank is taking the marginal districts first,” Juniper said. “Bellwether is next, then every place without a Crown-approved ending, because the new rules forbid any ending not entered in the master record.”

When Iona said Juniper had helped write those rules, she answered that she had tried to stop Orin from sealing every living note under government stone. Behind them, the radio hissed and reported that Elia’s Coda remained blank.

“Orin’s approved endings can hold the core provinces,” Juniper said, “but every seal makes the next season more fixed. Soon there will be no second season, and the country will become the version everyone accepted first.”

Iona requested a concrete definition, and Juniper provided one: “No marriages, no children, no new songs, and perhaps no rain.”

As the brass rail pulsed beside Iona’s bag, Juniper ordered the train onto the chorus line. The Chorus House could hold the other districts until dawn, she explained, but beyond it the old Coda had room for one approved farewell that would restore the road and close the loss.

“Authorized by whom?” Iona asked.

“Your mother died without giving that power to anyone,” Juniper replied. “Orin believes the Crown can grant it, but I believe the answer is no.”

The radio spoke in Iona’s own voice and told her to say farewell and be rid of them. Juniper snapped the case shut, made the rail vanish, and warned her not to trust the train because it could make a false ending sound sincere.

“Iona,” Nessa’s thin but clear voice came from the signal mast, “if you can hear me, my westbound train is here.” Nessa stood nowhere, yet her signal lantern burned above the NO sign.

Looking at the first manuscript, Iona saw her mother’s last line written twenty years earlier in violet ink that the Coda Law now forbade: And when the last road has learned my name.

Nothing followed, though a Crown clerk had been in the Song House that night to copy the unfinished words for its files. Before he left, Elia Vale had asked her daughter to hear what came next.

Iona had heard the last measures before her mother’s voice began them. They were not a prophecy or a plan for rule, but a tuning of grief, departure, and an ending that left room for the next voice, which Iona could have heard perfectly.

Instead, Iona had said, “I won’t write what you mean.” Her mother looked disappointed rather than wounded, which was easier to bear, and before dawn she completed the line, then died and left Iona with the shape of an ending she had refused to learn.

Lifting the radio, Iona told Nessa she could hear her and asked her to hold the westbound signal. Nessa laughed through the static and said that after nineteen years of holding signals, she was taking a train with no destination, which would become a country of one.

The line went dead, and Iona tightened the verse bag around her hip. She ordered the engineer to couple the dead road toward the Chorus House, by force if the rails refused.

The NO signal spun around and came apart, and its bars returned as THIS ROAD NOT YET SUNG. Juniper stepped aside, allowing the last train to enter the marginal country.

* * *

The chorus line had been built for sound and punished for carrying it, as its rails climbed without a grade and curved around notes too large to see. Whole stations passed inside other stations: a platform occupied its own reflection, a waiting room sat inside a church, and travelers from the day before boarded or hid from the real train.

Saven stood on nine unfinished bridges, though its people called the ground solid. Nessa waited atop the signal cabin with her golden thread tied to the arm, while twenty-three people who had heard her radio call stood behind her.

“Westbound train ready,” Nessa shouted as the engine entered Saven. Iona warned her that such words were used to end journeys, and she replied, “I know.”

Iona asked whether the westbound train had both passengers and a place, and learned that it had the people of Saven. As a red burst crossed the sky, the bridges began vanishing from their ends inward, and the signal lever jumped toward NOTHING.

Nessa pulled the golden thread through both hands and bound the signal to HER OWN NAME, which stopped the lever. She said her destination was the place her sister had rejected when she left, and she would tell her sister why she was wrong, that she had stayed because she was afraid, and then something the dark thread cut off.

“I am not going to finish your verse,” Iona called. Nessa closed both hands around the failing light and chose the next part herself: she was angry because her sister had left and let Nessa believe that she did not care, while in truth she cared so much that she made the absence another thing to manage.

A bridge formed beneath the signal cabin, another took its place across the gap, and a third followed before Juniper shouted for the train to turn toward the Chorus House.

The engineer hauled the reversed wheel through both palms while the dead road bucked and rails leaped over the white breaks. Nessa’s declaration poured light into the couplings, and the people of Saven roared approval as the train gathered speed, leaving the signal tower on newly spoken ground.

* * *

The Chorus House stood at the center of a country that no longer agreed on its position. Its tower could be seen from every side, rising beyond the north stars and leaning through the south dusk.

Brass balconies circled the highest note, and on the widest balcony Orin Vey stood beside the Crown’s coda engine. It was a forest of wax cylinders turning around scores in iron ink, while approved endings locked one province to the next and the retreating blank exposed the saved towns, with their iron bells, fixed rain, and doors that opened only for approved callers.

A single route remained, because the platform beyond the engine was gone and the original Coda hung over the tracks as a violet line beginning on nothing and promising everything.

As Iona stopped the train, Orin came down the stairs, a fifty-three-year-old brother whose shared looks had been buried under neat clothes and official seals. He stopped at the yellow line and told her she was late.

“The country disagrees,” Iona said.

“The country is running out of song,” Orin answered while an archivist rolled a gilded cage onto the platform. The fragments in Iona’s bag shone inside it like trapped insects.

“Give them to me,” Orin continued, promising an approved farewell that would hold the line and save the districts. When Iona asked where this safe version ended, he pointed into a darkness so complete that the air looked dusty.

“Here,” Orin said, reminding her that she had lived in that darkness for twenty years before deciding her judgment mattered more than his. He made the accusation sound like fact because he knew how to turn pain into an argument.

Iona looked into the coda engine and heard the lives already sealed there: a Bellwether bride who would meet the same groom on the same evening, a child who would keep that child’s age forever, and rain falling at noon every noon with perfect care while teaching the ground nothing.

“Your ending is working,” Iona observed, and when Orin agreed, she asked why he was afraid. He looked toward the violet line and replied, “Because it will not save you.”

Iona passed the bag across the yellow line after taking out Bellwether’s closing words, the old woman’s note, and the inspector’s confession. Orin caught her wrist with the same grip he had used when they were children listening to their mother’s music through the studio wall.

“Those voices are not the answer,” Orin told her, “but the weight,” although Iona said she knew. When he accused her of expecting the train to forgive him if she made this public, she replied that the train would carry whatever happened.

The archivist opened the cage, and one by one the passengers came forward, beginning with a bride who refused to surrender her four versions of his name, folded them into a paper crane, and placed it in the speaking horn where the conductor could not alter it.

The doctor laid the wedding program at the coda engine’s feet, the inspector admitted that he had inspected from memory and tore his report in half, and the goat, which had somehow boarded the train, chewed the cord around its own unfinished line.

“The fare is one true thing,” Iona said, and while she watched them interpret that condition, no one asked her to complete a verse for them, for a farmer in an oiled coat admitted loving his brother’s wife, a railway cook confessed stealing bread during the war, and an old woman explained that her son had died angry because she answered his final letter with a recipe.

None of their confessions repaired what had happened, since each was simply returned to the mouth that had shaped it, and where a line remained incomplete, its owner let the silence belong to the pause.

The loose notes rose through the Chorus House without forming a melody, instead creating a room in which many unfinished things could remain alive, even as the Crown’s coda engine cracked under the pressure.

Orin seized the largest lever and warned that stopping it would let the blank take the capital, but Iona told him to stop it, rejected his accusation that she merely wanted a fault she could punish, and set his hand on the mechanism.

“Make the choice you are made of,” she told him, ordering him to sign and speak the words, while he warned that this would strip the Coda Act and allow every district to demand a national ending no government could contain.

“Yes,” Iona said; “it already contains them.”

The coda engine shuddered, a blue gap tore through the station roof as bright as noon after lightning, and the train’s wheels lifted half an inch from the rail before Orin pulled the lever and stopped the machine’s iron notes.

One iron bell rang through the capital from end to end, people looked down from galleries frozen for years, and the new air smelled of rain rather than oil as the blank rushed inward beyond the Chorus House and reached the last nine carriages of Iona’s train.

* * *

The railway was vanishing beneath them, because sleepers appeared for one minute and dissolved in the next, the engine pitched forward and rolled back, and the rails curled like ribbons torn from a book ahead of an unfinished Coda still waiting for a line no official voice had authority to write.

Iona climbed into the speaking horn, where the radio’s warning proved accurate: temptation would sound sincere, borrowing grief, courage, and humility until every possibility wore the exact face of a better self, so she began with the truth she had delayed for twenty years.

“I was not too young to hear my mother’s ending,” she said, and although the blank paused, Iona felt it listening rather than forgiving.

“I was young enough to imagine the ending belonged to me because I knew her music,” Iona continued, explaining that she had mistaken love for permission and understanding for authorship, so when her mother asked her to listen, she made her ask twice and gave her nothing the second time.

Behind her, Orin shouted for the engineer to reverse while wheels screamed against the vanishing track, but Iona held the horn and admitted that her mother had asked only for her listening, not for the writing of a farewell she had refused because she wanted to remain untouched by the country her mother had made.

“Then I took this train so I could keep listening without risking an answer,” she said, as the blank pressed against the engine and silver sparks ran through the speaking horn.

“I cannot finish her sentence with her permission because she is gone,” Iona declared, and no one alive could give her what Elia had withheld.

When Iona looked at the violet line and the country waited, she refused to call the song her mother’s alone, because the borrowed verses on the train had come from people afraid that their words would diminish the great work and the laws had called those words waste while giving them no place to speak.

The signal cabin far behind struck once with Nessa’s golden light, Bellwether answered from beyond the dissolving miles, and every district Iona had crossed, including the ones she had never named, returned a different unfinished sound.

“I leave you no final order,” Iona said, “only the fare we owe one another: to hear without taking, to answer without imprisoning, to make an ending when one is freely made, and to leave room when no one has.”

The Coda trembled above the rails, and Iona completed the unauthorized farewell by declaring that she was neither her mother nor her Crown, but the woman who had left her last measure unheard, and therefore could honestly give the country no lock.

She opened her mouth, and the train gave the final word.

* * *

There was no thunder, only a note that left the speaking horn low enough to feel in the ribs and passed through Orin, Juniper, every passenger, the iron engine, and the sleeping fields before entering the Coda not as a seal but as a gate.

The country answered, not in one voice, as Bellwether sounded its broken clock, Saven built another bridge, the orchard towns dropped their paper fruit for real pears, and people along official roads removed identical shutters and painted them different colors.

Rain reached the capital an hour after noon, washing iron ink from thousands of locked endings, while the blank withdrew to a pale strip beyond the last county because a country needed room to end one day and begin another.

The missing rails struck beneath the engine, and the last train rolled forward.

When Orin caught Iona as she climbed down from the horn, his arms were thinner than she remembered, and she allowed herself one breath in his embrace before stepping away, because he had told her that the Coda Act was void and she had replied that it needed rewriting with an opening line.

“An amendment to the amendment,” he said.

“Good,” Iona answered, though when Orin observed that some of the people who had confessed might ask him for payment, she refused to let the train’s fare become coercion and told him to give them room to refuse.

The train sounded its horn, Juniper found the coda engine’s master key and removed it, and workers carried the official scores to an ironworks, where they did not burn the registry but cut it into blank strips for districts to inscribe.

Iona returned to the engine with an empty verse bag, where a railway child held out a fresh scrap and explained that the station’s ending had disappeared because the mayor had written it.

When Iona handed the child a pencil and told them to wait for the mayor or for themselves rather than handing the work to a train, the child considered the advice with the solemnity only fresh wounds allow.

The last train carried the marginal districts home, and although some crossings waited for a charter from the capital and others built their signs themselves, Nessa waited at Saven beside the semaphore with her golden thread restored, explaining that a keeper who abandoned her post could hardly object when others did.

The line still ended at the western horizon, but it no longer ended in blankness, for the rails crossed an unmarked meadow where two roads joined without a town between them and, at dawn, a fresh sign beside the track announced SALT AND SONGS and BEAUTIFUL ROAD, COMING SOON.

Beneath it, a child was already carving a second name into the wooden post while Iona watched the letters deepen, the road wait for a place that had not yet learned its own shape, and the country beyond it continue.
