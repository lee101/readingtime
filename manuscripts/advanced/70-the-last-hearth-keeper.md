# The Last Hearth-Keeper

![Cover art: The Last Hearth-Keeper](../art/advanced/70-the-last-hearth-keeper.webp)

On the Thursday before Christmas, Nell Anyike did the round at twenty past four in the afternoon, because the round had been done at twenty past four since she was nine years old, and because the light goes at four in December and the fire is easier to read before it goes.

She carried the long poker and the round book and a tin thermometer on a string. She opened the grate, wrote the hour, wrote the barometric pressure off the wall, wrote the flame, which was four inches and a blue-white and lay over to the left, and she wrote the vent, which was drawing, which is the only entry in the book that has ever been in dispute, and then she shut the grate and signed the bottom of the page where the Keeper signs.

That was the round. Two hundred and six of them, and the book went back to 1913, and the earliest hand in it was not hers and was not a Keeper’s hand, and every page since had the same nine columns and the same nine columns had been enough for a hundred and eleven years, and on the shelf above the desk there was a framed certificate from a guild that had one member and no money, and the certificate said, in the guild’s own phrasing, that the fire was not to be let go out.

The notice was on the table under the round book, face down, because she had put it there on Monday and had not turned it over again, and the notice said that the Institute on Calder Street would be demolished in the last year of its permitted use, and that the date was in April, and that the Guild of Hearth-Keepers had been written to and had not replied, and that the fire services had been notified and would attend once a year.

* * *

The demolition engineer was called Emeric Hald and he was forty-five, and he had been on the Institute since he was twenty-six, and he met her in the doorway with his reading glasses pushed up into his hair because he knew her and had never once had to introduce himself.

“I have the order,” he said. “Signed in November. It is not a threat, it is a document with a date on it, and I would rather you had it from me than from a letter.”

“I have had it,” said Nell. “It was on my table on Monday.”

“Then you have read it.”

“I have read it four times.” She had the poker in her hand, because on Thursdays she is always holding the poker, and she had held it in front of the aldermen, and in front of the two men from the transit office, and in front of a vicar, and it was not a threat either; it was simply what you hold. “You have been nineteen months on this building.”

“Twenty-two,” Hald said. “I did the party wall survey in my first year and I have been putting off this one ever since, which is a long time to be putting off a wall.”

He let her in. The Institute had a lecture hall, two empty classrooms, a kitchen that had been a kitchen in 1891, and under the floor of the hall, four feet down, a brick vault the shape of a boat, with the district gas main running through the middle of it, live, sixty-one pounds an inch, and it had been live since 1856 and had been the single most important thing about the site since before either of them was born.

“The main is not going anywhere this year,” Hald said, kneeling by the grille in the floor with his hand flat on the brick. “That is the problem. Every year it has not gone anywhere, we have deferred the reroute, because the reroute and the demolition are in the same works programme and the city has never funded the second without the first.”

“So it is safe while you keep it.”

“It is safe while it is not opened,” Hald said. “Nobody opens a live vault. The whole of my professional life has been the arrangement by which nobody opens a live vault, and I have never once had to explain that to anybody, because there was a fire lit over it, and you were standing next to it, and you were not asking me questions.”

* * *

There was a woman on the bench outside the Institute, and the bench was put there by the council in about 1961 for the convenience of the bus stop, and the woman had been on it since half past two.

The bench carried a brass plate, and the plate was not a dedication but a timetable of the bus service, and the council had screwed it up in 1961 because people were tired of standing, and the woman had turned up on the Monday and had been on it every day since, through weather, through two changes of route and a rebuilt stop. The drivers knew her. The man on the early shift brought her a cup of tea once a week and had done for eleven years and had never asked her why, and the reason he had never asked was that the whole street had worked this out without putting it into words, which is that if you ask the woman on the bench she will have to explain, and there is no version of the explanation that anybody on that bus wants at seven in the morning.

She was seventy. She had a paper folded into four and a thermos and the expression of somebody who is not waiting for a bus. Nell had walked past her on the Thursday before and the Thursday before that, and the Thursday before that, and the woman had not spoken, and had not moved, and had not looked up, and Nell had assumed she was an old woman waiting for a bus that stopped at six.

“Mind if I sit down for a bit,” the woman said, at about half past four, when the round was done and the light had gone from the hall and the fire was on its own.

“If you are going to the bus stop,” Nell said.

“I am not,” said the woman. “I am not going anywhere until half past six. I am at the end of my two hours.”

* * *

Nell had been a Hearth-Keeper since the age of nine, and the whole of the initiation was that a grown woman put her on a stool and gave her the poker and said hold that, and Nell held it, and her arm went down, and the woman said that is yours now, and then there was tea.

The guild had resisted the demolition of the Institute for a hundred and eleven years, and it had never once done so in words. There was nothing to defeat. Twice a century somebody had sent a letter and the guild had acknowledged receipt, and the letters went into the round book at the back, and the round went on, and nobody had ever been able to point at a moment and say, this is what they did, because at no point had anybody done anything, and that was the whole of the strategy, and nobody had designed it.

The first entry in the round book was dated the eleventh of February, 1913, and it was not a round, and it read, in a hand taught copperplate in a board school, that the fire was lit at four in the afternoon, in the grate, in the middle of the floor, and that it was not to be let go out, and that no reason was written down because none was required. It had been the same nine columns every Thursday since, in six hands, and the succession of hands was how Nell knew the order of things: a woman from 1913 to 1949, a man from 1949 to 1971, a woman from 1971 to 2004, and then Nell, and after that a hundred and eleven years of Thursdays with nothing in them but the hour, the pressure, the flame, the vent, and the signature.

The guild had eleven members once, and a secretary, and a coat of arms with a lit grate on it. The secretary died in 1971. After that the guild was two people who came, and after that it was the woman, and after she died, in a nursing home in 2004, with no ceremony and nobody told, it was Nell.

She had two hundred and six Thursdays in the book and had never once asked what the fire was for.

On the third Thursday in January she asked.

* * *

She asked Hald, in the hall, with the grille open and the poker in her hand, and he answered her the way an engineer answers, which is with a date and a drawing.

“The gas escapes from a main into a space and pools at the ceiling, because it is lighter than air,” he said. “The vault is under this floor and the ceiling of the vault is the floor of this hall. If there were a fire lit in this grate, the flame would stand in the room exactly over the vault, and it would be the brightest thing in the building, and any man coming in here at night with a lamp would see it in a doorway, through a window, in fog, from the top of the street.”

He put his thumb on the brick edge of the grille.

“Suppose a man does not know this building. Suppose he is looking for a leak, or for a man. He comes into this hall in the dark and he sees a fire burning in a grate in the middle of the floor, and he does not think, this is a fire, this is a hearth, this is somebody’s job. He thinks, somebody is standing there, and there is light where the light should not be, and the light is where the air is bad, because the fire has come up through the vault and the vault is where the gas is, and nobody lights a fire over a bad place. He walks out of this hall and he does not go back into it until the room has been cleared.”

“And that is what the fire is for,” said Nell.

“That is a thing a fire can be for,” Hald said. “I am telling you how gas behaves. I am not the guild and I am not your office and I have no view on the fire.”

* * *

The woman on the bench had not moved when Nell came out at half past six, and the light was off the street by then, and the Institute was dark above the door, and the woman was in the dark and the fire was behind the wall, and Nell said, “You have been sitting there every Thursday for two months.”

“I have been sitting here every day for thirty years,” the woman said, “two hours, from half past two until half past six, and on Thursdays I do not go inside because you are inside.”

Nell stood in front of her. The woman was not large. She had the folded paper and she took it out and put it on the bench and did not open it, and then she opened it and it was a logbook page, a carbon copy, gone soft at the folds.

“Nineteen thirteen,” she said. “They found him in this hall. There was no fire, and there was a man in the room, and he was forty-one years old and he was the night watchman, and when they came to the door of the Institute he was standing in the middle of the floor with a lamp, and the lamp was still lit, and the gas came up out of the floor and got him before he got to the door, and the gas came up because he had opened the grille to look for a leak and had put his head over it.”

She turned the paper round. There was a figure on it, a plan, with a grate drawn in the middle of a hall.

“They wrote that if there had been a light burning in that grate the whole time he was in this room, he would have known the room was bad before he opened the grille, and he would not have opened the grille, and he would be alive.” She put a finger on the drawing. “It is not a church and it is not a shrine and nobody has ever said a prayer over it. It is a lamp for one man, on one night, put in the place where the next man would be standing, and it is not a metaphor and I am not sentimental about it, and I have kept two hours of it every day for thirty years and I intend to go on doing it as long as I can get up the steps.”

“The fire is not symbolic,” Nell said.

“No,” said the woman. “The fire is the instruction. It is an instruction that was never written on anything, and the guild took it up because the guild was there, and there is a girl in there now who has done two hundred rounds of it without knowing what it says, and I have been sitting on this bench for two hours a day for two months waiting for her to come out and turn round.”

* * *

The report was in Hald’s office, in the second drawer of the left-hand desk, in a brown file with 1913 on the spine in white paint, because of course it was.

She read it on the Sunday with the office lights on and Hald’s coat on the back of the chair and his survey drawings squared off on the desk, and it was four pages, and the first two were about gas, and the third was the plan, and the fourth was a recommendation, and the recommendation said that a permanent light should be maintained in the hall over the vault, and that the City Engineer’s office considered the matter a safety provision and not a matter of custom, and that it should be scheduled in the works programme as an ongoing item.

Nell read the fourth page three times and then she took her notebook out and copied the recommendation word for word, and the date at the top of it was the eleventh of February, 1913.

“It has been scheduled all along,” she said, when Hald came in on the Monday. “It is item nineteen. It has been item nineteen since 1913 and it has been carried in the works programme ever since and it has never been built.”

“It has been built,” Hald said. “It is a fire and a grate and a poker. It is the only item on that programme in a hundred and eleven years that has actually been delivered, and I have never been able to work out whether that is a compliment to the programme.”

He put his glasses on and took the file back and looked at it the way a man looks at a thing he has known about for twenty years.

“The reroute is funded,” he said. “It is in the works for next spring, and the Institute is inside the same programme as item nineteen, and the Institute does not come down while the main is live under it. That is not the guild. That is a schedule of works with my memorandum in it from 2004, and if your guild had ever argued with me for one hour I would have taken the file home.”

“Then the building stays,” said Nell.

“The building stays until the main is in the new trench and the vault is bricked up and the fire is out, and then I have the order and I will take the order down. In the meantime you bank it in the winter and keep it lit in the spring, and the gas is where it has always been, and item nineteen is not optional.”

* * *

She banked it on the twenty-fourth of December at twenty past four, which is the hour, because the round has never been done at any other hour.

Banking is not the same as putting out. You break the coal back, you draw the grate to a hand’s breadth, you close the ash pan and you leave a pilot the size of a thumbnail, and the flame goes down to a line of orange under the bars and stays there all night and all the next night, and a man can find it in a doorway from the top of the street on the worst night of the year, and it costs fourpence a week, and it is the most that may be done with it in a cold month, and it is enough.

Nell did the round. She wrote the hour and the pressure and the flame and the vent, and she signed the bottom of the page where the Keeper signs, and she wrote the date, which was a Wednesday, and then she did not go home, because it was the twenty-fourth, and the bench was there, and the woman was on it.

Mrs Trask stood up. She was seventy and she had two hours on that bench every day and she had done it since she was forty, and she took the folded carbon out of her coat and held it out, and Nell did not take it, and Mrs Trask put it back in her pocket, which took some doing.

“Every day somebody has to know where to look,” Mrs Trask said.

Nell took the round book out of her satchel. It is a large book and it is a hundred and eleven years old and it has been written on tables in three other offices, and she found the end of the year, and she wrote the nine words on the end of the year in her own hand, and she dated it, and she signed it, and then she shut the book.

The Guild of Hearth-Keepers has one member. It has a written record, now, and an item in a works programme, and a woman on a bench, and the fire under the floor of the hall burning down to a line of orange all night, four inches high by morning.
