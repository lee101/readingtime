# The House That Remembered Every Guest

![Cover art: The House That Remembered Every Guest](../art/advanced/84-the-house-that-remembered-every-guest.webp)

The Pre-Arrival Statement for 9 Ferryman’s Row reached Nell Ambrose by post on the twenty-ninth of March, three days before she reached the door by train, and it ran to two sides and did not greet her.

The first line gave her full name, date of birth, and occupation, which was correct on all three counts, and the house had taken the trouble to say where it had got them: her bank, her doctor, the bindery on Cauldron Lane where she had repaired library bindings for two years. The second line described what she would be doing when she arrived. “You will come between four and six in the afternoon with one suitcase,” it said, “and you will be hungry, and you will not have slept properly since the fourteenth, and you will decide within ten minutes of crossing the threshold whether this was a mistake.”

There was no signature. At the foot of the second side, in a small upright copperplate that had never once been in a hurry, the house had written its own name and the date, and under that, PRINTED FOR THE OCCUPANT, and under that, in the same upright hand, PLEASE NOTE THAT WE CANNOT BE SILENT.

Under that, the statement set out the things the house was not able to do, which she had neither asked for nor needed. It could not lie. It could not lose a face, a name, a date of arrival, or the particular thing a person had been doing at the moment they came in. It could not be alone itself, and it had tried twice, in 1994 and again in 2003, and had found the arrangement unbearable on both occasions and had gone back to being unbearable in company.

The house had been built in 1831 for a man who made combs. It had had three hundred and four tenants in ninety-four years, which the statement gave as a figure of some pride, and beside it, in the same upright hand, it gave the average length of a tenancy as two years and five weeks, and then the rent, and then the nearest comparable rent, and let the two figures sit there together without a word of comment.

* * *

The rent was three hundred and forty pounds a month for a first-floor flat with two bedrooms, a front room, a back room, a bath with a bath in it, and a cellar, in a street where the going rate that spring was nearer seven hundred. Nell had done the arithmetic on the platform at Doncaster with the suitcase between her feet, and she had taken it, because at twenty-six she had been living in other people’s houses since she was fifteen and had learned that a cheap door is almost always the same as any other cheap door.

The front room was as described, which was the part that took her breath. There were six clocks on the mantelpiece, all of them working, all of them showing a different hour, and a hat on every hook, and a dining table laid for six with a cruet and a bread board and a small grey card that said, THE TABLE IS LAID WHENEVER THE HOUSE HAS MORE THAN ONE PERSON TO LAY IT FOR. There was a coat on a hook by the window, dry, with the shoulders still holding their shape, and it smelled of rain that had stopped eleven years before.

She put her suitcase down in the middle of the floor and did not open it, and then she sat in the chair by the window because it was the only chair that did not have somebody’s shape in the seat, and at half past four the light went off the mantelpiece and the standing lamp came on by itself, warmly, at exactly the level it had been left.

She went down to the kitchen at six because she was, as the statement had said, hungry, and there was a plate on the table under a plate, and the plate under the plate was a full plate, with a fork and a knife laid out beside it and an inch of something in a glass. She ate one mouthful standing up, then put the plate in the sink and washed it, because it did not seem to her the sort of plate a stranger should carry away in her hand.

Nobody came in. The room simply became occupied.

* * *

On the Tuesday she went down to the practice of Marchant & Deele, which occupied the ground floor of a former ironmonger’s on Chapel Street and smelled of a different kind of old paperwork. A young man called Deakin Rees took her into a room the size of a wardrobe and offered her a chair, and she told him the address, and he stopped with the file half open.

“Ferryman’s Row,” he said. “You have had the statement.”

“Yes.”

“And you have understood that the house is entered in the register of persons at that address.”

“There is a house in the register of persons,” Nell said, “and it has my kitchen in it.”

“It has,” Deakin Rees agreed. “Mr Grieve bought the freehold in the nineteen-eighties and died nine years ago, and his estate still holds it, and the estate cannot sell it, and the reason the estate cannot sell it is that the freehold cannot be transferred out of the tenancy without the consent of a person who is not the deceased and does not attend meetings.” He closed the file. “We act for Mr Grieve. We also, in a very small way, act for the house. The house has never instructed us. It posts letters.”

“It posted me a letter,” Nell said, “describing my journey.”

“It does that to everyone,” said Deakin Rees. “Three hundred tenants in ninety years. The schedule is at clause nine, and the schedule is enforceable, and the effect of clause nine is that the house will not permit any person to be alone in any room the house is occupying.”

“So it follows me about.”

“It keeps you company,” he said. “That is the whole of its intention, and it is a very good intention, and it is the reason the rent is three hundred and forty, and the reason the rent is three hundred and forty is that four people this year have looked at the standing order and given the deposit back.”

“Why would they do that?” Nell said.

“Because it is a good flat and nobody wants a good flat with a witness in it,” said Deakin Rees. “There is no privacy clause in this lease. There is a company clause, the company is mandatory, it does not stop at midnight, and it has no off switch. The house is not being malicious. It is being itself, and I have spent four years explaining that to people who have already decided that they are mad.” He turned a page he did not need to turn. “You are the first person to ask about the freehold before the tribunal.”

* * *

The woman in the back room was seventy-one and had been there nineteen years, and her name was Ines Harrow, and she was the only other person Nell saw that week who was not a piece of furniture.

They met on the landing, because the landing is where the house introduces tenants, and on the Thursday it had introduced Nell to four people, none of whom were present. “Mr Rafe Ostley, who was in the bath,” Ines said. “No. He is not in the bath.” “He is in the bath,” Ines said, “and has been since April the third, 2016, and you will find him when you run the water, and you should have your own way of doing that.”

Below them the cellar held what the house thought a person needed. Nineteen trunks. Thirty-one hats. A hamper of letters tied with tape, each bundle in a different decade. Four hundred rooms’ worth of other people’s evenings, shelved and labelled in the copperplate hand, every evening entered in its hour and its weather, and none of it discarded, because 9 Ferryman’s Row is not a building that forgets.

“Does it mind?” Nell asked. “Having all that in it?”

“It is proud of it,” Ines said. “It has never had a tenant who stayed. Do not confuse that with a house that is difficult. It is the easiest house in this city and it has no idea what that costs.”

“How do you bear it?” Nell said.

“I do not, most weeks,” said Ines Harrow. “I have a television on too loud and I talk to the walls, and I have made arrangements with the front room, which is that after eleven at night it may have me company and not ask what I am doing. That is an agreement between two adults and I recommend you make one. The first three years I could not sleep for the noise, because a house that will not leave you alone is a house that is always slightly occupied, and the difference between a neighbour and a memory is that a neighbour stops when you want it to.” She lifted the lid off the sugar bowl and looked at what was in it. “Nineteen years. I have a key. If I put it on the mat and go, it lets me in the same night, and it has never once pretended otherwise, and that is worth a great deal more than privacy, and I have never worked out how to say so without sounding ungrateful.”

* * *

She ran the bath on the Sunday, because she wanted to be warm and by herself for forty minutes, which was a thing she had been unable to arrange for eleven years.

There was a man in it, and he gave his name before she had the towel up, and it was Rafe Ostley, and then he apologised for the bath.

He was about forty and he was in shirtsleeves with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, and he was sitting forward with his forearms on his knees in the way of a man who has been told to wait, and the water was not running, and the water was up to his ankles and cold. When she screamed, which she did, he said, “I am terribly sorry about the bath,” and then, when she was standing in the doorway with the towel in her hand, “You can turn the cold off, I do not mind at all. It is only that I have to be in it while the watch is in here.”

The watch was on the soap dish. It was a steel-cased wristwatch with a crack across the crystal and a leather strap gone black, and it had been on that dish since the third of April, 2016, and it was the only reason the house had ever needed to hold on to a man.

“He left it in the bath,” Nell said.

“He did. I was in a hurry. I had a van at eight and I did not want the thing to go off, and I put it down in the tub to wash the muck off my hands, and I did not come back for it, because I did not think of it again for eleven years,” said the man, “and then I thought about it again, and here I am. I am extremely sorry. You cannot see the door from where I am sitting, and that is not a complaint, it is only a fact about the room.”

“And if I take the watch out?”

“Then the house will have finished with me,” he said, “and I expect I can go, and I expect I will want to, and I have thought about that for a long while and I do not think I would like it. I am not asking you to take it out. I am asking you to understand that nobody has ever asked me.”

He said it without any weight at all, the way a man says a thing he has had a great deal of practice saying, and Nell understood then that he had said it to every tenant who had ever come into that bathroom in eleven years, and that not one of them had stayed long enough to be told the rest of it.

* * *

The house would not let her be alone. That was its gift, it was the single thing it had been built to do, and it had never once asked her, and in ninety years three hundred people had lived under it and not one of them had ever wanted it, which was a fact about the house and also, she understood at about eleven o’clock on the Sunday night, a fact about herself.

She went to Marchant & Deele on the Monday and asked what happened if a tenant stopped paying.

“It goes to a tribunal,” said Deakin Rees, “in eleven weeks, and you lose, because the house is a party and the house does not lose and the house has three hundred exhibits.”

“And if the house sold?”

“It cannot. The freehold is Grieve’s, and Grieve has been dead nine years, and the estate is insolvent for the cost of an exorcism and the house will not be sold out from under an occupant.”

“Then I will buy it,” Nell said.

Deakin Rees put down his pen. “The estate will want a buyer by March,” he said. “If you buy it, you own a building with three hundred names of record and a person upstairs. What would you do with it?”

“I am going to renovate it for people who will not be able to be alone in it,” said Nell Ambrose, and meant it, and had no idea what those words weighed.

Before she left the office she filled in a second form, at clause fourteen, which she had read twice on the train, and it was one line long: I hereby apply for the front room to be unaccompanied between four and six on weekdays. The house took nine weeks to answer. It answered on the fourth of August, in copperplate, and it said that it would try, and that the front room was very fond of the six o’clock hour, and that it would want a little more notice than that, and that in the meantime the applicant was to understand that the house was not being difficult, it was only unused to being asked.

* * *

It took nine months, and none of it was interesting, and all of it was the work.

The estate’s solicitors found a schedule of pre-emption in a box of receipts, which gave the house a right of first refusal that nobody had ever triggered, and Nell had to buy the freehold in two steps and get the house to sign the second step, and the house signed it, in copperplate, on the fourth of June, with a note: WE HAVE NO OBJECTION, THOUGH WE SHOULD LIKE THE MATTER OF THE CELLAR TO BE DISCUSSED.

The estate took until November to agree a price, and the price was low, and the house wrote to every listed owner of the property to say that it had been sold, and one of them, a woman in Cheltenham who had been a tenant for one year in 1991, wrote back to say that she had been charged eighteen pounds for the service of the notice. Deakin Rees paid it and entered it in the file as a cost of the freehold, and Nell found out from the accounts in January and paid it back.

Three hundred and eleven names of record had to be regularised. Eighty-one were dead. Twenty-nine were abroad. Nineteen had been tenants for more than three years and did not know there were three hundred and ten others, and each of those was given a letter by hand, because Deakin Rees said a letter about this thing should be carried by somebody, and each was given an evening in writing.

The Register of Evenings began in July. Every room was assigned a declared company: a named evening, a named hour, a named weather, agreed by the person whose evening it was, who could vary it and who could withdraw it on fourteen days’ notice, and who was told, in writing, what would happen in the room when the evening was withdrawn, which was that the room would be left vacant and locked, and that no one would enter it, and that this was the only thing the house had ever been asked to do that it could not do well, and that it would do it anyway.

Ostley was the hard one. He sat in the meeting in the bathroom for fifty minutes on the eleventh of September, in a chair Deakin Rees had carried up from the front room, and he was so reasonable about it that Nell had to look at the table. She had come in with the release drawn up, the one that would have had the watch removed and the evening ended in a fortnight, and she did not put it on the table, and afterwards she wrote in the file that she had been unable to find a reason for it that was good enough to say out loud to a decent man over a cracked watch.

“I would like to keep the bath evening,” he said. “Not because I like the bath. Because the house is the only thing that has kept track of me since the third of April, 2016, and I would rather be a person of record in a house that knows where I am than a man in a room that does not.” He turned the cracked watch over on the soap dish. “And if I ever hand this in, you are to tell me first, and not put it in a drawer, and I am to be the one to say the day.”

* * *

On the fourth of January, at four in the afternoon, Nell Ambrose sat down in the front room of 9 Ferryman’s Row with a cup of tea and nothing to do until six.

She had locked the front room, which she had never done in a house that was not empty, and had put the key in her pocket, and had told the house in writing what she wanted, in the form at clause fourteen, with the hour and the date and the room, and the house had consented in copperplate on the eleventh of December, and had added, WE SHALL RESPECT IT.

At four o’clock the standing lamp did not come on by itself. The clocks on the mantelpiece, all six of them, went to four together and stayed there. The kettle in the kitchen, which had been going to whistle, did not. Somewhere below her, a bath that had been occupied since April the third, 2016, went quiet with a man still sitting in it; a landing held its breath between two tenants having an argument about a bicycle shed; and three hundred and ten other evenings in four hundred rooms’ worth of other people’s lives sat down where they were and waited, politely and unmistakably, to be let in.
