# The War of the Last Two Cartographers

![Cover art: The War of the Last Two Cartographers](../art/advanced/62-the-war-of-the-last-two-cartographers.webp)

The Crown’s notice arrived on a Tuesday in January, folded twice and sealed once, carried up the outside stairs by a clerk of the Ordnance Secretariat, who thanked Ivo Sarn for his service and declined, when invited, to sit down. The notice declared the office of cartography discontinued at the close of the quarter, dissolved the register of licensed surveyors, and required the surrender of instruments, plates and copper against receipt. Ivo read it twice at his kitchen table. The word used for all of it was modernisation.

He had been licensed in the year of the first ordnance survey, and in forty-one years he had drawn the Brunn-Osten frontier four times, once for each convention that a different ministry preferred. The fourth sheet, two metres wide, hung in his front room under varnish gone the colour of weak tea. Its border ran from the black water at Corran Mouth to the notch above Harrow Hill in four colours, because four ministries had each required the line in their own ink, and he had obliged all four rather than lose the work.

What the notice did not contain was a reason, and Ivo was a man who thought in reasons. A decision without a reason was a component rather than a policy, and a component could be queried. He asked the clerk, who was twenty-three and glad to be helpful, which instrument dissolved the register. Not the Secretariat, said the clerk; that lay with the Crown, exercised by warrant eleven days earlier. Ivo asked for the minute number. The clerk said it would come in the post, in the voice of a man carrying a great many such promises.

The guild had admitted forty-one licensed surveyors in his year. Thirty-nine were dead or retired, and the fortieth had handed in his own theodolite in the same week, without objection, having been pensioned at sixty. Ivo had watched the profession disappear the way a queue disperses: not in argument, but one clerk at a time, each given a form and a reason, until the form said there had never been a queue.

In the second week of January he went down to the river with a case holding the theodolite he had bought in 1981, a field book of ruled pages, the treaty of 1816 in a calfskin binder, and a bar of soap. Nadia Bex, thirty-four, held the eastern survey for the League of Osten and was the only other licensed cartographer he knew of. He had corresponded with her twice, both times about drainage, and had never met her.

* * *

The village of Wendle sat where the river bent, and it had been inside two tax jurisdictions since 1816, because in 1816 a convention made to settle a dispute about a mill had been followed so faithfully for two centuries that the village had grown around the mistake like a bone around a splinter. Ivo arrived on a Tuesday, walked in from the north, and was met at the post office by Merrit Ollo, who kept the ledgers.

She was fifty-one, appointed by the first jurisdiction in 1993 and retained without a contest by the second, which had found the arrangement cheap and the woman better than any successor she might have been allowed to appoint. She showed him the two books on one table, chained to a ring in the same wall. In the left, three hundred and six households, a water rate, a road rate, and an entry for every head. In the right, the same three hundred and six households, the same water rate, and half the heads. A village counted twice learns to count carefully, and this one had been careful for two hundred years.

The good road was the one nobody would pay for. Ivo asked about the Harrow road, paved in 1861 on a consent that required both jurisdictions to agree, and in 1994 one of them had raised a technical objection that voided it. The road had been resurfaced once with gravel and twice with money from a flood fund. The other road, the river road, had been unmade since 1888 and both jurisdictions resurfaced it every spring, each able to watch the other doing the same from its own windows. “Two masters,” Merrit said. “Two sets of potholes, and a great deal of gravel. We eat better than one master would allow.”

They ate, Ivo understood, by the double entry, and had been eating by it so long that the village had no memory of eating otherwise. He asked what she thought a border was for. She said, “Where my two keys are kept, and what I say in which book.” Then she unlocked the ring, showed him the second ring, which was identical and stood on the other side of the wall, and said that since 1816 the village had owned two of everything, including the ring.

* * *

On Harrow Hill the next morning, above the village, Nadia Bex was already at the old trig point with a red peg in the turf, a folding chair, a sun umbrella, and a tide table she had no use for. She stood when he came and offered him the second chair, and they sat on either side of the peg like surveyors on both sides of a boundary, which is what they were. The treaty of 1816 was short. Thence by the middle of the Wend to the notch of Harrow, she said. “Your ministry holds that the middle is the thalweg, the deepest channel.”

“In 1971. In 1888 it was the west bank. In 1931 the Ordnance Office held it was neither, and gave up.”

“The river is braided at the bend. The middle is a bar. It was on the east side in your grandfather’s time and on the west in mine, and both of those are correct, and I have the level books to prove the second and your own field books to prove the first.”

That was the whole of it, and it had been the whole of it for nine years. She said the words neither of them had ever been able to say in writing, that the river had a bar and a cut bank and four channels in a kilometre, and that a man who drew a single line through all of that was drawing a preference and calling it a treaty. Ivo told her that a man who drew four lines was drawing a country and calling it a survey. Both statements were true and had been exchanged, in writing, four hundred times.

Their war had no armies and no season of fighting. It had expense accounts, counter-petitions, boundary stones moved and re-moved, a joint commission in the fourth year that recommended further work, and two instrument cases carried by two patient men up a great deal of gravel. In nine years neither had lost an argument. They had each won every one of them, at their own tables, on their own soil, and the frontier had not moved a metre, because a frontier only moves when one of the two things claiming it agrees.

“You have three hundred people on the wrong side,” she said.

“You have them on a line you drew to suit a treasury that wants the mill rights.”

“I want the bar, Mr Sarn. The mill drowned in 1897.”

On the Wednesday the Crown’s surveyor set up on the north bank, and Ivo walked down to watch, because a theodolite set on a stranger’s ground is worth looking at. He was nineteen, in a coat two sizes too large for him, with a grandmother’s tripod and a warrant in a brass tube. He sighted the ridge, wrote the reading, wrote the time and the temperature, and Ivo, standing behind him, understood at once that this boy was not going to be talked to, because a boy who writes down the temperature is a boy with instructions.

Ivo said, quietly, that he should stop. He said it as a courtesy, in the tone of one man warning another about a bog, and gave his reasons, which were his own and were honestly held. “The instruction says the middle of the Wend. It does not say the channel. You are not measuring the river. You are measuring two men’s opinions with a piece of brass, and a court will hang nine years of work on whatever you write.”

“The instruction is dated the eleventh,” the boy said, still reading. “The braiding is in it, and I have made a note of it, and the note has a date on it, and the date is the eleventh.”

“It has four, in a kilometre,” Ivo said. “It has had four since 1888.”

“I am aware of the braiding, sir. It is in my instructions.” The boy did not look up. “The instruction says the survey shall be conducted without regard to any claim, and I have put both claims in the book, and the book is open, and I will read it to either of you at any hour you like.”

A week later Nadia found him at the same station and said her piece, and her reasons were also her own, and they were good. If the Crown drew a line, the League’s argument would die with it, because a frontier drawn by a survey is a frontier there is, and there is nothing left for a smaller state to argue with. “I am not asking you to lie,” she said. “I am asking you to leave a question open.”

“The instruction has no box for that,” Tobin Vray said.

* * *

The Crown’s own memorandum, dated two years before the notice and circulated to four ministries, put the reason plainly. Nine years of survey expenditure had produced two mutually exclusive frontiers; a frontier that cannot be fixed cannot be taxed, and a frontier that is drawn can be. Ivo read it in a records room in Corran with a clerk who fetched it without comment, and he took off his glasses, and he was not surprised, and the not-surprising was the worst part.

He went down to Osten that autumn and told Nadia Bex, in a hotel of the League with the standards of a hostel, that the Crown had not abolished cartography for progress. It had abolished it because two licensed men had spent nine years proving, correctly, at the state’s expense, that the state’s own treaty was wrong. A government cannot keep a service whose product is a disagreement. She agreed with him at once, and then said the thing neither of them could say in a hotel of that standard, which was that they were both right, and that being both right was all they had left.

“You cannot act on it,” she said.

“Neither can you. I need you wrong. You need me wrong. We have been waiting nine years for the other of us to be wrong.”

“Yes.”

“We could both be wrong, of course. That is also available.”

“Yes. It is the only outcome in which this is over by Friday.”

* * *

The flood came in the ninth year in the second week of March, and it came at night, and by morning the Wend had taken the east bank below the mill and put a mile of the old channel into a field. Ivo and Nadia met at the bend at eight in the morning, wet to the thigh, and worked the new water together without speaking, because there was nothing to argue about and both of them knew the numbers before they had them.

By the afternoon they had the same answer. The bar of 1816 was under nine feet of water on the far side, and the deep channel that the Ordnance thalweg had followed since 1971 was a dry gravel island with willows on it, and a third channel, an oxbow older than the treaty, held the water that everybody actually used. The border was not where the treaty said. It had never been where the treaty said. Nine years of expensive correctness had arrived, after all that, at a place where both of them could sit down.

Which left the instruction, and the boy, who had been sent up the hill on the second of March with a sealed addendum requiring the survey to be concluded within the month. Both of them had already asked him to stop, separately, for their own reasons, and he had gone on doing the work, and neither of them had ever asked him why. Ivo stood in the wet grass and looked at the book open on the tripod and said what needed saying, and said it once. “You each drew it where you wanted it, Mr Vray. That is all this is. You have two rivers, and you have both been right for nine years, and neither of you was arguing about water.”

Tobin Vray said nothing for a while. Then he took from the case a sheet of tracing material, laid it over Ivo’s fourth sheet, and drew the river as it was: the bar, the cut bank, the four channels, the oxbow, the island with the willows, and the moving middle, which is to say he did not draw a middle, because there was not one, and he ruled it as such and wrote the date and the temperature.

Nadia laughed. It was one syllable and it was not unkind. “Both of us told you to stop.”

“You did.”

“And we were each protecting a house. He is protecting a book.” She put her hand flat on the case. “Nobody has ever been neutral at anything in this valley, and now there is a boy of nineteen with a warrant and a good instrument and no house at all, and he will draw it correctly, and neither of us will be able to touch the drawing, and that is not fair.”

The boy’s pen did not stop. “I am a Crown surveyor,” he said. “I do not have a house in this matter.”

* * *

The order that came in April named the instrument. The line shall be established on the basis of the drawing made by the surveyor, Tobin Vray, and not upon any sheet previously submitted by the licensed cartographers, whose registration stood dissolved. Ivo read it in the front room, standing, with his own two-metre sheet on the wall behind him in four colours, and then he took the sheet down, which he had not intended to do and could not afterwards explain.

They told the village on a Thursday. The line went down the middle of Wendle because that was where the middle was, and the treaty had said so in 1816, and two hundred years of clerks had assumed it was describing something else. The Crown’s party set whitewashed stakes at nine-metre intervals. There were forty-seven inside the village. The line came up out of the river at the old ferry, took the churchyard wall at the third course of brick, passed between the houses numbered four and five, went under the school steps, and left by the boundary of the two ledgers.

Merrit Ollo unlocked the ring in the wall and then had to unlock the second ring, on the other side, because the line, when it came, put a foot of whitewash between the two rings that had been eleven inches apart since 1816. She stood with her hands on both. The village divided one hundred and fifty-eight households to the west jurisdiction and one hundred and forty-eight to the east, and the allocation was made by the Crown’s assessor, who apologised twice and did it anyway.

What divided was the arithmetic. The Harrow road had never been paid for and could not be, because one half of it lay in a jurisdiction that would have to consent; the river road, unmade since 1888, now fell entirely to the west side, and both halves of the village used it daily. The ring in the wall had become a boundary fixture and could not be paid for twice. Merrit’s two books were the property of one master, and the other appointed a new keeper in eleven days, and she was competent.

Within a month the two halves wrote to the Crown. The letters were identical: the same petition formula, the same complaint about the river road, the same request that the line be moved, and the same misspelling of jurisdiction in the fourth line, which Merrit had made in her own letter and which the schoolmaster had carried into his, because there was one literate hand in Wendle and a boy of eleven who could copy it exactly. Ivo saw the two side by side in a Secretariat file in the autumn, on one sheet, and the officer who handed them to him observed that it was a striking thing for two opposed parties to want the same thing in the same words.

“It is not the same thing,” Ivo said. “They want the line moved north. Each of them wants the other half of the village back.”

* * *

Nadia Bex came up the outside stairs in October, on her own time, having crossed four frontiers to do it, and sat in the kitchen where a Crown clerk had declined to sit in January. Ivo made tea, and then found that the tin was the last tin, and that he had been keeping it since the spring in a cupboard as a man keeps a bottle for an occasion, and that the occasion had passed unremarked.

They divided it. He poured and counted the cups, because there were two, and each of them wanted the stronger half and neither would say so, and they solved it the way they had solved nine years of a frontier, by measurement, and the measurement was fair, and both were dissatisfied.

Then they went through the facts, in order, the way a commission goes through a return. The treaty had said the middle of the Wend, and the middle of the Wend had been a bar, and the bar had moved, and every man who had drawn a single line had drawn a preference. Ivo had been right in 1888 and Nadia had been right in 1971 and the Crown had been right in 1931 in declining to choose. The village had been in two jurisdictions because two jurisdictions repaired two sets of bad roads. The profession had not been abolished for progress. It had been abolished because two licensed men had been right, publicly, for nine years, at the state’s expense.

“Yes,” Nadia said, at the end of it, to the last of it, and to the rest of it.

“Yes,” Ivo said. “We agree.”

“Nine years.”

“Eleven, if you count the two years before you were appointed, which you were, in writing, in a letter I have kept.”

“Then we agree, and the line is drawn, and the boy is at a survey of the northern marches, and Merrit Ollo is under a new master, and it is a Tuesday. None of it does any good.”

“It does not.”

“You can say the rest of it. I have read your file. You are going to say that it was not a waste.”

“I am not going to say that.”

“You are going to say something worse, then, and I would rather hear it before the tea is cold.”

Ivo refilled her cup, because hers was lighter, and because the tin was his, and because there was nothing else in the room that anybody needed from him. “The bar is where it is,” he said. “Two hundred and eleven to the acre on the first shoal, two hundred and ninety on the second. In a hundred years the water will have made its own decision, and the mark will be ten metres from mine, and it will be correct, and nobody will be able to argue with it.”

“That is the surveyor in you,” Nadia said.

“That is the man who has nothing left to hold.”

She took the cup, and drank it, and put it down on the cloth beside his, and did not say anything else for a while, because there was a great deal that had been true for nine years.
