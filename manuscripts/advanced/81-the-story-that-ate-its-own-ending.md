# The Story That Ate Its Own Ending

![Cover art: The Story That Ate Its Own Ending](../art/advanced/81-the-story-that-ate-its-own-ending.webp)

The city of Loom had a district that people would not name, and the reason they would not name it was not the reason Perrin Marsh expected when a woman on the Ninth Stair told him, in the voice people use for weather, that she did not feel like it lately.

He was twenty-nine and he worked in the Harbour Memory, which was the room where the city kept the things it had agreed to remember, and which had a counter, a ledger, and four clerks, and which was called the Memory because calling it anything else would have implied that it chose. His own work that week had been the quarterly re-copying of the summary of the founding of Loom, which was nine lines long and had not changed since the office was founded and which he copied out eleven times a day for people who came in with a complaint and left with a sentence.

The city of Loom was the inside of a library, which meant its streets were aisles and its weather was the temperature of a building in August. The Harbour was the returns desk, and it was where the city came when a book came home, and it was busier than the market and had been for a hundred and forty years. North of it the Long Room had the high windows and the best chairs and the least company, and above the stacks the Ninth Stair, which was the shortest route in the city to nothing that anybody wanted.

A place that is read is kept. That was the whole of the arrangement and it had been signed by nobody, because it had simply always been true, the way a floor is true. Perrin had been born in Loom and had never been told it and would not have believed you.

“A place that is not read is not unremembered,” the woman said. “It is thinner. You ask it something and it gives you the shape of the answer.”

“Which district,” said Perrin.

“The one you are standing in.”

* * *

The Chronicle of the Salt Kings had been the most-read work in Loom for one hundred and forty years, and it was nine volumes, and it was legally a person, and it held a lease. Its registered address was Stack S-9 on the Ninth Stair, its tenant was itself, and it could be sued, cited, and issued with demands, and once in 1788 it had been issued with a demand for its own return, which it failed to answer, and the fine had been paid out of the fines fund with a note that the fund was not to do that again.

The lease was a real lease, with a term of nine hundred years and a rent of one reading a year, and it was signed at the foot of the shelf in the library’s own hand, and the shelf was not a metaphor for a bookcase, it was a shelf. In 1791 volume six had gone missing and the office had issued a demand for its return, and the demand had been answered on the seventh day, and the answer had come in the volume’s own writing, and the minute records that the office accepted the delivery without comment and adjusted nothing.

Volume nine had not been read for four years. It was nine hundred pages long, the good part of it was the first two hundred, and the rest of it was the kind of writing that a city reads in order to be seen reading. Perrin had the borrowing record in front of him, because a man named Osei Brill had come into the Harbour with a complaint about a fine, and the complaint was not the kind they usually got.

Borrowed: volume nine. Borrower: a reader, resident, name withheld in the minute because the borrower had asked. Date: four years and two months ago. Returned: never. Progress as recorded by the borrower’s own slip, in pencil, at the foot of the page: 1 to 200. 201: no. 202: no, and then nothing at all for four years, in a neat column of ruled no.

“You did not finish it,” said Perrin.

“No.”

“There is no penalty for not finishing. There is no penalty for anything. That is the whole of the Library Act.”

“That is what I have understood,” said Brill, “and I would like it written down that I understood it.”

* * *

The librarian was Ivet Salter and she was sixty and had not read a novel in thirty years, which she mentioned in the first minute, unasked, in the manner of a person who has been asked too many times. She read the instruments, the leases, the returns, the succession of the Reading, and the marginal notes, and she had read one novel at twenty-nine and had given it up in a fortnight and had been tired for a year afterwards.

She was also, by the custom of the office, the only person in Loom who could be summoned by a work. Perrin had put the request in writing, in the form the office used, which was a form, because the city had found in the nineteenth century that the only way to be heard by a book was to fill in a box. On the desk in front of her was a sheaf of paper gone the texture of cloth.

“Nine volumes,” she said. “Eight are sound. Nine is four hundred and ninety pages, and it has been losing them from the back for four years, and the first three hundred of those were legible when it came off the shelf, and I have been putting them back in order every spring and they are not there in the spring.”

“By which you mean it is being destroyed.”

“By which I mean it is not being kept.” She squared the sheaf. “Everything in this city is kept by being read. That is not a figure of speech, it is the founding arrangement, and it is why we do not lock the doors and why we do not mend pages, and why the district you cannot name has no name in it, because a name is a page and a page is a page. Read the city and the city stays. Stop, and it does not go anywhere. It goes thin. That is all it does, and it is enough.”

Perrin said, “Then somebody should read volume nine.”

“Two hundred and ninety-four people have asked the counter for it in four years,” said Ivet Salter, “and thirty-one of them were sent to volume eight, and I have stopped sending them, because sending them was the last thing I did that made any difference at all, and it is not enough either.”

* * *

Volume eight ended, as it had ended for a hundred and forty years, with a box. The box was printed, and the print was nineteenth-century, and the words in it were: THE READER IS REQUESTED TO STATE THEIR PREDICTION OF THE NINTH VOLUME. There was space under the words, and the space had been filled in, in ink, by a committee in 1802, and the committee’s sentence had been copied into the Harbour Memory and recopied every quarter ever since.

Perrin had written it. He had been twenty-four and a copyist, and the prediction was four sentences long, and it said that when the ninth volume was finished there would be no tenth, and that a city sustained by an unfinished thing could not survive the finishing of it, and that the reading of Loom would therefore cease on the day the last page of the Chronicle was read, and that the Harbour would remain open, because somebody would have to.

He had not opened volume nine since the day he wrote that. There was no rule against it and no one had ever asked him, and once a year the form came round the counter for the annual attestation and he signed it without reading the four sentences, and once a year somebody in the room said the number of pages in volume nine out loud, and once a year Perrin found a reason to be in the Long Room.

“The founding is in nine,” he said.

“The founding is on the last leaf of nine,” said Ivet Salter. “In the year the library opened, in a room with the same floor, a founder read the charter of Loom aloud, and the city is the people who heard it and understood it. There is no other clause. Nine hundred pages, and the whole city is in the last forty of them, and the last forty of them went in the spring.”

“And the Memory has nine lines about it.”

“The Memory has nine lines about it because that is all the founding has ever been reduced to, and the nine lines are a paraphrase of page eight hundred and sixty, and page eight hundred and sixty is in a book that is dissolving in a locked case on a staircase that nobody uses.”

* * *

It got worse, and it got worse quietly, and the reason nobody noticed was that a city losing its own reason for existing does not shout, it goes flat. The Lampwrights’ Row had come out of the catalogue in March and had to be entered by hand. A woman named Tamsin Rooke had stopped coming to the Harbour and had begun to be difficult for her neighbours, and the clerk who took her statement had written unstated beside her name, which was the word the office used for a reader who had not read for a season, and which is not a diagnosis, it is an entry.

The office also kept an instrument for this, called the attestation, and every spring the Harbour asked the city to confirm, by signing, the nine lines it had been given, and the city signed, because a signature takes four seconds and nobody argues with a nine-line summary of a thing they have never had cause to doubt. In April the committee struck the sentence about a room going thin out of Ivet Salter’s report and minuted that the office reports the weather and not the mood. She initialled the minute. She has initialled a great many minutes.

By August the returning desk had stopped being busy, because a returns desk is sustained by the city’s habit, and the city had begun to feel, as Perrin put it to the committee and could not afterwards think why he had put it that way, like a library at the end of a very good day.

“It feels like a mood,” he said.

“It is a mood,” said Ivet Salter. “That is how the instrument works. It does not send a letter. It takes the edge off a room and no one can say when it started, and by the time somebody can say, the room has been thin for a year, and the year is not recoverable, and I have written that sentence in a report four times and had it removed by a committee who thought it was an opinion.”

* * *

The clause was in section nine of the Library Act, in a schedule headed Powers not commonly exercised, and it had been there since the library opened, and it had been invoked once, in 1841, on an application by a woman who wished to read a whole novel aloud to her invalid mother, and had been refused because her mother could not follow it.

Ivet Salter put the schedule on the desk between them with a brass weight on it, because paper lifted in Loom if you turned your head too fast, and she said, “Where a person is not sustained by reason of the want of readers, the Reading may be sustained by sustained reading in common, in a public room, of not less than nine hundred pages, over not less than one hundred and twenty days, by not fewer than two hundred persons.”

Perrin read it twice. “Out loud,” he said.

“In common,” said Ivet Salter. “Which is the word the Act uses for out loud, and which the Act has used since the founding, and which everybody in this building knows means one thing, and which nobody has had the legal occasion to require in two hundred years. The two hundred persons may be different every day. The nine hundred pages may be anything. The one hundred and twenty days is the part that is going to hurt, because that is a hundred and twenty days of a hall, and a rota, and a person counting pages aloud at the end of every sitting, and it will be slow, and it will be watched, and some of it will be dreadful.”

“Who would come.”

“That is not my department, and I have not been asked to make it my department, and I have been in this office for nineteen years and I have never once been the person who decides what a reader does with an evening.” She took the weight off the schedule. “What I can tell you is that the clause requires attention, and attention is a thing a city either spends or it does not have, and a story cannot be held up by a summary of itself, and it cannot be held up by taste, and the only thing I have ever seen hold one up is attention, and I have watched two people try to do this job in a reading room and both of them were unbearable about it, so if you are going to run it, run it badly and run it anyway.”

* * *

It began on the first Thursday of October in the Long Room, with the curtains shut, because the Act required a public room and the Long Room had windows and the windows had opinions. Twenty-two hundred names were on the rota in eight weeks. The hall took four hundred at a time, and the sitting was an hour and a half, and the rule was that the whole of the first two hundred pages of volume nine were read every single time, by the same people, in the same order, on the record, from the beginning, because that was the part that was good, and a city that wanted the end of a thing did not get to skip the part it had already decided was worth it.

The person who counted the pages at the end of each sitting was a retired inventory clerk named Debo, who had counted the collection for thirty years and had never claimed to have enjoyed any of it, and who stood at the front of the hall with the ledger and said the number out loud, and the number was always wrong by about forty, because people who are listening do not hear every page, and Debo recorded the number they had reached and not the number that had been read, and the difference was the interest, and the interest was the whole of the thing.

Perrin read the first forty pages on the first Thursday and could not get through them by the third Thursday without his voice doing something at page thirty-one, and he read them anyway, and by the eleventh Thursday he could get through them, and the reason he could get through them was that he had stopped reading them to nine hundred people and started reading them to the woman on the fortieth row who had come every time with a flask.

It was four months. A woman called Ostyn Vaile read page four hundred and eleven in December and put her head down on the table, and was not disappointed, and the person next to her read the next page, which is in the regulations, and it is in the regulations because it happened once in 1841 in a different form. A retired arbitrator named Sabatini disputed the correct reading of a pronoun for eleven minutes in February and was outvoted and conceded the point in a manner the minute describes as unminuted. Three hundred and nine people stopped coming. Four hundred and eleven new names were added in March, most of them by people who had stopped coming, which Ivet Salter entered in the ledger without comment because the ledger had a column for it and she was the kind of clerk who filled in columns.

* * *

The Hall was the second reading room, which held four hundred and nine, and it had held four hundred and nine on the twenty-first of March, and the doors had been shut at two o’clock and had not been opened again until the last sentence, because the clause says in common and Debo had ruled that an open door is a conversation, and the room was, for ninety minutes, entirely indoors.

It was completed on a Tuesday afternoon in March, in a hall with the curtains shut, at twenty past two in the afternoon, in a room holding four hundred and nine people, and the last page of the Chronicle of the Salt Kings is thirty-one words long, and Perrin read it out, and the room then sat in the condition the minutes describe as attentive.

The last page says that the charter of Loom was made for the hearing of it and not for the keeping of it, and that the ninth volume is the ninth volume and not the last, and that a city which has learned to read is not finished. Nothing in the city went out. No ward went thin. The lamps in the Ninth Stair stayed at the strength they were set at, and the Harbour Memory opened at nine the next morning, and the nine lines on the founding were wrong, and the wrongness was a relief, because a wrongness is a thing that can be looked at.

The prediction of 1802 had said the reading would cease on the day the last page was read. Perrin took the form out of the drawer where it had lived for five years, with his own hand on it, and read the four sentences, and put it back, and then he took volume one down off the shelf in the Long Room and opened it at the first page, because it was the only thing he could think of to do, and the first page was not the first page.

The first page had a line in it now that had not been there, under the title, before the chapter, in a hand that was nobody’s, and it said that the reader is advised to begin again. By four o’clock the hall was empty, the rota had been wiped off the door, the Harbour Memory was closed, and the Ninth Stair had gone back to being the way up to nothing that anybody wanted. Perrin Marsh, who had by that evening read all nine volumes of the Chronicle of the Salt Kings from the first page to the last, carried volume one back up to Stack S-9, put it in its place, sat down on the bottom step with it open on his knees, and began again from the beginning.
