# The God Who Forgot Her Own Name

![Cover art: The God Who Forgot Her Own Name](../art/advanced/27-the-god-who-forgot-her-own-name.webp)

Sava Orren unlocked the eastern archive at half past five that evening. She was forty-four years old, broad through the shoulders from twenty years of lifting archive boxes, and careful never to let her left sleeve drag across a page. Rain had been leaking through the temple roof for months. This night the stone floor shone with fresh puddles, each one holding the trembling orange light of her lamp.

They worked through the night cataloging records saved from the flood of twelve years earlier. Boxes scented of wet wool and cedar. Sava read place names aloud while Tam folded linen around each brittle bundle. Her back stiffened. Her knees objected. Neither mattered. A name forgotten was not a small loss. It was a door with no handle.

Near dawn, the river bells gave seven slow peals. Flood warning.

Sava looked toward the high windows. Rain ran down them in ropes.

“We have until second evening tide,” Tam said.

“Then we have until second evening tide.”

Archival knowledge had one practical use: it let people know where buried things were likely to be. Sava and Tam followed the riverkeeper into the eastern archive’s lowest room, where records too wet for public shelves were stored in waxed cases.

Bel unlocked a narrow drawer marked MISCELLANEOUS PETITIONS.

“It came out of the wall,” he said. “A work crew clearing silt heard a bell from inside the stone. A small one. They pulled a god from the crack.”

Inside the coffer lay a figure no taller than Sava’s hand. She had a pale ceramic body, two bent arms, and a skirt glazed the deep green of river weed. A bronze pin pierced the top of her head. It should have held a name tag. The cord remained, but the tag was blank.

The figure’s eyes opened.

“Finally,” she said. Her voice was a dry whisper, like two shells rubbed together. “Are they admitting the wall is soft?”

Sava set the coffer down with both hands. “Who are you?”

“That is the defect.”

“Your name is worn away.”

“I noticed some time ago.”

Bel stared at Sava. “That cannot be right.”

“It is.” The tiny god straightened. “When a small god is used too often, the name thins. A cup wears at the rim. A bell wears where the rope strikes. People called me for lost buttons, bad bargains, spilled milk, and three lost cows they had actually tied to the wrong pasture.”

Sava had heard of worn names, but the doctrine concerned households and house spirits, not temple gods. “Who called you?”

“Everyone, in the wrong tense. A petition must borrow strength from a god without spending the god. Most priests understand this. Most people do not.”

“You are angry.”

“I am thumbsize. I have arranged my features efficiently.”

Tam made a sound that became a cough. Sava bent closer. “Do you remember your function?”

The god nodded. “I kept the river within its banks during the last flood. Your levee should have been safe, but the old channel beneath the tannery shifted. It is below you now. If it wakes, the lower ward will go first.”

Bel slammed his palm on the table. Water leapt from the coffer and struck the ceiling.

“Can you stop it again?”

“Yes.”

The single syllable filled the room.

“Show me the rite,” Sava said.

The god pointed one green-glazed arm at Tam’s case of flood records. “Find my name. Then bring enough witnesses that the river cannot choose a road through their grief.”

Sava opened the case. Twelve years of interviews, repair tallies, dock notes, and rescue lists filled it. The lower ward had flooded during a spring storm. The west gate collapsed first. Water entered one house at a time while bellkeepers rang the old tide sequence and neighbors carried one another through chest-deep water. Sava had been thirty-two. Her younger brother Jovan, twenty-six, had found her clinging to a roof beam. He had tied his belt to hers and gone back for an old man. Neither had returned to the roof.

Her throat tightened. She laid those pages aside and continued.

The temple senior, Mother Caela, entered in black mourning wool despite the summer heat. “Bel says she can repeat the miracle. What is the cost?”

Sava showed her the copied line: THE RIVER REPEATS A SAFE COURSE ONLY WHEN IT MAY CROSS THE MEMORY OF THE LAST FLOOD.

“Every witness loses the flood?”

“Possibly. No account agrees on the scope.”

Caela pointed toward the river. “The lower ward will drown without her.”

“And if we choose in panic, we will spend memories we did not choose.”

“Then convene the people she can place at risk.”

Bel pointed to a sluice schedule. “Seven hundred and six people live between the old channel and the high road.”

Sava asked, “And why should they abandon a ward with four hours left?”

“Because I ordered it. That was poor wording. Ask better.”

They went door to door. Some chose the high road. Others tied furniture to upper floors and prepared ropes, trusting the levee. Sava explained the uncertainty at every door. A witness might keep the facts of the flood but lose the hour of a hand gripping hers. She told them she might know Jovan had died and feel nothing when she saw his portrait. More than two hundred still volunteered.

Sava went home after the noon bells.

Her husband, Anor, was forty-six and kept the plants for two temple kitchens. He sat on the back step with muddy cuffs rolled above his ankles. Sava told him what the god had said.

Anor listened while sorting basil stems. “Do you believe her?”

“I believe she knows what she knows. I also think the old rite was written by people standing downstream.”

“So you fear being deceived.”

“I fear choosing badly for everyone.”

He rose, steadied her by both shoulders, and looked into her face. “Ask the ward for permission.”

“They have given willingness, not permission.”

“Willingness is not absence of fear.”

“No.”

“I remember Jovan carrying the old man. I remember your coat caught on a nail and him laughing while he cut it free. If tonight takes those things, I will still know he was dear to us, as fact if not feeling.”

Sava’s eyes burned. “I may not know you at all.”

“I will introduce myself until you do.”

It was practical, which made her believe he meant it. They returned to the gatehouse near sunset.

The tiny god stood on the tide wheel, her green skirt braced between two brass spokes. The water beneath the floorboards had already begun its patient climb. Sava had brought a drum, chalk, a roll of sailcloth, and the river keeper’s tools. These things could not fight the flood, but hands still needed purposes.

Bel opened the inspection gate. Cold air rushed through the tunnel. Below, black water struck a broken brick and divided around it.

“There is the old channel,” he called. “It has woken.”

Sava climbed onto the wheel housing. “We are ready.”

Caela began the rite. “Do all present consent?”

Each adult volunteer gave a name, ward, age, and chosen token. Children went up the tunnel with Bel’s wardens. As families reached the high road, the two hundred and thirty-six volunteers remained and made the old cadence.

The god spoke the nameless question.

“No,” Sava said.

She unrolled the sailcloth. Across it she had drawn every shape people had given her that day: a roof beam, a closed gate, a cradle, a ferry, Jovan’s belt buckle. Beneath each shape she wrote its description with a single surviving witness.

“This is what we know,” she said. “It is not what you will take.”

The god tilted her head.

“They did not authorize you to erase every comfort along with the pain,” Sava continued. “They offered you the road, not everything they carried while walking it. The rite says the river takes what the witnesses give. We give you the flood’s remembered moments. We keep the fact of it, and no more.”

“You speak for two hundred and thirty-six people,” Caela warned.

“I speak because they told me their names.”

“Then the amendment is recorded. Does the god accept it?”

The tiny figure put both hands against the wheel.

“The river will take what I show it,” she said.

Water burst from the inspection gate.

Bel shouted for the capstan crew. The current tore the wheel housing from its bolts. The tiny god held on while Sava caught a support ring, then marked Bel’s safe angle in chalk across the lower stone. Workers above answered Tam’s bell with hammer blows.

The broken brick shifted. Water forced itself into the dead channel and struck the foundation. The wheel swung the floodgate shut, trapping the first surge. The impact lifted Sava nearly over the edge. Anor caught the rail and hauled her onto the walkway.

“Nearly, Anor,” Bel called.

“Keep saying nearly.”

The river tried again. Sava braced against the housing while Bel counted turns. Tam relayed the tide pattern, and Caela recited every witness’s name so none became an unknown road. Ordinary strength did the rest. The gate seated, the river turned toward prepared ditches, and the lower ward remained above water.

When the final bell sounded, the god slid from the wheel and lay in Sava’s palm. She was duller than before, as if holding the river had thinned her further. The pin above her head held a tiny white tag where her severed name had returned, one clean word newly cut.

Neya.

“Neya,” Sava read.

“I remember,” the god murmured.

The lower ward stayed above water. The old channel drained before midnight. Three streets suffered damaged walls, but the high road held, and the people who had prepared ropes helped everyone who needed them.

The cost became plain the next morning. Tam knew from the flood report that the river had reached the archive steps, but could no longer remember seeing the water inside. Bel knew the ferry had overturned, but its tilt no longer existed in his mind.

Sava went to the portrait room and stopped before Jovan’s face.

She knew the name beneath it. She knew he was her brother. She knew he had died in the flood.

She did not know the color of his coat.

Anor stood beside her, careful not to turn grief into a test. “I can tell you a story,” he said, “but I cannot make you feel it.”

“No.”

“What do you need?”

“An hour. Then tea. And the truth written down, without asking me to pretend.”

They sat together on the archive steps while workers carried buckets through the rain. Sava began a new record: JOVAN ORREN, TWENTY-SIX, SAVED AN OLD MAN, LOST IN THE WEST GATE FLOOD. Anor added what he remembered: strong swimmer, patient teacher, repaired the hinge on her bicycle, laughed too loudly in churches.

Sava listened without rejecting any of it.

The council met that evening in the flooded chapel. They recorded Neya’s rite, the amended offering, and every consequence in the central archive.

Farmers, shopkeepers, boat crews, and temple workers kept a petition desk, but changed its purpose. Neya would no longer answer for lost buttons, small bargains, or convenient forgetting. Citizens would organize kitchens, tool libraries, shelters, and mutual aid first. Only when those failed, with no time for human help, would they approach the small god.

Neya stood on the council rail, recovered enough to look cross. “You may become very inconvenient.”

Sava smiled. “Likely.”

“And if someone needs a lost button?”

“Someone can sew it back on.”

“Poorly?”

“Then it will be memorable.”

That night Sava returned the god to her own archive, not the public shrine. A small god worn thin by constant use, she reasoned, needed a quiet place where every call was counted. On the blank record beside her first entry, Sava wrote: NEYA, KEEPER OF THE RETURNING CHANNEL, NAME RESTORED BY THE SECOND FLOOD.

Then she added the final line, which she read aloud before closing the case.

Her name may be borrowed, but it must not be spent.
