# The Four Winds Took a Vacation

![Cover art: The Four Winds Took a Vacation](../art/advanced/85-the-four-winds-took-a-vacation.webp)

The wind stopped on the sixth of July at twenty past eleven in the morning, in the middle of a sentence that Hanne was saying to Lark about a gate, and neither of them noticed for the length of a breath and then both of them noticed at once.

It was not a gust dying. Hanne had lived seventy-eight years in a valley where the wind stopped the way a hand stops, with a reason. She finished her sentence first, which she did not often do, and then she stood quite still in the dairy doorway with her hand on the frame.

“It has stopped,” Lark said.

“It has.”

They walked out into the yard together, and the barley on the far side of it was standing straight up, every stalk vertical, not one of them lying over or lifting, and the smoke from the kitchen chimney was going straight up and staying there in a grey column, and the washing on the line was stiff and quiet, and the dog, who was called Bell and was nine, stood with his ears out sideways in the manner of an animal trying to hear a noise it has been told is not there.

“That is a great deal of standing barley,” said Lark.

“It is,” said her grandmother, and then, after a while, “well.”

* * *

By the second day the valley had stopped being a valley and had become the inside of something.

The wind had not blown the threshing at Barleyman across the water in the first place, and the threshing at Barleyman was going on the second day, and from the top field of Nethergill you could hear not the general noise of it but the individual beat of the drum, on and on, with the pause in it. The dog barked and Lark knew which farm it was from the bark, and then she knew the direction, and then she worked out that the dog could not hear the bark either and was only feeling it leave him.

Chaff stood in the air over the threshing floor and did not go anywhere. The midges of the fen came up off the ditch and stayed at head height all afternoon, and the wasps found the jam and then found Lark, and the flies got into the milk in the dairy and into the milk in the churn and into the milk in the cat. The birdsong was the worst of it, because without air in it the song lost its shape and got enormous, and she could hear the blackbird doing the same four notes over and over from the churchyard, and the churchyard was a mile off, and the same blackbird had been doing the same four notes since she was four years old and had never been that clear.

“It is not quiet,” said Hanne, twice, to two different people, both of whom had told her that it was very unusual.

“It is not,” Lark agreed.

“It is the loudest week of my life, and I have been in a room with a man who was dying.”

The smell was the part that got them. There had been woodsmoke in the lanes since the first frost, because eleven houses in Aster End had open chimneys and no money to close them, and on the second day of no wind that smoke did not go anywhere at all, it simply lay down in the lane in a layer you could put your hand into. The midden at Fenner Row was four miles off and had been a matter of committee since the war. The whole of the summer’s hay, the parsnip beds at the rectory, the hot green sappy smell off the Fallow mill, which had been burning green wood since March and had been discussed in the tap-room twice and never again, all of it came down the valley in one afternoon and stayed in the valley like a hand laid flat on a table.

“They have taken their gloves off,” said Hanne, coming in from the yard in the evening. “That is what it is. It is a town where everybody has taken their gloves off at the same moment and has not noticed that they have done it.”

They had not stopped loving one another and they had not stopped being polite, and both of those things were working much harder than usual. Lark heard her own name said in an angry voice on the lane and went in to find two women who had been at the same school being extremely reasonable about a boundary wall. At the tap-room in Fallow a man apologised out loud, in the middle of a sentence, for something his father had done in nineteen forty-nine, and everybody present agreed not to look up, which was the correct procedure and had, Lark thought, been invented that morning.

* * *

On the third day Lark walked up to the top field with her grandmother, and halfway across it she said, “Whose house is that?”

“Which one.”

“The one that smells.”

Her grandmother did not pretend not to know. “The yellow curtains,” she said. “The Gleaves’ place, off the Estmere road.”

“That is my mother’s work.”

“It is,” said Hanne. “It has been since Tuesday last spring, and before that it was a woman called Mrs Loyd who kept a consumptive man in a back bedroom, and before that it was Mr Beck, and the Beck boy, in the year before that.”

“All the week the west wind has brought that down the valley.”

“All the week the west wind has brought that down the valley, and the west wind blows down the valley on a Tuesday and a Thursday, and those are market days, and the market is at Estmere on a Wednesday and a Saturday.” Hanne stopped walking. “So that house has been in everyone’s nose two days a week for two years, on days when the carts were in and there was a reason to be on the road.”

Lark stood in the barley and let it settle.

“So the wind was the thing keeping it out,” she said.

“The wind was the thing keeping it out,” said her grandmother, “and it did it on a rota, and none of us ever worked out that the biggest secret in the valley had a timetable.”

They came down off the top field past the burnt acre that everyone had been told was lightning in the August of a year nobody could now name, and past the mill with its smoke standing straight up out of the chimney in a column you could have put a hand into at two hundred yards, and Hanne said, “The Parish Council knows about the mill,” and Lark said, “I know about the mill,” and Hanne said, “You are thirteen. Knowing and saying are different offices and they are not held by the same people.”

* * *

By the fourth day the heat of the standing air had got into the stone walls of the dairy, and the valley had begun to argue about what to do, in the way it had always argued, which was at the smithy and the tap-room and by letter to the district.

The barley was ready and could not be cut, because there is no good way to put cut barley down in air that is not moving; it lies in a mat and it sweats and it goes off in patches, and the farmer at Coble End, who was seventy-one and had cut hay for fifty-eight years, said so twice, loudly, to anybody who came in. The flies had got into the milking parlour and the cows had stood with their tails hanging. The Valley Show and Fair had been fixed for the Saturday of the eighth, four thousand people in a field above Fallow with a band, a cake tent, a tug of war, and a prize for the best marrow.

Lark had been up to the copse above the byre three times by then and had got as far as the wall. Bell would not go within thirty yards of it, and would go no further, and would sit down facing it and bark in a low way that was not warning but comment, and one evening the two of them stood on the wall together and looked in.

Hanne had a theory, which she held without evidence and without apology, that the reason there was a swarm in the byre wall every single year was that the wall had never once been cold enough for them. In an ordinary year the wall was cool from October to March and the bees went somewhere else and thought better of it. This year the wall had been warm since the sixth of July, and it had been very busy in it, and there had been a queue at the dairy window every afternoon until Lark put a net over it.

There was a hedge in there, and the hedge had four things in it.

The first was pale, and low. The second was grey and stood a little taller than the pale one. The third was black and was sitting down. The fourth was white, and was moving, in a small continuous circle, the way a person moves in a queue. She could not see them properly and she did not tell her grandmother what she had seen, because she was thirteen, and because they were the shape of hens in a hedge and there is no point in telling a grown person about hens.

* * *

They went up on the sixth day, at noon, with a rope and a torch and no particular plan.

The rota was nailed to the byre gate, inside the hedge, and it was a timetable in four different hands, ruled in pencil and ink and one in what appeared to be charcoal, and the headings were DAY, HOURS, HELD BY, and UNDERLINED. It ran for nine days. Beside the ninth day somebody had written, in a very small hand, backdated, which Lark did not understand until Hanne read it aloud and made a noise and had to sit down on the gate.

“Backdated,” said Hanne. “They have backdated it. They have gone back and put the start of it on the first, before it happened, and signed it.”

“It is day six,” Lark said.

“It is day six,” her grandmother agreed, “of nine, and they are all here, and they are all still here, which is why none of us has had weather.”

Nobody came out for a while. Then the black one spoke from about four feet up inside the hedge, without moving.

“We are not permitted to leave the rota before the ninth,” it said, in a voice like a boot on gravel, “and we would like to leave the rota before the ninth, and we cannot leave the rota before the ninth, because if we leave before the ninth then the relief does not take over and it is a missed handover, and a missed handover goes on the record.”

“Is there a record?” said Lark.

“There is a record,” said the black one, “in the hedge.”

Nobody said anything for some time. Then the white one, who was still going round in its small circle, said, and could not get more than about half of it out: “I am sorry about the smoke.”

“It is not your smoke,” said the black one.

“It is the smoke of eleven houses and I have carried every one of them and I am sorry about all of it,” said the white one.

Lark found the day sheet in the dairy in the evening, under a sack of meal, on a slate the size of a bread board, ruled in chalk, with the heading THE STANDING and a list beneath it going down to twenty-one items. First item: no fires lit in the hour, on any hearth, in any house. Fourth item: the church bell rung twice at the top of the hour, so that anybody in trouble can be found. Ninth item: the valley does not discuss a matter that has not been asked about twice.

“I was born in still air,” said Hanne, from the dairy door, once, and did not say anything else about it, and went to bed.

By the middle of the following morning there were eleven fires out and a boy on the church tower ringing twice on the hour, and the smith had had a word with the black one, and the parish council had met on the Fallow green, in windless heat, over the business of the fair.

* * *

They held the fair on the eighth, and held it in the air, and it was the loudest anybody in that valley had ever heard a band.

Without a head wind to steal the top of it, the Estmere Silver Band went up the hill and arrived whole, phrase by phrase, and the whole valley had the tune in it at once. The cake tent leaked. The tug of war went to a team from Coploe on a technicality and was disputed for half an hour. In the last dance, with the light going brown in the barley, a man from Coploe put his hand on the shoulder of a woman from Fallow and said something that four hundred people could hear, and the woman said it back, and about six hundred people heard that too.

Up at the top field, with the barley standing straight up around them, Lark said, “Do you think they’ll keep to it? After?”

“No,” said Hanne.

“Why not?”

“Because we did not in nineteen fifty-one, and we will not in August,” said her grandmother, “but we did keep it for nine days, in nineteen fifty-one, and there are two hundred of us who know that a person can, and that is a thing you cannot un-know by Wednesday. It is not a rule any more. It is a demonstration.” She put her hand flat on the ear of the barley and left it there. “That is what they are for.”

* * *

On the ninth evening, at about eight o’clock, with the sun going down behind the ridge above Coploe, it came back all at once, from four directions, into a valley that had been holding its breath for nine days.

It came up the river first, which is how Lark saw it, in the reeds at the bend below the mill, and then over the top of the byre and along the lane and out of the copse, and it came out of the west with the Gleaves’ house in it, and it came out of the north over Estmere, and it took the barley flat and gave it back, and every leaf in the valley turned over at once, and the standing column of smoke off the eleven chimneys went over and away and was gone, and the threshing at Barleyman went off into the night and was a noise in a distance again, and the dog, who had been a megaphone for nine days, put his head down and sighed.

The two of them were in the barley at the top of the field, and the ears went over Lark’s face and stayed there, and her hair went everywhere, and she shut her eyes, and she laughed, and her grandmother shut her eyes too and stood in the middle of it with her hat in her hand.

“I told you,” said Hanne. “I used to live in this.”
