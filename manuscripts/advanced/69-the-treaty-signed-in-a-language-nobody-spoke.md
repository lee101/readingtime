# The Treaty Signed in a Language Nobody Spoke

![Cover art: The Treaty Signed in a Language Nobody Spoke](../art/advanced/69-the-treaty-signed-in-a-language-nobody-spoke.webp)

The treaty was nine words long, and it was the only document in the world that said where the border was, and in 1997 a commission of the two river powers was convened to read it out loud.

The commission met in a room above the Vaure mint with the blinds down, because the two delegations had spent two hundred years learning not to be photographed agreeing. Dr. Elif Kaya was thirty-six and the only living reader of Aurelian, and her job, as the chair’s secretary put it in the first minute, was to say the words, and not to say what they meant.

“One of you,” said the chair, an elderly Ostrian with a pension and no vote, “will read them, and the other will not object, and we will all be very glad.”

“I will read them,” said Elif.

“You will not read them in translation,” said the Vaure delegate, a heavy man in a grey suit who had brought four assistants and a folder. “You will read them in Aurelian, and my philologists will hear whether your Aurelian is the Aurelian of the schools.”

“There is one Aurelian,” Elif said. “There is one manuscript, which is the treaty, and it is a charter, and it is not a copy of anything. Everything else is an argument about it.”

The young diplomat on the Vaure side, who was twenty-eight and second secretary and had been in the room for nine days without speaking, wrote in his notebook: she has read this file. It was true, and he did not know what to do with it, and he was going to find out.

* * *

The treaty was signed at Kell Bridge on the eleventh of October, in the year 1797, in a room over a toll house, by eleven men, of whom four were drowned in the war it ended. It was nine words, and a schedule, and two signatures, and one of the signatures had bled through the page in a way that scholars had argued about for a century.

Elif had the charter in a case on the table in front of her, and the room had spent three days on the schedule, and she had spent three days on the ninth word.

“Aurelian marks the last word of a clause with mi,” she said, “and that is all. A single clitic, two letters, and in the whole of the written language it has three analyses, and no analyst has ever produced a manuscript that decides between them, because there is one manuscript.”

“Show us the nine words,” said the Ostrian delegate.

Elif turned the charter so that the light from the blind fell across it, and the ink was brown, and the schedule beneath was a table of dates in three columns.

“The first eight are agreed,” she said. “Kelis, the river. Em, one. Autha, river. Bankon, of the two banks. Thalen, and the dead. Rekhen, in one book. Enneth, together. The ninth is Saren, and Saren can be read as a verb or as a noun, and mi sits on it, and mi can be the focus marker or it can be the word that opens a condition.”

The Vaure delegate’s chief philologist said, “In the Ostrian schools mi on a final word is the focus marker, unambiguously, in nine printed grammars.”

“In the Vaure schools mi is the protolytic conjunction, in eleven printed grammars,” said Elif. “Both are right. Aurelian allowed both. It is one of the few things about Aurelian that everybody agrees on, and the agreement is what is in the way.”

* * *

The schedule was the reason it mattered.

Elif had gone through it line by line in front of forty people on the second day, and it was three columns of dates running from the eleventh of October to the eleventh of March, and the first column was headed with a word for eels, and the second with a word for lamprey, and the third with a word whose transcription had never been agreed between the two schools.

“If the ninth word is a verb and mi is the focus marker,” she said, “the treaty is a statement. One river, of two banks, and the dead of both banks in one book, and the dead of a man are counted in one book. The schedule is a fishing calendar. The two banks share the water, the catch, the graves and the dead, and no party has any title in the river, and the dates are the closed seasons, agreed, renewable, and every one of them has been kept.”

“And if,” said the Ostrian delegate.

“If the ninth word is a noun and mi is the conjunction, the treaty is a conditional. The river is one and is held of two banks, and the schedule is a schedule of delivery, and the third column is not a fish. The east bank passes on the date in the third column. There is no sharing in it. There is a cession, and it happens on a day, and two hundred years of argument are about the day.”

She put her finger on the third column. “I have read every manuscript witness of the date. The date is legible. That has never been in dispute. The dispute is whether the date is a day or a fish.”

* * *

The summit was held in the treaty room at Kell Bridge, the same floor, and the border was drawn on a map on the wall, and the map had eleven outlines on it in eleven colours, because the mapped border had moved eleven times in two hundred years, most recently in 1931, and every movement had come from a new reading.

“The River Commission of 1889 found for the verb,” said the Ostrian delegate. “The Joint Boundary Tribunal of 1931 found for the noun, on the ground that the verb reading has no cession provision and therefore cannot govern a cession, and it awarded the east bank from the eleventh of October.”

“The 1931 tribunal was reversed on appeal,” said the Vaure delegate, “and the reversal is the reason the map has eleven borders on it, and the reason the fishery officers on both banks have been impounded since 1934, and the reason the people in this valley cannot fish.”

“It is the reason the impoundment is lifted the moment the reading is settled.”

“It is the reason the impoundment is lifted if the reading is settled in our favour,” said the Vaure delegate, and the Ostrian delegate began a reply that had been prepared in 1889.

Elif sat at the end of the table for two days and said the nine words eight times, in Aurelian, slowly, and did not translate them, and on the second afternoon the chair asked her, in the recess, whether she would give the commission her opinion, and she said no.

“You have read it. You are the only person alive who has read it. Give us the reading.”

“I have two readings,” Elif said. “They are both in the text. One of them has been true in this valley for two hundred years and one of them has been true in eleven courtrooms.”

“That is not a position, that is a postponement.”

“It is the truth of the document,” she said. “I am not a party to it. My mother is from the Ostre bank and my father is from Vaure and I swim in the river, and neither of those things entitles me to decide which of two true things is more true, and if I gave you a reading it would be mine, and you would cite mine for a hundred years, and I would be the woman who ended the sharing of the river, and I did not write the treaty and I cannot unwrite it.”

* * *

The fisherman was called Yusuf Boztepe and he was seventy-one, and he fished the middle of the Kell from a boat with a blue bow and a patched stern, and he was on the middle of the river most days, which is where a man goes when he does not want to be asked which bank he is standing on.

Elif had been coming down to his stretch of water in the second summer of her commission, on the Ostre side, on a public path, with a notebook, because she had been told by a man in Vaure that an Aurelian speaker had been alive in 1919 and had died in 1951, and that his grandson fished.

Yusuf did not know the word mi. He knew the nine words. He said them on the riverbank the way his grandfather had said them to him at the age of five, sitting in the boat with the oars shipped, and the two of them went through it together twice a day for a month, and Elif wrote down what he said and compared it with the charter at the end of every week, and the words were the words, and the sound was the sound, and the whole of the two hundred years of scholarship turned on the ninth.

“Do you know which reading it is?” she asked him, in the second month.

“No,” said Yusuf. “Ask me something else.”

* * *

He had been translating it for fifty years. He had been doing it on the bank, sitting on the low wall above the slipway with a thermos, from about four until the light went, and other fishermen had come up over the years and stood there and listened, and he had given it to them in the same words every time, and the version he gave them was not the version in either school.

Elif understood it on a Tuesday in the third month, and she understood it because she had been reading the fish registers.

The registers were the thing. There was a closed season for eel on both banks from the eleventh of October, and there had been one for two hundred years, and nobody enforced it, and everybody observed it, and when the fishery officers had tried, in 1931, to impound the river on the strength of the third column of the schedule, every boat on the water had simply stayed in. The impoundment failed because two thousand men had decided the schedule was a fishing calendar, and had decided it correctly, for two hundred years, on the strength of a reading given out on a riverbank by a man with no letters.

“The commission is going to settle it,” she said to him. “In the autumn. In this room. And it will go one way, and the river will not be shared after that, and the border will be a line and the fishing will be on one side of it.”

“Yes,” said Yusuf.

“You have known that for fifty years.”

“I have known that for fifty years,” he said, “and I have never lied to a fisherman about it, and I have never lied to one about the other thing either, and both of those are true, and if you want me to stop then I will stop, and the fish will be found, and everyone will be quite clear about what I was.”

He put his hand flat on the wall, the way a man does when he is about to say the thing he has been carrying.

“The reading I give them is the wrong one and it is the only one that has ever been true. The river is the wrong reading. The river has been read correctly by six hundred men on a wall for two hundred years and the text says what it says and the reading that would win in that room would take the water out of both banks and give it to one of them, and I am not going to be the man who does that, so I give them the river, and I have never once pretended to the men on this bank that I do not know what I am doing.”

* * *

The booklet was in a tin behind the seat board of the blue boat, and it was not a manuscript.

It was a school exercise book from a school that had closed in 1931, and it had ninety-one pages, and it had been filled up in order over a hundred and six years, and it had a name on the fly leaf, and the name was Leyla Tezel, and Leyla Tezel had been nine years old in 1911, and the entry was in a child's hand with the pen pressed hard.

The words are these and grandmother says they are ours both, so I have written them down careful.

Elif read it on the bank with the wind coming off the water and the tin on her knees, and then she read Yusuf’s account of where it had gone, which was: Leyla’s mother, then the family of a man who mended nets on the Vaure side, then a woman who sold milk on the bridge, then four households, then a boy of nine in 1931 who was given it by his grandmother and who was the man sitting in front of her.

“The woman in 1911 wrote down the reading her grandmother gave her,” Yusuf said. “That is the one I have. I have never seen a copy of it. There is one, the treaty, in a room, and there is this, on a riverbank, and they are not the same document, and only one of them has been in a room.”

“Your grandmother’s reading is the peace reading.”

“It is the one where the water is both,” he said. “That is what it says. I have not said to you that both readings are in the charter, because that is your business and not mine, and I have not said that one of them has two hundred years of people in it. I have said what it says.”

Elif asked him, in the road above the slipway, on an evening when the light was going and there was nobody on the water, whether he would come to the summit and say all of that in front of the two delegations.

“No,” he said. “I am seventy-one and I am a fisherman and they have two hundred and forty translations and I have one wall.”

“Then may I have the booklet.”

“You may have the booklet when the young one can read it,” said Yusuf, “because it is not mine and I have not been its keeper. I have been its voice.”

* * *

The young diplomat of the Vaure delegation, Ilhan Verga, second secretary, twenty-eight years old, spent four months of that summer on the riverbank with a tin of yellow paint and a sheet of oiled paper, learning nine words from a boat.

He had the charter in the commission’s archive and Elif’s transcription and forty-one pages of Vaure philology, and none of it was any use, because what he had to learn was not a text. It was a sound with a lifetime in it, and it took four months, and he learned it the way Elif had learned it, which was from Yusuf, on the wall above the slipway, at four in the afternoon, with a thermos.

On the last evening the old man put the tin on the gunwale and opened it and took out ninety-one pages of a school exercise book and gave it to him, and did not explain it, and walked up the road.

Elif read the fly leaf. She read the child’s hand. She looked at the young man for a while and then she said the one thing she had decided to say to him and had not said to the tribunal, and it was this.

“I have taught you the words in two ways and you have heard which is which, and when you stand up in that room you will say the one that was given to you, and I am not going to tell you which one that is, and I would take it very ill if you worked it out from me, and I would take it as evidence that you had not been listening.”

* * *

The room at Kell Bridge was full on the twenty-second of October, the hundred and ninetieth anniversary, and both delegations were at the long table, and the chair had ruled that the reading would be the last business of the morning.

There were nine words. Ilhan Verga stood up and said them in Aurelian, and he said them without a paper, at the speed of a man who has said them four thousand times above a slipway, and the sound went into the old plaster and stayed there.

He finished on Saren-mi, and he put the weight on the ninth word exactly as Elif had taught him and exactly as Yusuf had said it to her in April, and the ninth word filled the room the way it had filled a thousand kitchens in two hundred years.

Nobody in the room could tell. That is the plain fact of it. The mi is two letters long and the two analyses differ by a grammar, not a sound, and the two delegations had spent a century arguing in print about a distinction no living ear can make, and when the young diplomat said it, it sounded like a treaty, which is what it had always sounded like to the people who live on it.

The chair asked him to say the ninth word again so that the delegations might take it down, and he said it again, and the stenographers wrote what they heard, and the stenographers heard the same thing, and one of them was from the Ostrian school and one of them from the Vaure school and the sound was the same.

The chair asked for the translation, and the room waited, and the Vaure delegate looked at Elif, and Elif looked at the charter, and the charter was nine words and a table of dates, and the third column was legible, and it was a day, and it was a fish, and the reader was the only person in the room who knew, and she had said in the recess of the second day that this was not hers to decide.

“The commission will take the meaning into consideration,” said the chair, and the adjournment was ten days.

In the ten days nobody asked her.

On the river, between the two banks, in the last of the light, Yusuf Boztepe sat in the blue boat with the oars shipped and the line out, holding for the middle, because that is where the fish are and that has never been in dispute by anybody.
