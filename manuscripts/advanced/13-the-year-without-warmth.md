# The Year Without Warmth

![Cover art: The Year Without Warmth](../art/advanced/13-the-year-without-warmth.webp)

The bells stopped ringing on the morning the river turned white.

Ice climbed the bell ropes and sealed every clapper. It spread over bread in the market, across sleeping cats, and along the warm bricks of the bathhouse. By noon, the harbor was a plain of tilted masts. People pulled sledges through streets where carriage wheels had sunk to their axles.

At noon, the Snow Queen appeared above the ruined bell tower.

She was small in the distance, white against a pewter sky. When she spoke, her voice arrived inside each citizen's coat.

“Your city has borrowed a winter it cannot repay,” she said. “Heat has been hidden from the poor and measured out to the rich. I will freeze every equal share until the debt is named.”

No one thanked her.

The first night, twelve people slept in the public granary. The second night, the granary’s iron roof cracked. People carried buckets of bean soup to the South Ward, where wood was too expensive to buy and the Queen’s cold made every cough sharp as broken glass.

Mina Venn was a weather clerk. Her job was to keep honest records of rain, wind, and frost. When the cold began, she measured the depth of ice with an iron rod. She marked each reading on a sheet of blue paper.

By the third week, her measurements were used to ration fuel. The Emergency Council called her office. “We need the cold classified,” Mayor Brann said. “Find out where it bites hardest.”

“It does not bite unevenly,” Mina said. “The spells do.”

That was harder to admit.

Lord Pell kept a great glass furnace in the North Ward. Its chimney puffed all winter. He said it warmed carpenters, glassworkers, and a hospital. Mayor Brann agreed. The workers agreed. The people sleeping beneath the old granary did not.

The Snow Queen had frozen the city’s old protections along with its old wrongs. She offered warmth, but she offered no list of who deserved it and no promise that the list would stay unchanged.

Mina packed a measuring case with brass thermometers, string, chalk, and three dry inkpads. She walked with Jori, a lamp boy who had learned to read thermometers during the blackouts. Together they measured the wards. They found that the frost obeyed old property lines. It melted around Pell’s furnace, the bathhouse steam, and the bank’s brass doors. It gripped the rentshops and the narrow alleys behind them.

“Can you show the Queen?” Jori asked.

“She is not missing. She is waiting to be argued with.”

* * *

On the sixth week, Mina presented her map at the Council chamber. The map showed cold in red and warmth in gold. Lord Pell pointed to the hospital. “See? Life is protected.”

“It is,” Mina agreed. “So is the bank. Why does the south have no gold at all?”

No one answered.

The city had always measured warmth by what people could buy. The Queen had forced everyone to see that measurement, but she had not offered a better one. A map could reveal injustice. It could not distribute a loaf of bread.

Jori had learned to draw the city’s old property lines from chimney smoke and ivy. He added symbols for empty windows, boarded shops, and rooms where one family had slept for three generations. Mina corrected the symbols when the Queen’s cold shifted. A map could carry an argument, she said, but it could not carry a person’s fear. They would need both.


That night, Mina went to the bell tower. The ice around the bells was blue and clear. Inside it, she could see small shapes moving: clerks, messengers, street sweepers, children hauling water. They had been working the winter without sleep. The Queen had not trapped ordinary life by accident. She had trapped the work that made life possible.

Mina met her on the frozen stair.

“What will you accept as payment?” Mina asked.

The Queen looked at her without blinking. “Truth.”

Truth had become a dangerous word. The city council had cut the hospital fund during the freeze, then hidden the figures in a locked cabinet. The bank had called unpaid loans “voluntary patience.” Every household had a private sorrow. Which truth could calm a winter?

Mina held out her blue-paper map. “These are my measurements. They show the Queen’s cold follows the city’s old choices. They do not show why those choices were made.”

“Measurements are not reasons.”

“No. So I need testimony.”

The Queen considered this. “Speak, and the ice will listen.”

Mina understood the trap. Testimony would become another flood, drowning precise facts in grief. One person’s winter could not be counted for another. She asked for a hearing, not a confession.

* * *

The next morning, the Council opened the granary doors. Anyone could speak, but not everyone could spare a whole day. The Queen sent cold into the public halls whenever a powerful person refused to attend. Her magic made absence uncomfortable, though she could not make honesty appear.

Pell came with a ledger. He had kept the hospital furnace burning. He had also charged three times the usual rate for coal and used the extra money to secure loans. He showed the committee the black numbers. “I kept the hospital open,” he said. “You cannot call every choice a theft.”

Mina’s mother spoke next. She had worked in the North Ward laundry. She had lost two fingers to frostbite because the boiler fuel was diverted to the bank. “You kept the hospital open,” she said. “You closed the laundry.”

The room went quiet.

Over the following days, the map gained layers. Not just temperature, but hours of heat, fuel delivered, wages withheld, rooms made unlivable. The council wanted a simple solution. Mina understood that simplicity could be a kindness only if it did not hide the bill.

People began bringing their own measurements to the granary. A baker marked the hour his ovens went cold. A ferryman recorded the thickness of ice that stopped his boat. A child drew a blue square around the place where the bell rope turned white. Mina copied each detail, then added a place for uncertainty. A memory could be true without being complete.


A thaw spell, someone suggested, would end the emergency. It would also release the cold stored in every wall. The ice would fall from roofs, burst pipes, and flood cellars with meltwater. The city’s people were cold and tired, but a sudden thaw could kill as surely as winter.

Mina proposed a gradual thaw. The Queen could open the sky one district at a time. Before each opening, crews would mark roofs, clear drains, and move families. The Council would publish the schedule. Rich households would pay according to the heat they had used. The poorest would receive free shelter.

The Queen listened. “And if they delay?”

“Then the ice stays,” Mina said. “The delay will belong to them.”

The City’s emergency council called the plan “Mercy in Layers.” It was not a beautiful name. It was useful.

Pell opposed it. He argued that the measurements were unreliable. Jori had discovered why: the Queen’s ice did not melt evenly around metal. He had corrected the readings by using copper wire and clay cups, then compared every result with the old rain gauges. His work was careful enough to withstand doubt.

The Queen’s magic began to thaw the North Ward first. Steam hissed from every roof. People opened shutters and breathed air that smelled of wet stone. But the hospital roof cracked, just as the council had feared. Mina’s crews had marked it, yet the repair funds had vanished into a bond signed before the emergency.

Lord Pell had followed the law, technically. The law allowed emergency fuel to be tied to future taxes. He had not stolen the repair money. He had made it impossible to spend.

Mina stood in the hospital yard as water dripped from the icicles. The patients were safe. The laundry workers were not. The next district was the South Ward, where the thaw would begin the following dawn.

Pell offered to give them the bond. In exchange, he wanted the map removed from public display and the Queen’s records sealed. “People need a simple story,” he said. “They cannot bear all the causes.”

Jori looked at the black line around the hospital roof. “Then we give them a clear story,” he said. “Not a kind one.”

Mina refused the bargain. The Council seized the bond by emergency rule, using the evidence map to show that the funds had been promised to public safety. Pell stepped down from the fuel committee. He was not erased. His furnace had warmed the sick. His ledgers had saved the city from lies, even when he used them to make lies comfortable.

* * *
The South Ward thawed slowly. First the roofs, then the lanes, then the locked storerooms. Families found damp blankets, cracked jars, and letters softened into pale pulp. They did not cheer. They carried each other’s chests uphill. A woman named Ada opened a schoolhouse and hung a bell that had never been enchanted. Its flat, ordinary voice carried across the wet street.

At the end, the Queen stood in the open harbor. Her staff was a column of clear ice. Around her, the last frozen workers drifted down from the bell tower. They were exhausted, thin, and alive.

“You did not give me a payment,” she said.

“You gave us the winter,” Mina replied. “We found the cost.”

The Queen looked toward the city. Smoke rose from many chimneys, but not all. The rich still had more heat than the poor. The map had not made them equal. It had made the difference visible.

Then she melted.

The ice did not vanish. It ran into the harbor in silver sheets, carrying soot, petals, nails, and the small tools of the people who had kept the city moving. The water rose. Mina and Jori watched the tide mark climb the new stone wall.

By spring, the city had a public heat map, an open fuel ledger, and a rule that any emergency order affecting the poor had to expire. None of these rules were magical. The Queen had broken the old silence. The city still had to do the patient work of speaking plainly.

Mina kept the blue-paper map. She added a final line in red ink: “Warmth is not only what falls from a sky. It is who is asked to produce it, and who can refuse without freezing.”

Beneath it, she wrote the names of the workers the Queen had freed. The list was long. There was not room to list everyone, so she began a second page.
