# The Goddess Who Was Also a Small Town

![Cover art: The Goddess Who Was Also a Small Town](../art/advanced/63-the-goddess-who-was-also-a-small-town.webp)

The tank at the top of Ammar held enough water for four hours a day, and this was printed on a card nailed to the pump house in two languages in the same typeface, because the Regional Authority supplied the card and the town had not thought to supply a better one. Saira Melki arrived in the last week of May with the card in her hand, a case of instruments, and a term of reference: investigate the failure of the aquifer serving the settlement of Ammar and report.

The valley was a bowl of pale stone with two thousand people at the bottom of it and a wadi through the middle, dry in most years and catastrophic in the rest. Above the town, on the rise where the older settlement had been, a white shrine with a courtyard and a row of empty bowls along the bottom of the steps.

Mayor Nasrin Odeh received her in a room with a ceiling fan and eleven plastic chairs. She was sixty-one, born in the town, and had come to the shrine every day of her life since she was six, and did not consider this a fact about herself. She handed Saira the municipal file, four inches thick, and said that everything the Authority would need was in it, except the thing they were going to ask about.

“You will want the well logs,” the mayor said. “Kestel Dam, the private boreholes, Bore Four. My father kept a book of the shrine water and the water committee has kept one since 1931. It is the same book. That is not an accident, and I am not going to pretend it is a coincidence.”

In the shrine courtyard a woman of about fifty was sweeping the paving with a broom of palm frond. She had grey at her temples and a dress with a mend at the elbow, and she worked without attention, the way of a person performing a duty that has long since stopped requiring thought. Saira asked the priestess who she was. The priestess, thirty-one, three years in the post, answered without embarrassment: “She is the one who sweeps. She has done since before this shrine was built over the older one. We have no name for her in use. People say Ilma when they need to be discourteous.”

* * *

Saira put a data logger in the casing of the municipal wellfield and read the water table from a chalked mark inside the pump house wall that four generations of attendants had left for the purpose. In 1968 the mark had been at eleven metres below the surface. It was at thirty-three, and the record was not a curve but a set of steps, each one coinciding with a year in which somebody had drilled.

The file explained the steps, and explained them competently. The municipal field went in at eighty-four metres in 1968, three wells, on a survey paid for by an Authority worried about the entire basin and not about this valley. In 1979 the Kestel Dam went up eleven kilometres upstream to irrigate the fields below it, the recharge from the floodplain stopped arriving, and the survey that justified the dam was right about the farms and wrong by a factor of three about what the farms would take out of the wadi in a dry decade, in the way of surveys written by people who had never had to drink the consequence.

Then came a hundred and forty private boreholes between 1988 and 1995, listed in the mayor’s file with the name of the family, the depth, and the pump. Every one was legal at the time. The Authority’s threshold for a private bore was thirty metres, set in 1974 to stop people digging shallow holes in clay, and it had the effect of requiring nobody to regulate a well in sandstone, which is where Ammar’s water is. The farmers were not fools, and the driller had been right, and forty of the hundred and forty still produce.

Bore Four went down in 2003 to two hundred and ten metres and came up brackish, and the town has been drinking it since 2016 in a blend of two parts fresh to one part salt, because the fresh part is what is left. Saira wrote in the margin that nobody had acted in bad faith, and that the town of Ammar was being poisoned by a series of correct decisions.

* * *

She found the goddess where she had left her, in the courtyard, at dusk, when two boys of about twelve, paid badly by the water committee, were filling the bowls from a hose. Saira sat down on the step beside her and asked which aquifer the Authority meant, and the woman answered without looking up.

“There are four,” Ilma said. “The shallow alluvium, which is what your tank uses, and which is finished. The Bain sandstone, which is what the field uses, and which is your problem. The deep marl, which is salt, and which nobody is allowed. And the one under the shrine.”

“How deep is the one under the shrine.”

“Two hundred and ninety-six metres. It has been two hundred and ninety-six metres since the shrine was built.”

Saira wrote the number down, and then the date, and then closed the book on her knees, because the figure had not varied in the whole period for which the shrine had existed. She asked, in the voice she used for a borehole log, for the level in the fourth aquifer over the last hundred years, and Ilma gave it to her the way a clerk gives a figure off a return.

“Forty-one metres below the casing in 1888. Nineteen in 1922. Nine in 1949. Eleven in 1968. Sixteen now. I keep them in the order the wells were dug, which is the order you would keep them.”

“You have kept them for a hundred years.”

“I have kept everything for a long time.”

* * *

The water committee met on the second Monday in the school annexe, and the municipal engineer Younes Attar put up a chart with four lines on it and a great deal of competence. The field was being mined at three times its rate of recharge, this was now permanent, and the only sustainable abstraction in the valley was a regulated one at forty litres per person per day, which was less than half what the town used and a third of what it wanted.

A councillor named Bishr said the engineer had used the word sustainable, which was a word for a thing that could go on. Attar said he understood, and that he had given them the truth in the order truth came, and that they did not have to like it. Nobody disagreed with a figure on the chart, and the discussion ran two hours and returned to the shrine.

The festival of the Standing Water was in nine days and used four hundred and twelve bowls, and the committee had proposed cutting the shrine’s allocation by a third for the drought year. Mayor Odeh listened to the whole of the second hour and then said the thing she had been in office four years to say, which was that the festival had never been cancelled in a bad year, that the year before last the bowls had been full and the gardens green, and that the year before that the wells were low and the gardens were not green, and the shrine was in the accounts of the water committee because the shrine was in the accounts of the water committee, and would the committee like to explain that sentence to the people of Ammar.

“Somebody has to be able to explain it,” Saira said.

“Somebody can. Not me. I have been at that shrine every day since I was six. In 1971 the flood came through the wadi and took nineteen houses, and the goddess was asked for water every day for a month, and it rained on the fourth day, and nobody has ever established whether that was because of the asking or because the fourth day was going to rain anyway. Everybody in this town knows what happened on the fourth day, and I have no instrument with which to argue with them.”

* * *

In the courtyard that evening, in the rain that came and did nothing, Saira told the goddess about the chart and the bowls. Ilma listened with the air of a person hearing a case she has heard in four hundred other rooms, and said, “They are both right. The engineer is right and the mayor is right, and they cannot both be right, and this is the only kind of problem there is. You can only move something, and the something is always the children or the gardens or the dead, and none of those has a member of the water committee.”

“You said there is an aquifer under the shrine.”

“There is.”

“At two hundred and ninety-six metres, which no bore in this valley has reached, because a bore to that depth costs more than the town has.”

“A bore to that depth costs more than the town has,” said the goddess. “The town has something else. You would not call it a bore, and you would not call it an instrument, and I have never been asked to explain it in writing, which has been convenient for both of us.”

Saira did the measurement anyway, because she was a hydrologist before she was anything else. For thirty-one days she photographed the bowls with a date board, weighed the shrine’s own account, which the water committee had kept since 1931, and put a rented depth sounder on a line in the old well behind the shrine. In the fourth week the cistern in the pump house rose in the night.

The rise was four to eleven centimetres, and it happened on the nights when the town had something to pray about, which in a drought in a town of two thousand people was most nights, and it clustered hardest around the new moon, when the families of Ammar walk out to the Standing Water and say what they want at the trough, then pour what they have into the bowls. She ran the numbers against a rainfall record, a pumping record, and the Authority’s groundwater model, and the model could not produce them.

The goddess explained it in the courtyard, once, in the plainest language Saira had heard from anyone claiming a supernatural cause. The town asked, and asked properly, in the sense that a town asks when it puts water in a bowl at midnight instead of pouring it on the field, and the asking was a handle, and the handle turned the water. She had been doing it since the town was four houses, and the terms had never changed, because they had never been negotiated and there was no party with standing to renegotiate.

“And if the town stops asking.”

“Then I stop turning, and the bowls fill themselves, and the cistern is a tank, and your engineer is right about all of it, and the gardens go, and the dead are buried with water from a hose, and there is one more settlement in this valley with a shrine and a tank and no reason to keep either.”

“And nobody dies of thirst.”

“No. That was never the arrangement. The arrangement was gardens.”

* * *

Mayor Odeh brought her father’s book, the shrine’s water book, to Saira’s room under a cloth, and they turned the pages for an evening. It recorded the bowls, the date, the committee’s contribution, the state of the cistern, and the number of people at the festival, two thousand two hundred in 1989 and one thousand one hundred in 2019, while the number of bowls had not changed.

“They will do it again next month,” the mayor said. “Two thousand people in that courtyard, bowls full, the cistern four centimetres higher in the morning, the taps shut, the gardens green. If you take the goddess out of the water, Saira, you are taking the gardens out, and you will be able to say that on the radio in the capital, and the gardens will be the last thing anybody mentions.”

“That is a reason to keep a schedule. It is not a reason to keep a goddess.”

“It is a reason to keep a goddess. The schedule is the instrument. The goddess is the signature.” The mayor closed the book. “You will write your report and it will be true, and it will be filed, and the bowls will be filled or they will not. In 1981 the Authority told us the shallow wells were finished. Four hundred people left inside two years, because you cannot be a town that has been told it is a finished town. So I will say the true thing to you and not to them.”

* * *

The last producing well in the municipal field was decommissioned in the last week of August, and the town was on tanker supply from the regional reserve by the middle of September, and Saira went down to the wadi at night with a headlamp because the wadi was where the answer was not, and because she had run out of other places to go.

The goddess was already sitting on the sand in the dry bed, in a place where the light of the town stopped at the weir. She had brought no broom. When Saira sat down beside her she said, without preamble, that the reserve was finite, that everyone who had ever drawn from it had drawn it down, and that she had been holding the conversion steady since 1888 by drawing less each year, and that the conversion had been falling for thirty-one years.

“Then it is the aquifer,” Saira said. “Of course it is the aquifer. Two hundred and ninety-six metres of sandstone is a reservoir, and a reservoir is a subtraction. How much is left.”

“The last figure in my ledger is the seventh of this month.” Ilma read it the way a woman reads a line off a return. “Four hundred and six years at the present rate. Longer if you stop drilling, shorter if the town grows. It is not a moral question, it is a mean. The wells at two hundred metres draw from a different chamber and do not touch it, which is the one piece of good news I have been given in a century.”

“Four hundred years.”

“Four hundred and six. It is the only figure I have ever had that was worse than the one before it.”

Then she cried, which Saira had not expected and had no procedure for. She did it the way of a woman who has not practised it, without noise and without much change of expression, and it was not beautiful, and there was nobody to hand it to, and the sand at their feet did not darken, which Saira noticed and did not write down.

“You should say something,” Ilma said, after a while, in the voice of a person filing a request.

“I know. I am not going to. If I say it kindly it will be a lie, and if I say it accurately it will be a report, and you have four hundred years of reports.” Saira put her hands on her knees. “I will stay. I will not pretend that staying is a comfort, and I do not know which is kinder, so I am going to keep my mouth shut.”

“That is allowed,” said the goddess, and wiped her face with the heel of her hand, and looked at the sand. “You will tell them the number.”

“Yes.”

“Not before the vote.”

“No. Not before the vote. It is your reserve and your figure, and you are a person to be dealt with under whatever rule this town has about persons, and I do not yet have a rule, and I have three weeks to find one.”

“Find it quickly,” said Ilma. “They are very good at rules. They have had nine hundred years of them, and not one has ever been about this.”

* * *

Saira’s report was bound at the regional capital in November: four hundred and eleven pages, an abstract of two, forty-one tables, and one folded chart with four lines on it that the engineer Younes Attar had drawn and let her use. It was registered on receipt, stamped, and put on a numbered shelf in a building with a mechanical climate system, where it was the only object in the valley’s future that anybody had read all the way through.

The town had been promised a meeting on the goddess and the water, and the council scheduled it for the second week of January, and it ran three hours, and the vote was close. The motion to retain the Standing Water in the municipal account and to continue the bowls’ allocation through the drought was carried by twenty-three votes, on a night when the fans were broken and people voted standing up in the aisle.

The goddess was not on the ballot. She had never been on the ballot and could not be put on it, because the amendment of 1993 to the water code, drafted to allow a community abstraction right to be held in a body’s name, used the word person, and because Ilma held abstraction licence W-2881, issued in 1931, renewed twice, in the name of the shrine, in her own hand, with a signature the water committee had been receiving for ninety years.

A councillor stood up in the middle of the count and pointed out, mildly, that you could not vote for a person. The clerk looked at the statute, the amendment was made, and the motion stood or fell according to where a two-thousand-year-old deity had been placed on paper, which was the same place as the woman who ran the bakehouse.

* * *

The last of it was in the school hall in March, because a town with an amendment still has to meet, and Saira sat at the back in a borrowed coat while the people of Ammar put questions to a woman of about fifty in a dress with a mend at the elbow.

“Do you like it?”

“I am the streets,” said Ilma. “I am the cistern and the shade and the shade’s edges. The town is not on me. The town is what I am for. When the water goes, the walls are still the walls and the cistern is still a hole in the ground, and the town will be a town of two thousand people for a while, and then a set of houses for a hundred years, and the bowls will be in the shrine for a thousand years, and the shrine will have nothing to be a shrine about.”

The hall sat in the silence of a room in which two thousand people have all, at some point, been told the same thing by a parent. Then a girl stood up in the front row. She was eleven, and a teacher who had run out of better ideas had sent her to ask a question, and she asked it badly and at length and in the wrong order: whether the goddess had ever been asked anything, whether she got bored, whether she was lonely. The hall laughed at the third, and Ilma did not, and the girl came down the aisle and stopped at the table and asked, “Are you all right?”

“I am two thousand years old,” said Ilma, “and there are four hundred and six years in the ground under you, and the town you keep is going to need me for every one of them, and I am very tired, and nobody has ever asked me that before.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the only one I have. I am a piece of town infrastructure. I do not have feelings about it. I have a schedule.” She put her hand flat on the table. “Ask the Mayor.”

“I asked the Mayor. The Mayor says you will not tell us anything, because of the oath.”

“There is an oath.”

“You made it?”

“I made it,” said the goddess, “and it is the only thing I have ever made that anybody else keeps, and I would like to be asked this once a month, and I have thought about how to say that without it sounding like an order, and I have not found a way, so I am going to say it as an order.”

Saira, at the back, with a report four hundred and eleven pages long on a numbered shelf in the capital, did not move, because she had a figure in her pocket that she was not permitted to read out until the vote, and the vote had been in January, and the figure was four hundred and six, and there was nothing she could do with it here that was not theft.

Yusufa took two bowls from the shelf by the door, one in each hand, and set them down on the table in front of the goddess, and asked her again, the way you ask a cistern when the taps are dry: not to be answered, but to be used.
