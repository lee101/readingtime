# The Astronomer Who Married the Equinox

![Cover art: The Astronomer Who Married the Equinox](../art/advanced/67-the-astronomer-who-married-the-equinox.webp)

Vittore Marchetti had been the Astronomer of the College for two years and seven months, and he had been ill for all of them, and he died on the eleventh of February in the room he had been given at the top of the east tower, and the ceremony was set for the twentieth of May, and the College did not move it, because the equinox is a fact about the sky and the College does not move facts.

Ilse Marchetti was thirty-one. She was the widowed Astronomer, which meant that in the eleven weeks between her husband’s death and the appointed day she was an office and not a person, and that on the twentieth of May, if she said the words, she would be married to the March equinox, a point of light eleven thousand kilometres above the Atlantic, a piece of sky with a schedule.

“The College is not asking you to decide anything,” said Provost Ottavia Renn, without looking at the paper on her desk. “The College is asking you to attend. The terms require attendance. If you do not attend, there is no marriage, and if there is no marriage, the equinox is where it was last year, and last year the calendar was four tenths of a second out.”

“That is not a sentence with a meaning,” Ilse said.

“It is a sentence with a finding,” said the Provost, “and the finding is nine hundred years old, and the finding is in the charter, and the charter is in the case in front of me, and I did not write it.”

* * *

The terms were on the south balcony under a glass case that had been unlocked once a century since 1124, and they were on vellum, and there were four of them, and the fourth was the important one, and everyone in the College knew the fourth was the important one and nobody could say why.

On the balcony floor, set into the granite in a ring eleven feet across, were the second marks: one short bar cut in the stone for every Astronomer who had performed the marriage, cut in the year of the marriage, and the cuts ran back round the ring to 1124, and there were one thousand and six of them. Ilse’s husband’s bar was at the fourth place from the bottom and on the wrong side, because in the year before him an Astronomer had cut his bar on the wrong side to get out of the light, and nobody had ever corrected it, and there is a note about it in the 1731 inventory.

The ring is not complete. There is one mark that has never been cut, because the point in the sky the marriage is to is not a place on the horizon and has never been cut by anybody. It sits at the ninth bar from the bottom, level with nothing, the same height as the bars a foot to either side of it, and it is a square of granite left smooth. The astronomers call it the empty second, and a novice is made to stand on it once, so that he knows what he is not allowed to do for the rest of his life.

“You may put your hand on it,” the Provost said. “Everyone does. It does nothing. That is not a flaw, that is the design.”

* * *

“The design does what,” Ilse said. “That is the whole of my question and you have not answered it once in eleven weeks.”

“The design binds,” said the Provost, and then stopped, and looked at her, and for the first time in eleven weeks looked as though she had been asked a question instead of a question about a question. “It binds the equinox to the College. That keeps the calendar honest. That is all it is. The equinox is a fixed point and the sky drifts, and a thing that drifts will drift further, and nine hundred years of standing on a cold balcony in a black gown is cheaper than nine hundred years of a whole empire being four tenths of a second wrong about the length of March.”

“That is a very expensive way of being right.”

“It is the only way we have found,” said Provost Ottavia Renn, “and we have looked, and there are two others, and both of them are the Treasury, and both of them are arithmetic.”

* * *

“I am not going to do it,” Ilse said, on the third of March, in the High Room, with the balcony shut behind her.

She gave three reasons and she gave them in the order she had thought of them. The first was that she had stood in this room for eleven weeks and nobody in the building had asked her a single question about what she was going to do, and that a ceremony performed by a person who is not asked is a ceremony performed on a person. The second was that she was thirty-one and widowed in a season and did not intend to spend the rest of her life on a balcony in May. The third, which she said last and which she said quietly, was that her husband had died at the top of the east tower and she was not going to stand on a ring of cuts and say words to a point of light about a season in which he had died.

None of those three reasons was a reason about astronomy, and the Provost noted that, because that was the difficulty.

The Provost took the refusal better than anyone in Teshkar had expected, and she took it the way a good engineer takes a bad measurement, which is by going to find out whether the instrument was at fault or the reading. She had it circulated in an afternoon. The death of the Astronomer, it ran, was not a bereavement but a vacancy, and a vacancy at the ceremony is a crisis of faith in the instrument, and a crisis of faith in the instrument is answered in the one manner the College has ever found to answer it.

“We will pray you back into it,” the Provost said. “I am appointing a Deputy.”

* * *

Timo Vasar was nineteen, and had come up to the ridge at fifteen from a school in the lower valley where the only instrument was a brass sextant with a cracked index mirror, and he had been at the College four years, and he had been made a Deputy Astronomer in June, and he had been extremely pleased about it and had also been extremely annoying about it.

He arrived in the High Room the same afternoon with two books under his arm, one of which was a handbook of ceremony and the other of which was the handbook for the instrument, the being the College’s long transit telescope, forty feet of brass on a stone pier, which was called the Long Tube, and which was the only object in the building that the marriage was actually about.

“Deputy Astronomer Vasar,” he said. “I have been sent to pray for you.”

“That is not what you are for,” Ilse said.

“That is what I am for,” said Timo Vasar. “There is a memorandum. It is two lines long. It says that the Deputy will attend the Astronomer daily, will be guided by the Deputy’s own conscience, and will report to the Provost.”

“Who wrote it?”

“The Provost, the Sub-Chancellor, the Precentor, and a man from the Treasury, and the reason the Treasury sent one is that the Treasury computes the calendar when the College does not, and the Treasury computes it by arithmetic.”

The High Room was cold the way a room is cold when it has been cold since the fifteenth century, which is to say not badly but absolutely, and it had a slate floor that nobody was permitted to sweep, and the dust from the roof beams had settled into the corners in the shape of the year.

“Sit there,” Timo said, pointing at a stool at the eyepiece end. “Not on the square. On the stool. It is a seat, it is not a mark, and the Provost is not going to have you arrested for sitting down in a room with a bench in it.”

He was at the eyepiece before she had settled, and he made her do four things, and he was right about all four, and he did not explain any of them until she had done them, which she understood afterwards to be the only kind of lesson the College knew how to give.

“The tube is not pointed at the equinox,” she said, after a while.

“It is not pointed at anything,” said Timo Vasar. “It is pointed at 1963, and 1963 is a number, and 1963 is where the sky was when somebody wrote a table, and the table is the reason the College can say what time the equinox is. The sky is not late. The table is early. Those are different sentences and only one of them is available to us.”

* * *

They began on the ninth of March, and the High Room acquired a routine. The College’s own observation of the transit, taken by the Assistant, recorded to a tenth of a second. The College’s own observation taken by the Deputy. A second observation with the tube slewed eight degrees west and brought back, to catch a man who has leaned on something in the night. And a set of figures compared against the Attested Time, which is the time the College publishes, which is the time the Treasury copies, which is the time a shipping company in Selv Harbour writes on a bill of lading.

The differences were small and they were not random. Ilse found the first one on the fourth of April and did not tell him for a day, because she wanted to be certain of it, and on the fifth of April she told him, and he went quiet in a way she had not heard him go quiet, and then he said, “Right,” and wrote something down, and then he said, “Do not tell the Provost yet.”

“Why not?”

“Because I would like to be wrong for one more week,” said Timo Vasar.

The week took six weeks. The College’s rule, printed in the handbook in a hand nine hundred years old, is that after the marriage the Astronomer corrects the instrument to the attested time, and every instrument book in the College for nine hundred years is a book of corrections, and every correction moves the reading in the same direction, and the reading and the Attested Time agree, always, to a tenth of a second, and the Attested Time is right.

Timo built what he called a second opinion. It was a portable transit of his own construction, three feet of brass on a tripod, made out of a broken garden gate and the good sextant from the lower valley school, which the school had given him when he left, and which he had promised to send back when he was a Deputy and had not sent back. It could not do what the Long Tube did. It could tell them what time the equinox actually was, from a modern catalogue and a hilltop and no ceremony at all, and it was not very good, and it was good to a fifth of a second, and that was twice as good as it needed to be.

On the nineteenth of April they observed together, and the portable transit said the equinox fell at 04:11:19.4, and the Long Tube said 04:11:19.0, and the Attested Time said 04:11:19.0, and the two instruments agreed with each other and disagreed with the truth, and the disagreement was four tenths of a second, and it was four tenths of a second in the same direction every night they looked.

“Four tenths of a second in nine hundred years,” Ilse said. “That is nothing. That is smaller than the width of the mark on the dial.”

“It is smaller than the width of the mark on the dial,” said Timo Vasar, “and it is larger than the difference between the year we are in and the year the Treaty of Ander, and this College was founded to keep a number honest, and this is what the number was doing while we were on a balcony saying words to it.”

He took her down two flights and out into the cold, and showed her the pin that comes out of the alidade on the side that nobody looks at, because it is under the counterweight. “That holds the polar axis at the inclination for 1963. The axis has been walking toward Polaris since 1963, and the walk is very slow, and every time this tube is rebuilt the inclination is written down and screwed in, and the screw goes in from the printed table, and the printed table is right, and the table is the thing the instrument is corrected to. So the instrument is corrected to the table, and the table is corrected to the instrument, and between them they have never once been compared with the hill.”

“The College has not been marrying the equinox,” said Timo Vasar. “It has been marrying the tube. The marriage is a calibration contract with a ceremony on the front of it, and the ceremony is the part we can see, and the contract is the part that does the work, and the work is this: once a year a person stands in the cold and says aloud that the instrument is right. It has been said nine hundred and six times. Nobody has ever said it out loud.”

* * *

The letters were in the dead tray in the copy room, the tray where unbound and undelivered post went, and they had been in it for five weeks, and they had been opened once, by a sub-librarian who had looked at the address and put them back.

They were addressed to the Astronomer, after me. Ilse had not thought about them, because the College had provided a great many people for the purpose of not thinking about things, and she had allowed herself the one, this time.

She read the first at ten past four in the copy room on the nineteenth of May, the day before, and it was not a love letter. It was a set of figures taken from the Long Tube, with a correction to the tube’s zero point, and beside each figure in her husband’s small upright hand was a sentence, and the sentences were instructions.

The first sentence was: it is the tube and not the sky. The fifth was: the graduates will not believe a number that has no year on it. The ninth was: do not let them marry you to it again without the instrument in the room. The twelfth was: get a second opinion from something that has never been to a ceremony.

The second letter was the one that undid her, and she read it twice, and then she sat down on the edge of the desk she had been reading at, with her hands not steady, and the High Room was six flights up and the porter was asleep, and she sat there a long time.

The last line of it was this: it was always the instrument, Ilse, it was never me, I am sorry you will have to do it yourself.

* * *

The ceremony was performed at the appointed hour on the twentieth of May, and it was performed by a single person.

The High Room was locked and the south door of the balcony was opened, and the wind came in off the snow and went straight through her, and she was in the black gown of the office, and the gown had been made for a person who was about to stand still for eleven minutes and had not been designed for a person who was about to stand still and be cold.

“Attend,” she said, to a point of light eleven thousand kilometres up. “Present and attending. The term is fulfilled to the best of the office, and I want it minuted that the term is fulfilled. The ninth bar of the ring is not cut, and I am not going to cut it, and I do not accept that any of you are married, and I will not be married, and I will do the office as far as the office goes and then I will stop.”

Ilse Marchetti performed the ceremony alone, without a spouse, on the balcony, reading the terms to the sky, and the equinox did not move.

The Provost Renn attended, and the Sub-Chancellor, and the Precentor, and the man from the Treasury, and the Assistant, and forty-one of the senior students, and they stood behind her at the rail while she read four sheets of vellum aloud in a wind that lifted the waxed linen at the corner, and not one of them interrupted, because the terms do not permit it, and because the College had never in nine hundred years had anything to interrupt.

At the end she folded the sheets, and the Provost asked whether the College was satisfied that the term had been fulfilled, and Ilse said, “The College is satisfied that I fulfilled it. Those are two findings, and only one of them is mine.” And the Provost wrote that down, in the minutes, in full, in front of everybody, which she was not required to do.

The instruments were found, over the following decade, to be off by four tenths of a second, and corrected.

The polar axis was reset, and the reset was entered in the instrument book, and the book says that the error was discovered by the office of the Astronomer in the year of the correction, and does not say by whom, and does not need to. The Attested Time did not change by a tenth of a second, because the Attested Time had been right, and there is a line in the tenth-year report that says so in those words, and it is the only line in nine hundred years of reports that anybody has ever been able to check. The 1963 table went down to the press room at Ander, where it was later used for something else entirely. Timo Vasar was not the Astronomer and never once asked to be, and he was still at the College in the eleventh year, doing the Transit with a catalogue of his own, and he had returned the sextant to the lower valley school and had been thanked for it in writing.

On the south balcony at Col de Serre there is a ring of one thousand and seven short bars cut in the granite, and one square of granite left smooth, and the square is the ninth from the bottom, and it is the same height as the bars a foot to either side of it, and in winter it holds ice on the sunless side. The ring is walked round twice a year by two people who are not married to anything, for the purpose of counting, and the count is a formality, and it is performed anyway, and Ilse Marchetti stood at the rail with her own hands on the cold iron and did not cut it.
