# The Sea That Sued the Shore

![Cover art: The Sea That Sued the Shore](../art/advanced/71-the-sea-that-sued-the-shore.webp)

Pell kept his office above a chandler’s shop on Anchor Lane, where the rent was low, the stairs were narrow, and no client of his had ever arrived by carriage. The firm was called Pell & Odds because the second name belonged to his father’s partner, who died in 1897 with the firm insolvent, and because in ninety years nobody changed the sign. He had swept the floor that morning and set out two chairs.

The matter arrived in an oilskin bag shaped like a sextant box, carried up those stairs by Nell Fargo, who was twenty-two and had been articled to him three years, and had cycled in from the pilot station, where the messenger buoy had anchored at seven with an express packet and no statement of the matter. Inside were nine sheets of wet foolscap, a chain, and a waxed note from the buoy-master.

“The plaintiff will appear by deposition at Sallow Point on the ninth,” Pell said. “Low water at nine-forty, so you will want to be quick about it.” He said a thing once and did not say it twice. Nell squared the sheets, which she always did with wet paper and got wrong.

They were depositions, and they were not the depositions of an event. A deposition in that court is the testimony of a party taken from the party in person, and this plaintiff was a person, so it had to turn up.

“The sea,” Pell said. “It has been in litigation for four hundred years and has never once been represented by anyone but this firm. My father was admitted to the sea’s side in 1834, and I am the only attorney admitted since, and I am sixty-four, which is why there are two chairs.” He turned the newest sheet to face her. “The case fills one paragraph. The sea does not write.”

The claim took less than a minute to state. The Shore Tithe was a custom of the coast, older than any charter, by which the inhabited shore paid the sea one-fortieth of everything it took out of it, reckoned at the equinox. In 1604 that year’s tithe had been discharged, so the shore said, by delivery of one white hart, received without dispute. Since then the shore had paid nothing, and the sea had never accepted it.

“Then they have stopped paying,” said Nell.

“They stopped paying in 1604,” Pell said, “which is not the same as the defendant having ceased to pay, and the difference between those two is the whole case, and the whole case is hopeless. We shall not win the money. One-fortieth of four centuries is a sum neither side could pay, and if it were paid the plaintiff would stop, and a plaintiff that stops has been settled. I want an order requiring an account, once a year, in any form the plaintiff can read. Then the next four hundred years will be slower than the last four hundred.”

“And the Alderman thinks we are mad.”

“The Alderman has been sitting on the fourth landing for an hour.” He put the chain in his pocket. “Nell, take out a clean sheet. If we are going to be ridiculous we shall at least be legibly ridiculous.”

* * *

The Court of the Strand sat at low water on the third flat below the salt house, because the salt house kept the tide book, and the tide book was the court’s jurisdiction. There was no bench. There was a granite boulder two paces high and four long, half-buried in the shingle, and the sea kept its upper face wet and had never managed to keep it dry.

Alderman Tarrant came down the shingle at ten past nine with the tide book under his arm and a leather tube in his hand. He was forty-nine and had held the office of Clerk of the Shore for eleven years, and the office was municipal, and he was not a lawyer and had never pretended to be.

“The court is in session,” he said, and laid the strip flat against the granite. The words stood up out of the film of water in small dark letters that did not run and did not move, and they took the better part of a minute to arrive, and the whole flat waited while they did.

“Take the depositions of the plaintiff at Sallow Point on the ninth day at the hour of low water, and hear the defendant upon the same ground at the same hour. Let the file of the court be brought up to date in the interval. Let the party that would have the file brought up bring the entries, and no other.”

On the walk down, Nell said, “A rock is not a judge.”

“A rock,” Pell said, “has never been cited for bias, has never taken a bribe, and has never raised its voice. I would take those three over a third of what is on the circuit.”

Nevin Crow was already on the flat with two clerks and a handcart of paper, and introduced himself as instructed by the Salt Commissioners for the defendant, and hoped there would be no difficulty about the form of the proceedings.

“There will be no difficulty,” Pell said. “The form has not changed since your client’s great-grandfather’s time and I have no wish to improve on it.”

“The Commissioners will tell you the thing that frightens them, since nobody in this case has. If this court orders an account, the Commissioners must produce an account. Their returns begin in 1811, and before that they kept day-books, and half of those are gone in the ordinary way. An order to account does not make them. It makes us admit they were never made.”

“I understand,” Pell said. “Then we are agreed on one point, and I shall put it in the record. The defendant does not want the money. The defendant does not want to be asked.”

“The defendant wants to be left alone,” Crow said, “and your client wants to be asked every year until one of us is dead, and only one of us can be held to anything.”

The defendant produced a single leaf of vellum from an iron box, and Tarrant read it aloud twice. It was the receipt of the tithe of 1604, subscribed by the Clerk of the Shore of that year, and at its foot was a blot of grey sand in the shape of a thumb, with an impression in wax beside it. It was a mark, not a signature, and nobody disputed whose mark it was.

At that the stone wrote, in a corner of its wet face, four words: LET THE DEFENDANT PRODUCE. Crow produced the day-book for March 1604, in which the tide-clerk had entered the delivery of one white hart, alive, delivered at the equinox and released beyond the mark.

* * *

The salt house was one room, one press, and one door opening onto the flat, and the sill of that door was worn into a channel by four tides a year. The Record of the Strand stood in the press in a long roll, and it had been kept at the water’s edge since 1561.

“I want the Record.”

“It is a roll, and there is no floor long enough to unroll it, so you unroll it along the flat in the wet.” Tarrant unlocked the press. “That is why it is never in the building long.”

They unrolled it along the flat, a hundred and forty paces, the seaward end in the water, and at the ninth place Pell stopped.

“Nineteen leaves,” he said. “Not cut out. Cut off at the gutter, which wants a knife and a steady hand, and whoever did it did not touch the binding, because the roll is still whole.”

Tarrant came back with a slim volume bound in boards. “The register of destructions,” he said. “It is complete. Every entry is signed and dated by the clerk who made the destruction, as the Salt Books Act of 1698 requires, and I have checked it, because it is the one thing here that has never been in question.”

“Then nobody has done anything wrong.”

“No,” said Tarrant. “That is what the register says, and the register has never been wrong about a date in a hundred and twelve years.”

“Have the day-books for those nine years.”

“They are not here. The building was swept in 1901 and the old sweeps went into the sea with the rubbish.” He hung the key on its nail. “If the quires were damp, they were damp, and the Act permits it, and my predecessors were not liars for following a statute with a number on it.”

Pell asked for the flood dates, and Tarrant pointed without turning round at the inside wall, where the years the sea came up to high water were painted in white lead in a column sixty feet long, the oldest so worn the figures had gone to ghosts. Because the paint was renewed every spring and never scraped, the earlier coats stood proud of the wall in ridges.

“Springs,” Pell said. “You repaint it every spring.”

“Somebody has to,” said Tarrant.

* * *

The deposition of the sea was taken on the ninth, at low water, and the procedure was nine thousand years old and consisted of a lead line, a sounding rod, and tallow in the bottom of the lead. Pell was the only man alive who might conduct it, a schedule of 1726 being what allowed the plaintiff to be put to the question by an attorney admitted against interest.

“What do you hold of 1604?”

The lead went down. It came up heavy, and the tallow was grey with the things in it, and he read them off one after another, in the order the sea had taken them, which was the order everything is taken in. Nets. Ballast. A mast with the ring still on it. Three names, not his to say twice. A dog. A shutter. A kettle. No hart, and nothing white.

The flat was quiet, and Crow stood up and said, “Thank you, Mr Pell.”

“Your point is that acceptance is not ingestion,” Pell said. “The hart was not given to be held. It was given to be let go, and it walked away, and the plaintiff’s mark on your vellum is its signature and nothing more.”

“It is,” said Crow. “And it is the only document in this case made by the plaintiff’s own hand, and it says the plaintiff received something, and your soundings do not contradict it, they merely fail to confirm it, which is not the same thing at all.”

Pell had no answer, and Nell saw that he had none. What he had thought, at the ninth gap, with the tide coming up his boots, was that the safe floods four times a year, that the water which entered the safe went into the sea, and that the sea does not sort.

He said, “Fold the roll,” and they folded it, and he said, “Bring the wall tomorrow, and the register, and a dry sheet for each of the nine years.”

* * *

The hearing was held on the eleventh, at the same hour, on the same ground, and Crow argued the statute of frauds, and argued that a discharge once made was a discharge in fee simple, and argued it with the schedule of accretions, the schedule of foreshore leases, and the Commissioners’ minute of 1852, and he put in evidence that no sum had been demanded in four hundred years, and that a party who waits four hundred years to ask is not asking, he is establishing a rate.

“That is my whole case,” Crow said, “and I put it at your service, Mr Pell, because it is your client’s case too, and because the two of us are the only men on this shore who would both rather be elsewhere.”

“Your case is good,” Pell said. “I have been listening to it for two days and I could not defeat it, and I am not going to try.”

“Then what are you doing?”

“I am going to make your file produce a witness.” He set the register of destructions on the stone’s flat face, and the wall beside it, where Tarrant had chalked the four years in which the sea had actually come into the salt house. “The Act of 1698 permits the destruction of a damp quire. The Act does not define damp. Your predecessors supplied the definition, and it is on that wall, and it says four times.”

“Seventeen, nineteen, eighty-six, ninety-nine,” said Tarrant, reading his own figures. “The rest of the wall is not a destruction.”

“Then the register has nine entries and the wall has four floods, and both were kept by the same office, and they disagree.” Pell turned to the shingle, where two thousand people had come down to hear whether the sea was owed anything. “Here is what I am going to do, and I am saying it now so that neither of you can tell me afterwards that I sprang it.”

He turned back to the stone, and to the tide, and to the two thousand people standing in the shallows.

“My client’s evidence is its memory, and my client has never had a lapse in it.”

* * *

He put the lead where the gap was, and asked the sea, in the form the court allows, what it held of 1621.

For a while nothing happened, and Tarrant began to say that the question had been put improperly, and then the water at the seaward end of the roll went flat in a shape about four feet long, the way a pond goes flat under a cloud, and the salt standing on the shingle at that edge lifted and set itself down again in lines.

Not words. Words arrived afterwards, and were read by three people at once, and were gone by the time the tide touched them.

I do not know that there was a year. I do not have 1621. I have what came into me in 1621, in the order it came, and I have never thrown anything away, and I have never put anything in order that was not already in order. Some of it is standing up on this beach now because you have asked me for it, and because it was written at the water line in wet. You may read it until the tide.

Tarrant read it. He had to, because the clerk of the court reads what the court has made, and his voice went, and he read it again standing up, and the flat was so quiet that a gull going over would have been an event.

Received of the shore one-fortieth of the catch of the year, in kind, at the equinox, the same being tendered by the fishermen of Cawsand and elsewhere and not otherwise. The white hart of 1604 discharged that year’s tithe and no other.

Nine times, in nine years, in nine hands, and the last of them in 1899, and not one of the nine clerks a liar, because each was doing what the register said was permitted and what the wall said was not, and each believed the register, and the wall was behind him the whole time.

Tarrant came down from the flat and stood in front of his own table with the tide coming round his boots, and said, in the voice of a man reading out a list, “I have repainted that wall every spring for eleven years.”

“Yes,” said Pell. “You did it out of habit, and it is the only reason we are all standing here.”

Crow did not look at the register, which was the correct professional thing to do, and after a moment he said, “My clients will want to know whether the other six quires can be accounted for.”

“They cannot,” Pell said. “That is my point, and it is now yours, and the court will hear both of us say so. Your file is not a bad file. It is a file with nine holes in it, made on purpose by honest men, and every one of those men was relying on the other eight, and the man who could have stopped it was behind the wall with a brush.”

* * *

The judgment was delivered on the fourteenth, at the hour of low water, and it took the court the best part of two hours to receive, because the stone does not hurry and Tarrant must read it aloud as it forms.

“It is ordered that the defendant shall deliver to the plaintiff one white hart, and shall deliver one yearly, and that the first delivery be made before this court adjourns.”

“It is ordered that the defendant shall keep an account of the tithe in a form the plaintiff may read, and shall read that account itself, once a year, at the equinox, aloud, upon the strand, at a place where the plaintiff can hear it.”

“It is ordered that the account be kept whether or not it amounts to anything.”

Then Tarrant said, “The court has more,” and the words began again, smaller, in the corner, the way a man writes a footnote because he has thought better of it, and the clerk of the court read them out, and the clerk of the Shore read them after him, because he was the one who would have to live in them.

The court notes that the defendant has produced no record of payment for the years following the discharge of 1604, and that the Record of the Strand was found to be discontinuous in nine places, and that the presumption of continued payment is accordingly refused; and every sum said to have been paid since 1604, not being evidenced, is held to be unpaid and may be called in.

The solicitors for the Commissioners read that twice, and then a third time, and the number two hundred and fifty appeared three times in the margin of the schedule of accretions before the day was out, because two hundred and fifty years of foreshore had been held on the footing of a payment that no longer counted, and the sea, which had never asked for a square yard of it, now owned the argument.

The hart was brought up from Bramble Down at five o’clock by two men and a boy, and it was not white, and the boy had lied about that, and the court allowed it, because no statute required it. Tarrant entered the delivery, and the plaintiff’s mark was taken on the sand in the ordinary way, and the engrossment of the judgment, which was the first receipt the sea had held in four hundred years, went into its keeping where the water was deepest.

* * *

The account was first rendered in the spring following, and the Commissioners reckoned it on their own returns, because the returns were the only evidence they had, and the returns for a coastal fishery in a bad year are not impressive documents, and one-fortieth of what they showed, less the costs of reckoning it, which the Finance Act of 1901 allowed against the tithe, came to four shillings and two pence.

Tarrant read it on the strand at the equinox, at the third flat, in a voice nobody had ever heard him use. The sum was tendered in coin and taken by the water in the ordinary way. The court recorded that the plaintiff was noticed.

The year after it was three shillings and ninepence, and the year after that two shillings and onepence, and then it went below a shilling and stayed below a shilling, because the reckoning was honest and the returns were small and the costs of reckoning it grew every year, since costs are what they are and nobody has ever made them shrink by wishing.

By the fourth decade it was a farthing, and a farthing is not nothing, but it is beneath the sum for which a person may be troubled in a court, and Pell wrote to the Commissioners as counsel for the plaintiff, and the letter is in the file, and it says that the annual sum is below the minimum of this court and that the plaintiff does not ask to be paid.

The stone wrote: LET THE ACCOUNT BE KEPT, WHETHER OR NOT IT AMOUNTS TO ANYTHING, which was the third sentence of its judgment, said again because a court may repeat itself, and because the Commissioners’ solicitor had been heard to hope that the court had not noticed which way round the third sentence was.

So it was a farthing, and then a third of a farthing, and then for eleven years a third of a farthing, because the amount was reckoned to the nearest whole farthing, and the nearest whole farthing to a third of a farthing is no farthing at all, and the court made a further order in its own hand, that where the reckoning produced no farthing the plaintiff be tendered one farthing in addition, on account.

The account was read at the equinox for two hundred and fifty years, by nine clerks of the Shore and then by forty-one and then by one, on the third flat below a stone that had by then been described in a schedule appended to a transfer of the freehold as a natural feature and not as a court. The sums were three farthings, two, one, one, none, and one on account.

In the two hundred and fiftieth year, at the equinox, Tarrant’s successor read the account out in the same words, and the sum tendered was one farthing, and the water took it as it takes everything, and the court, which is a boulder two paces high and four long and has never once raised its voice or lowered itself, recorded that the plaintiff had been noticed, and the plaintiff was noticed.
