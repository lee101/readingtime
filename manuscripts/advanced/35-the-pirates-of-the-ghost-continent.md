# The Pirates of the Ghost Continent

![Cover art: The Pirates of the Ghost Continent](../art/advanced/35-the-pirates-of-the-ghost-continent.webp)

The Sallow Kid came out of the Spindrift with her ballast ice groaning, and Merrin Vale woke with salt on her teeth and a calculation she could not finish: ballast at eleven percent, lift two knots under, and the Screaming Slats lying across their beam at dawn, a shelf of moving air that had chewed through four ships and wanted a fifth.

“Mornin,” said Bex from above. “We’re pretty.”

They were not pretty. The Sallow Kid was forty years of patched copper and bad weather, and she leaned into the current like a man leaning into a lie. Bex was twelve, three days into his first watch, and everything he steered toward looked like a reason to be proud.

Under Merrin's boots the chart bench held the Deadlight chart: every current in the high air from the Slats to the Sundering Coast, drawn in her own hand, corrected in two colors of ink, the last correction three years old.

“No we’re not,” she said. “How’s Renn?”

“Captain’s at the rail with the glass. She’s got the southwest fogged over and she won’t say so.”

Captain Isolde Renn stood with a spyglass the size of a scupper and her shoulders set like a dropped boom. She was forty-one and at sea since she was nine, with the stillness of a person spending real effort on being calm. Her grey coat had the cuffs burned to the elbow and no insignia anywhere, because she had given up her commission and kept the coat.

“You’re in the Slats,” Merrin said.

“I’m beside them.”

“You’re in them.”

Behind the fog, forty feet out, grit moved sideways in the air, and loose canvas on the rail snapped flat, held flat, and popped.

“Old Slate came through this in ’09,” Renn said, “and came out with all four masts and his name. We do it too. The chart you’re standing on has the Slats’ western edge eleven degrees off. I checked your correction against the glass log in ’23.” She lowered the glass, and her voice got quieter. “You were nineteen. You were good. You were wrong, and then you were wrong harder, and there’s a difference, and I have spent three years standing on it.”

“I know,” Merrin said.

“Then say the rest of it.”

“No.”

Renn turned back to her glass. The fog moved.

* * *

The commission arrived on a signal kite two days later, and it was a very good commission, which was the problem.

A salvage factor called the Ninth Ledger had put a bounty on the Deadlight chart and a second on the ship carrying it. Whoever delivered either would be paid in ballast ice, harbour writs, and a pardon for two generations of undeclared salvage. That last part is what makes a kind offer from a salvage company recognizable.

“It’s a bribe dressed as a schedule of fees,” Ossa Quill said, reading over her shoulder. Ossa ran the rigging and the winches and had hands like a dock crane. “They want the chart because it’s the only document anybody’s got that says where the wrecks are.”

“They want it because it works,” Renn said. “Does it?”

The Deadlight looked back at Merrin, ink and salt and three years of not being looked at.

“Yes.”

“Then we’re pirates on a schedule, and I’d rather be pirates on none at all. Take us north.”

* * *

North of the Slats, where the air thins and the light comes up hard and blue off the cloud floor, the sky begins to keep things. Not clouds. Things.

The first was a spar pointing nothing, three hundred feet of line behind it and no end. Then a keel turned over, its planking soft as bread, its bell still swinging a slow inch on a stiff wire. Then twenty yards of bow cut off at the stern as neatly as a pie, and past that another, and another, frozen in the act of arriving.

“Wrecks,” Bex said.

“These are the ships people stopped looking for,” Merrin said, and heard how it sounded, and made herself finish. “That’s what the chart is. Not a salvage survey. A list of everything anybody gave up on.”

By her count there were two hundred and eleven. Nobody had found the Ghost Continent because it was not hidden. It had been made administratively invisible, name by name, harbour office by harbour office, for four hundred years. And a place made of the names of things nobody is looking for does not have to be found, she thought. It has to be admitted.

* * *

The Ninth Ledger came for them at the second rank of the frozen fleet, out of the grey under a hull that was probably still there and a mast that was certainly not.

Corvin Ashe had a hundred and forty crew and a warrant. He swung alongside with his deck gun traversed, and was almost pleasant about it.

“Merrin Vale,” he said, across forty feet of nothing. “I knew your mother. Sorry about the Halcyon.”

“You know nothing about the Halcyon.”

“I know the court found a navigator’s error, and I know she signed the log herself, and I know the other navigator they blamed had been dead eleven days by the time it sat.” Ashe tipped his hat, which is an insult done nicely. “I don’t need your chart, girl. I need you to hand it over so I can say you were reasonable. Nobody shoots the reasonable.”

Behind Merrin, Captain Renn had gone white, because nobody aboard the Sallow Kid had known the second half of that, and because Merrin had never once said it out loud.

Ossa got the gun crew off the bellows, one, and under the deck the ice in the ballast hoppers began to sing.

“Captain,” Merrin said.

Renn did not turn. “Take the chart. Take Bex. Take Ossa. Slip north on the sounding current and do not look back.”

“Captain—”

“That is not a discussion, that is a route. I have a warrant on me for a thing I did, and you have a chart for a thing you did, and there is only ever going to be room on this ship for one of those tonight. Go.”

* * *

They went. Merrin took the wheel with Bex at her shoulder and Ossa aloft, shouting the sail up rope by rope in the cracked voice they used when the rigging mattered. The Kid's stern went under the horizon behind her, and she watched two guns go off in a line, heard the Kid's ninth gun answer once and then not at all, and did not turn around either.

They found the Ghost Continent on the second day of the run.

It was land. It had weather and a shoreline and a smell of pitch and cold iron and old bread. But the cliff face of it was planking, plank after plank, lashed and pegged and caulked, hull riveted to hull, three hundred yards of ship in every direction, grown solid by four centuries of wind-ice in the seams. Ropes hung from the ridges like a dripping forest. Trees stood in the hollows, grown out of bilges, the right shape for trees and the wrong colour for wood. Above it all a mast stood a thousand feet high with a lantern burning at its top in clear air.

And standing on the beach, in a queue, were perhaps nine hundred of them, living in the holds of the wrecks that had not been admitted. There were rigging-menders and a woman who kept a soup line out of a galley lost in a different century. They had not gone hungry, exactly. They had gone unmourned, and nothing that happened to them was recorded, so nothing that happened to them counted.

A man at the front of the queue wore a shipmaster's coat with a name on it Merrin did not know, and he said, in the flat patient voice of a man explaining a rule never once tested, “You can’t put us on our chart.”

“I’m not putting anyone anywhere.”

“That chart is the only door there is. You put a name on a map, the world has to admit the thing exists. Write us down and the whole coast learns we just happened.” He folded his arms. “Then they come with dredges and salt the ground so it can’t grow back. And if you don’t write us down, we stay. It’s not a punishment. It’s what we are now.”

Behind him, somebody's child was laughing at a gull, and it was the most ordinary sound Merrin had heard in three years.

* * *

The weather turned before the end of the day, the way it does on a coast made of iron.

The glass fell nine-tenths in an hour and the wind came up out of the south at a force the Sallow Kid had survived once in nineteen years. Out over the shelf, where the planking is thinnest, the air holding this place together began to take it apart. Planks came off the cliff in slabs. A three-masted hull standing since before the harbour offices was levered off its bed and went over backward, and the air where it had been leaned inward, and Merrin understood what the word Unmooring meant.

It was the wind that took things nobody was looking for, and it was walking the shore now, methodically, lifting the unnamed off their hooks one rank at a time. It had reached the third rank. In twenty minutes it would reach the beach.

Ossa, at the rail, staring down: “If you write them down we lose all of this. If you don’t, that wind takes half of them before dark.”

Bex said, “So write the wind down.”

Bex was twelve. He said it like he was handing her a tool.

Merrin stood on a beach of made land with the wind walking toward her and two hundred and eleven names in her own handwriting, and for the first time in three years the thing in front of her was not arithmetic.

She had read the line wrong off Hollis Dane's shoulder in the cold on the Halcyon's third night over the Slats. Then she had said so out loud, in a room, to a widow: that Hollis had taken the sound wrong, that it wasn't Merrin's fault, Merrin was only holding the lamp. And everyone had been so kind about it. And she had let it stand, because kind was easier than accurate, and because she was nineteen, and because a mistake you owned was a mistake you could be sent away for.

That was the mistake. Not the reading. The kindness.

She spread the chart on an upturned crate, unweighted, in the wind, with Bex and Ossa holding two corners and four hundred people watching from behind a wall built out of wreckage.

“All of you standing on this beach,” she said. Her voice went. She let it go and started again. “I am not writing you onto a chart. I’m not giving you to a harbour office and I’m not giving you to a dredge. Nothing I write here tonight carries a name anywhere but onto this vellum.”

She picked up the pen.

“My name is Merrin Vale. In the year of the Halcyon I read a line wrong and I let a dead man hold it, and I have said so out loud, in front of nine hundred people and a crew of two, and I own it, all of it, in the order it happened.”

The wind stopped.

Not eased. Stopped. The whole coast went as quiet as a room, and in the quiet Merrin felt something shift under her feet, a sound like a held breath let out, and the two hundred and eleven lost ships came out of the hiding the way a shoal comes out of dark water.

They came out loud. Every one, all around the horizon, standing off the invisible into the visible at once, hulls and masts and bellwires, two hundred and eleven abandoned ships in ranks like a cathedral. The Unmooring broke in the middle and went off sideways across the sky like a dropped rope.

The nine hundred on the beach made a sound. Bex made it. Ossa made it. Merrin was not sure she did.

“Now run. Get up the planking, into the hollows.” And they went up the cliff face like water going the wrong way, while Merrin watched the ships of the air come home one by one, ringing the coast, every one of them named.

* * *

They got ninety-one children, two hundred and six adults and older people, and about six hundred more who came down off the ranks over the next four hours, carrying things they had carried a long time.

Two days of lashing followed, every sound rope in the visible sky brought into use, which turned out to be several thousand tons of it. Merrin navigated by it, giving each hull a berth and a line in the book.

The Sallow Kid was found on the third afternoon at the foot of the ice shelf, three hundred miles downwind, her sails shot to ribbons, her rudder gone, and Captain Isolde Renn in the chains with a sprained wrist, alive, alone, still holding the glass.

Merrin brought her alongside in a hull that was not the Kid and could hold nine hundred people.

Renn looked at the fleet, the two hundred and eleven sails, the woman with the pen, and said, “That’s a fleet.”

“It is.”

“Where did you get it.”

Merrin thought about lying, and then decided she was finished with that.

“An hour south of the Slats at two-fifteen, on the day of the Halcyon, in front of four hundred witnesses,” she said. “I read it wrong and I let a dead man hold it, and this is the whole of what I have, and you can look at it.”

Renn took the chart and was a long time looking at it. The wind moved through two hundred masts above them like a crowd settling down to listen.

Then she gave it back and put her good hand flat on the rail. “Ash?”

“Taken. Half his crew. Ash went down with the Kid’s second gun, and I don’t know that I’m sorry about it, and I don’t know that I’m not.”

“Good.” Renn looked out at nine hundred rescued people and a coast that would be a place now — charted, defended, ugly, ordinary, alive. “Log the fleet. Every hull. I’m not the captain of this.”

“Yes you are,” Merrin said.

“That’s a demotion I accept from you and nobody else,” said Isolde Renn, “and you can put it in the book that way.”

Merrin wrote it in the book that way.
