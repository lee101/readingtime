# The Fae Market That Charges for Names

![Cover art: The Fae Market That Charges for Names](../art/advanced/36-the-fae-market-that-charges-for-names.webp)

At eight seventeen on a wet Tuesday, Ilyra Rook received a summons on pink paper and learned that her wife’s name had been scheduled for auction.

She was forty-one, a licensed debt collector for the Ashen Ledger, and dressed for the office in a charcoal wool coat buttoned to the throat. A silver chain crossed her vest. Her black gloves were mended at the palms with silver thread. She carried the ledger under one arm and a brass seal in her fist. The seal was legal only when pressed by someone who had accepted the debt. She had not accepted this one.

The summons smelled of rain and crushed violets. It said that the estate of Morrow Vell owed the Nightglass Market one thousand six hundred true names. The collateral was Rhea Sol, beloved of Ilyra Rook, to be offered at the third bell after midnight.

Rhea was thirty-four, a stone mason with copper hair in a practical knot, strong hands, and a voice that could make a roomful of masons lower their hammers. Fourteen years earlier, she had nearly died of silver fever. Ilyra had signed a green bond naming Rhea as surety because the fae physician would not wait. She had thought she was promising repayment. She had not known that fae law made a beloved name collateral, or that a name could be sold, used, and made unanswerable by the woman who bore it.

She went to the market.

The Nightglass Market appeared when the last city bell faded, inside the abandoned tollhouse behind Saint Orra’s Row. Ilyra passed through a black rectangle in the brick and descended stairs slick with moss. At the bottom, a door opened onto a cavern of black silk and amber light. There were no coins on the stalls. Glass bottles held laughter, small suns, salt knots, and memories of dead strangers. A vendor sold a box of thunder with a silver clasp. Another displayed three green eyes in a velvet tray, each blinking at a different rhythm. Lanterns the color of insects revealed the market rather than lighting it.

A clerk met her at the first arch. Small and sharp-waisted, she wore a red waistcoat beneath a coat stitched with tiny silver doors. A pale scarf covered her mouth, but her voice arrived clearly in Ilyra’s ears.

“Collector Rook,” she said. “You are late.”

“The summons came two hours ago.”

“Time is a poor servant here. Coin is refused.”

“Coin is a lie told by metal. A name is a promise: claim, price, and risk.” Seven rings clicked on the clerk’s hand.

Ilyra kept one hand on the ledger. “I’m here to collect.”

“You are here because your name is in the bond.”

The clerk walked into the auction court. A bronze bell hung beneath a glass roof without a clapper. At its center, a silk cord divided bidders from sellers. One golden thread hung from the cord. Ilyra heard Rhea’s voice say her name, and her knees nearly failed.

“Lot seven,” the clerk said. “Rhea Sol. Beloved by one. Remembered by three hundred and twelve. Current price, nine hundred true names.”

A human man in a gray coat, perhaps thirty-eight, raised a hand. He wore polished black gloves and carried a velvet case containing a name-shaped darkness. “I bid twelve hundred.”

The clerk tapped the cord. “Name offered?”

He opened the case. A black card emerged, blank except for a date six years old and the mark of a public court.

“That is not a name,” the clerk said.

“It is the name I no longer use.”

“Then it is a secret. Secrets do not pay.”

Ilyra stepped forward. “Lot seven is collateral on a human bond. I am authorized to collect the balance.”

She presented the pink summons and the green bond. The clerk read without touching them. “Morrow Vell, the witness, is dead. The term has matured. The surety was spoken in the debtor’s hearing, but she did not answer.”

“Then the bond is void.”

“Human law may say so. The market does not.”

The clerk pointed to a brass door in the floor. A notice read: REPAYMENT BY LIVING NAME.

“Offer a living name of equivalent weight, and the collateral is released,” she said. “A title is not a name. It is a hat.”

Ilyra thought of the word she had carried for forty-one years. “Mine is.”

“Your birth name?”

“Ilyra Rook.”

The clerk’s eyes brightened. “Once surrendered, it cannot be reclaimed by this court. The payment is held in the market’s vault until the debt is settled. The living person remains, but the name becomes unusable for filing, inheritance, search, or sworn promise. The market will call you surety.”

Behind a black curtain, a woman in a lace veil sat beside a basin of water. Her fingers were stained blue to the second knuckle. A sign read: MEMORIES, VERIFIED AND UNWANTED.

“Bring me the memory of the night the bond was signed,” the woman said. “I will give you the witness’s exact recollection: whether the surety consented, whether the physician lied, whether the name was spoken under fear. If the recollection clears the bond, Rhea is returned. The memory will be gone. You will know what happened, but not how it felt.”

The clerk raised two fingers. “A third path. The living person named as surety may challenge the bond before the third bell. If the challenge fails, the challenger pays. If it succeeds, the debtor’s estate pays.”

A fae with a lantern for a head pushed forward. Inside the lantern, a flame wore a silver crown. “The estate is empty. Morrow Vell died without kin. The market will take the challenger’s name.”

The outer bells rang. A velvet rope near the entrance snapped, and Rhea stepped through.

She wore an ochre wool coat, scuffed at the knees, and carried a mason’s canvas roll over one shoulder. Dust silvered her hair. She looked at the silk cord, the golden thread, and Ilyra.

“You said you were collecting a debt,” Rhea said.

“I was.”

“You did not say whose.”

“The summons did. The summons is not an explanation.”

The clerk bowed. “Rhea Sol. You are named.”

Rhea set down the canvas roll. “I heard the challenge. I renounce the guarantee.”

“No,” Ilyra said.

Rhea’s gaze sharpened. “You signed it.”

“I did. You do not get to decide my answer now.”

“The market will take your name if I challenge.”

“Then do not challenge. I will not leave you as collateral.”

“You did not make me collateral. You let that happen.”

The crowd leaned closer. Rhea stepped to the silk cord. “I did not answer the physician. I did not give my name. I do not consent to its sale.”

The clerk raised a hand. “The surety speaks.”

The golden thread lifted. A note rang from the bell above them, though it had no clapper. Rhea gasped and put one hand over her heart. A pale line appeared around her throat, thin as a necklace.

The gray-coated man raised his case. “I renew my bid. One thousand five hundred.”

“Nothing yet,” the clerk said. “The name is being transferred. It will remain audible to the bidder, but no longer answerable by Rhea.”

Ilyra understood. The market did not mean to erase Rhea. It meant to make her unnameable to anyone who did not own the name. The beloved would become a person whom no one could summon, command, or comfort with the word that proved she had been loved.

“Name your use,” Rhea said.

“To walk through a door that knows me,” the man replied.

“No,” Ilyra said.

The clerk turned to her. “State your remedy.”

The legal remedy was clear. Ilyra could put her own birth name into the bronze basin, surrender its use as a promise, and release Rhea. The loophole was a challenge by Rhea, with Ilyra’s name as the wager if the court rejected the truth. The memory route might expose the physician’s lie, but it would leave Ilyra knowing the facts of the night without the feeling that had made the facts bearable.

The third bell began to toll. Its sound traveled through the floor, tables, and black water beneath the market. At the final stroke, the auction would close and Rhea’s name would pass into a stranger’s keeping.

Ilyra removed the silver chain. Beneath it was a lock of her own hair tied with black thread, worn against her collarbone through every winter she had worked as a collector. It was not a name, but it was a promise: she would not sell what she had been trusted to carry.

She placed the chain and lock in the basin. “I understand. The name must be spoken freely. Once spoken, it cannot be reclaimed. Rhea Sol will be released.”

Rhea seized her wrist. “There is another way.”

“There is a way that lets someone else decide what I lose.”

“That is not a bargain. It is a reason.”

Ilyra remembered Rhea unconscious in a bed too narrow for both of them, the physician’s silver instruments, and the green bond beneath her trembling hand. She had signed because the alternative was watching Rhea die. She had spent fourteen years telling herself that a necessary act was therefore harmless.

“It is my debt,” she said. “I choose the clean repayment.”

Rhea stepped back. Ilyra placed both hands on the basin and spoke her own name.

The market took it with a soft click, like a lock closing in a distant house. The golden thread blazed. Ilyra felt the absence begin in her mouth, then behind her eyes. Her name had always been a key inside her, a bright thing that opened doors in her own life. It left without tearing anything else. She remembered having a name, remembered its shape and weight, remembered the way it had sounded in Rhea’s mouth. She could not remember the word.

The clerk wrote one black stroke. “Lot seven is released.”

Rhea’s hand flew to her throat. The pale line vanished. She said, “Ilyra.”

Ilyra heard the word and knew it had been meant for her. The knowledge was clear. The feeling beneath it was gone.

Orrin Kest lunged for the cord. The clerk caught his case. “Bid closed. You offered a date, not a name.”

His black card crumbled. Inside was a blank where his old name had been. His anger looked less like greed than fear.

The clerk turned to Ilyra. “You are unnamed by law. The market will not accept testimony under a personal name.”

“Then I testify as the debtor.”

“That is a title.”

“It is the only thing I have left.”

The clerk considered. “Very well. Debtor.”

The word gave Ilyra a shape in the market’s records, and the shape did not open any door. She could work, speak, and be remembered. She could not be summoned by law.

Rhea took the chain from the basin and looped it around her wrist. “Come home.”

They climbed the mossy stairs. Behind them, the market resumed its business beneath the silent bell. Outside, rain had stopped, and the city smelled of wet stone, lamp smoke, and cooling bread. Ilyra had no name to give the watchman who opened the tollhouse door. He looked at her face, then at Rhea, and opened wider without asking.

At home, Rhea poured warm water into the basin and washed ink from Ilyra’s fingers. Their marriage had been made by shared meals, repaired roofs, and the patient decision to keep choosing each other. Rhea pressed her mouth to Ilyra’s knuckles, wrist, and the inside of her forearm. Ilyra’s body remembered desire even when her mind could not name the old ache.

“Stay,” Rhea said.

“I have nowhere else to go.”

“That is not the reason.”

“I know.” Ilyra caught Rhea by the waist. “I want you to stay.”

They undressed without hurry and climbed into the bath together. Rhea’s laugh shook water from her eyelashes. The absence inside Ilyra was no longer empty. It had room for Rhea’s hands, Rhea’s breath, and the knowledge that they were both adults who had chosen one another with full understanding of what had been taken.

At dawn, Ilyra returned to the Ashen Ledger and surrendered her seal. Pell, the master collector, looked over the documents.

“You have no name.”

“No.”

“Can you work?”

“I can collect debts under the designation ‘the debtor.’”

Pell’s mouth tightened. “That is not a profession.”

“It is what I have.”

He wrote it into the register, then placed a blank line beneath it. “When you have a new name, bring it here. I will not charge a filing fee.”

Ilyra accepted the paper. Her name was gone, and the market had kept every part of the bargain it was owed. Rhea’s name was still hers. The market had not taken the living woman beside her, only the word that had once been used as a lock.

At sunset, Rhea met Ilyra beneath the tollhouse in a green coat, carrying two cups of hot cider. The steam rose between them, fragrant with cinnamon and rain.

“Tomorrow,” Rhea said, “we will begin again.”

“Without a name?”

“Without anyone else’s claim on it.”

Ilyra looked toward the black rectangle of the doorway. Somewhere below, a bell waited for midnight, and somewhere in the market, a name was being offered to the highest bidder. She did not enter. She turned toward the street, where the lamps were coming on one by one, and walked home beside the woman whose name had been returned.

The road was wet, the city was awake, and for the first time in fourteen years, no bargain stood between them.
