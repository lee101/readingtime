# The League of One

![Cover art: The League of One](../art/advanced/143-the-league-of-one.webp)

Halversgate pays one man. It has paid him for eleven years and it has never once been told what he does, and in eleven years he has not given his name, and he has not agreed to be photographed or filmed, and there is no picture of him that anybody in the town has ever recognised.

He can be anywhere in Halversgate inside a quarter of an hour of being called, from wherever he was, by roads and lanes he has never been shown.

Every call costs him one day of his life. Not an hour. A day, and it does not come back, and he has been doing this since he was nineteen and has never told anybody the number.

* * *

The arrangement began in a February with a letter through the door of a house on Weir Lane. It said that a person would come when they were needed, at any hour, and that the town should leave a lamp in the window of the library, and should not wait to be sure.

The library put a lamp in the window. The lamp is still there and it is still lit every night and nobody in Halversgate can tell you when it was last checked.

The money came afterwards, for four years, in an envelope, and then stopped for two years, and then a woman called Enid Sallow brought it back with a form. It is twelve pounds a month, paid out of the harbour fund, and it goes into a post office box emptied on the first of the month by a man called Grover Fell, who signs for it and takes it round in a van.

He has never seen the man who collects it. He has never seen the man who brought the envelope in the first place. He has seen the van.

* * *

The chip shop on Dock Street is called the Salted Wharf and it opens at half five in the morning.

The woman who runs it is called Lissa Meikle and she is forty-four, with dark hair going grey in a stripe at the front and a red fleece she wears every day of the year, and she has been opening at half five since her mother died.

She knows the man only by what comes through the door. In eleven years he has come into the chip shop four times, and on three of those four he asked for a bag of chips and a cup of tea to take away, and on the fourth he asked for nothing and she gave him the tea.

That is the entirety of it. She is careful to say that he has never once been difficult, and that she has never once been frightened of him, and that she has never got used to him either.

* * *

The votes are the other thing the town knows. It has voted twice to stop paying him, and the vote was close both times, and the second time was closer.

The first was in the November three years ago and went against him by thirty-one out of two hundred and six. The argument then was that the harbour fund was for the boats.

The second was last November and went against him by nine out of two hundred and eleven who voted. The argument then was that two thousand one hundred pounds was two thousand one hundred pounds, and nobody in the hall used the word dues.

He was not in the hall for either. When the Advertiser asked four people in the street to describe him, three of them said some version of thin and grey and older than he looks on paper, and none of them could say a single thing he had done.

That is the thing about him. He does not do things in front of anybody. What anybody in Halversgate can describe, when pressed, is a timetable.

* * *

In February there was a call at four in the morning.

It came to the lamp, and then it came by telephone to the library, because the library takes the night number for the reading room and the reading room is awake all night for reasons nobody has investigated.

The call was from a house on the Weir Lane terraces where a woman of eighty-one had gone down at the bottom of the stairs at about a quarter to four and could not get herself up, and the daughter was in Tarnwick and was not coming back before Sunday.

The library had the woman on a list. The list is called the night list and it has twenty-two names on it and it is kept in the desk drawer and it is not printed, and the library assistant on the night shift is required to telephone somebody on it.

The assistant telephoned at ten past four.

He was there at nineteen minutes past four, and the woman was upstairs on the sofa with a blanket round her, and the door was open behind him, and the assistant has said since that she heard his shoes on the stairs and did not see him go up and could not say how long he had been in the house for.

The daughter came back on the Sunday and does not remember being in the hall or seeing the stairs lit.

* * *

Then there was the bus.

The number 9 goes from the harbour round by Weir Lane and up to the reservoir and back, and it leaves the harbour at five past four every morning, and the driver has been the same driver for nineteen years and is called Nadia Pryce and she is fifty-one and has a timetable she does not need.

On the eighteenth of March the number 9 did not go. A tree came down across Weir Lane at about ten past four in the morning and put the road out, and the crew got the nine past by half five, and by six the whole town knew, because it is a town of two thousand people and there is one bridge out.

The tree lay across the only road to the reservoir side of the town. Eleven people live up there. Three need the bus to the county hospital at Corve for dialysis on a Tuesday, and one of them was seventy-eight and had been on that bus at a quarter past seven, as he had been for nine years.

He came at ten past five, which is when the crew finished.

Nobody saw him come. Nadia Pryce says she was in the cab with the park light on, and the light went off the glass, and she has said the same words to everybody who has asked her, which are: the light went off the glass and I did not see a thing.

The tree was eighty feet of elm across forty feet of road. The council's contractor came at nine and said Thursday.

It was gone at twenty past five.

* * *

That is the sort of thing people put in an argument, and people do put it in the argument, and it is not a stupid question to ask.

It is also, anybody who has watched it will tell you, useless.

Nobody can check whether the elm would have gone on Thursday. Nobody can check the elm. It is a photograph in the Advertiser of a cleared road, and there is nobody anywhere who can say where the wood went, or whether there was wood.

* * *

Lissa Meikle keeps the night list in her head, which she did not intend and does not advertise, because she is at half five six days a week and the chip shop is where the street goes in both directions, and people talk to her while she waits for the fryer.

She did not keep it at first. It came in pieces over about three years. Somebody would say a thing in the queue and she would file it without noticing, and then in the winter it came back while she was doing the orders.

She could tell you that the coast road culvert under Ferry Row had been blocked for eleven days in the February and clear in the February after that.

She could tell you that the signal lamp in the water tower had not been lit since the year before last, and that it was the same boy who went up and lit it, and that the boy is called Tam Wexler and is fifteen.

* * *

She asked him once, in the winter, at seven in the morning with nobody else in the shop.

She said, “Do you not get tired.”

He said no.

She said, “That is not a no.”

He said, “It is the answer to the question you asked.”

Then he took his chips and went out and down Dock Street towards the water, and that was in the February before the second vote, and she has never asked him anything since.

* * *

The committee is four people and it is called the Harbour Fund Committee and it meets on a Thursday in the back room of the bowls club, and its job for thirty years has been a harbour that needs a set of lights and a paint.

In March the fund had two thousand one hundred pounds in it and a forecast for the next year that did not close, and the twelve pounds a month had been in the accounts since the February the arrangement began, as night harbour dues.

Night harbour dues had never been questioned because there had never been anything to question. There is no harbour dues schedule. There never was. The item has been carried forward for eleven years by a woman named Enid Sallow, who is seventy-nine and signs it off with an initial she never explains.

* * *

It was the second vote that made it a question. That is the strange part and nobody can get round it.

The first vote was the town. The second vote was the town. The third was a room over a bowls club with three people in it, because Enid Sallow had gone to her sister's in the August and did not come back, and it is not recorded anywhere except in a file.

They kept it, two to one.

And then Grover Fell put his hand up, which he has never done in eleven years, and asked whether anybody had told the man the money had stopped.

* * *

Nobody had.

Grover Fell is fifty-eight. He has driven the post office van in Halversgate since the year it was privatised and before that he drove a milk lorry, and he collects the envelope on the first of the month.

He has never opened it. He would tell you that this is because he is a careful man and that it is also because he has never wanted to be the man who found out.

What he can tell you is the route. The envelope is posted in a town forty-one miles away, in a county with a different name on the sign at the border, and the postmark says the post office at Cove Head, and the man who posts it is about sixty and does not give a name and has not given one in eleven years of coming to the same box at the same time.

The eleven pounds went out on the first of March. There was no envelope on the first of April.

Nobody told the chip shop.

* * *

He drove to Cove Head on the Monday and parked outside a post office, and the man was there, because the man is always there on the first of the month, and the box had a notice on it saying it was being kept clear.

The man did not give a name and Grover Fell did not ask twice.

He said, “The money's stopped.”

The man said, “Yes.”

He said, “Since the first of April.”

The man said, “Yes.”

He said, “There's nobody to pay.”

The man said, “No.”

Then he said, “Do you want the box back.”

And Grover Fell said he had been sent to make an enquiry and did not know what he was doing at that post office, and the man said that was all right, and Grover Fell got back in the van and drove the forty-one miles with the radio on.

* * *

The woman in the Weir Lane terraces in the February needed a plate at nine at night, because she had been in bed since four in the morning and had eaten nothing since the previous afternoon.

Nobody knows who ran it. Lissa Meikle had closed at two. The girl on the late shift at the garage said a woman had come in at ten past and had been very quiet and had paid in coins and had gone straight out.

That is the first week of April. By the second week there were three of those. By May, Lissa Meikle was taking a cup of tea and a packet of biscuits at ten in the morning to a flat on Ferry Row and setting them outside a door that is never opened, and the door has never once been opened while she was standing there.

She does not do it because she has been asked to. She has never been asked to. She does it because she has worked out what a day is like when nobody comes.

* * *

There is no clause and there is no contract. There is an envelope and a post office box and a lamp in a window and a list of twenty-two names in a drawer, and not one of those four things has a legal opinion in it at all.

Nobody thought about that for eleven years because it never once occurred to anybody that he would not do it anyway.

He has never sent a letter. He has never sent anything. Nobody has any evidence that he receives the envelope, and nobody has ever asked, because nobody has ever had the address.

* * *

On the second of June there was rain at nine in the evening and by eleven there was standing water across the flats at the bottom of Marsh Lane, and by midnight a woman was trying to push a car through forty feet of it with her lights on and was getting nowhere.

She got out and stood in it and shouted, which everybody agrees she should not have done, and she was heard, and it is the only time anybody in Halversgate can say for certain that he heard something rather than that he knew something.

The car was found at the lock-up on the coast road at nine the next morning, undamaged, dry inside, with the keys on the seat where she had put them.

There is no way to get a car from Ferry Row to that lock-up. It is four miles. There is one bridge and one road out, and the road was under half a foot of water until two in the afternoon.

* * *

In August somebody in the Advertiser wrote four hundred words about the lights and the ferrymen of other towns and what a coast owes itself, and it got read, and eleven people wrote in and said the arrangement had saved somebody's life in the March, and five of them could not say when or how.

The Advertiser printed all eleven.

All eleven were wrong about the arrangement. None of them knew the money had stopped in March. He had not told anybody, because there is no reason to tell anybody when there is no payment, and because a man who has gone eleven years without being asked what he is does not begin volunteering the answer.

* * *

In the third week of September the school bell at the low road went in the night.

Nobody knows how. It is on a bracket in a field and it is rung four times a day by a rope, and there has been a boy on that rope for two hundred years because somebody has to be, and the boy was ill that week.

The bell went at eleven at night and about two hundred people came out of their houses, and there is a recording, because a man on Ferry Row had his phone out of habit, and in the recording you can hear the ringing and then, at about the fourth ring, it stops.

Not fades. Stops.

The bell is a casting from the last century and the rope is new and the bracket is iron, and the whole arrangement is four thousand pounds of somebody's money.

It rang again on the Monday morning at the proper time and has rung every school day since. The bell is fine and the rope is fine and the bracket is fine. Nobody knows who was in that field at eleven o'clock at night.

* * *

He is fifteen and he goes up on the evening of a Friday and comes down, and it has been a job in his family since his grandfather's time, and it is paid eleven pounds a month out of a tin in the switch room, and the tin has been empty since March and he had not told anybody.

He got out of the bucket and put the torch on the platform and telephoned the library, which is the number that goes round at night now, for anything at all.

The library telephoned at ten past nine. There was nobody there at eleven and nobody there at half eleven.

The library telephoned the police, which took the rest of the night. The police were in Halversgate until Thursday, found nothing, and used the word nuisance twice in a report and once to the boy.

* * *

The library has not closed the night number. That is the last piece of business anybody can report.

The reading room is awake all night. The lamp in the window is lit. The night list has twenty-two names on it and it is kept in the desk drawer and it is not printed, and the assistant on nights must telephone somebody on it, and there is somebody on it.

In February it was cold and the woman on the Weir Lane terraces could still not get up the stairs, and somebody carried a plate up at nine at night and knocked and it was taken in.

Nobody saw who took it.

* * *

The bus route and the tree and the eleven people on the reservoir side of the town.

He came at ten past five and the tree was gone at twenty past five.

* * *

There is a bench at the harbour end of Dock Street, cast iron, with the town arms on the back of it and the paint gone off the arms.

It faces the water and not the town, which is why it is the one bench nobody sits on with their back to a parade of ships, and it is where the town goes at about a quarter to eight in the morning to look at the weather and the boats and each other.

On the first of March of the year the money stopped, and the number of calls in the year that ended was two hundred and nine.

Two hundred and nine days of a man's life, taken out of him and not put back, in the twelve months in which Halversgate saved one thousand five hundred and eighty-four pounds by not paying twelve pounds a month.

* * *

The bench is there every morning and it is not occupied, and nobody can say who has ever seen it occupied, and the town has voted twice to end an arrangement with a man in it rather than ask him a single question.

At a quarter to eight the water is grey and the boats are knocking and Lissa Meikle comes down with the shutters up and stands at the door for a minute, the way she does, looking at the weather.

The bench has a cup of tea on it, and it is cold, and it has been cold for some time, and Lissa Meikle looks at it and then looks at the water and does not go over, because the last time she went over she was told there was nobody to help.
