# The Moon Owes Us a Favor

![Cover art: The Moon Owes Us a Favor](../art/advanced/12-the-moon-owes-us-a-favor.webp)

Tal Vey collected what the gods forgot before they learned regret. She did it in a black coat tailored like a verdict, with silver thread at the cuffs and a narrow copper chain looping her right wrist three times. Minor gods knew her by the chain’s three pale knots. Major ones knew better than to use her name.

The god of unfinished journeys sat behind a desk made from a collapsed rainbow, wearing travel boots under a toga. One boot was unlaced.

“You are late,” he said.

“You retained counsel after sunset.”

“I retained talent. You merely retained consequence.”

That was their oldest joke. Tal set the brass debt case on his desk. Moonlight bent over her knuckles, though there was no window.

Three centuries ago, she read, the Moon had granted humanity a night during which nothing new could arrive: no infant, meteor, or plague. The first astronomers stored that night beneath their mountain observatory in a lens of lunar ice. Now the Moon had called a hearing against the world.

“Is the debt to be severed?” the god asked.

“The favor is to be recovered.”

“Severing is the usual outcome.”

“Recovery means the contract ends. Nothing more.” Tal knew the difference. A severed favor returned nothing to the claimant. A recovered one had to be accounted for. “Where is the observatory?”

“On the Vharn Teeth.”

“And the astronomer who first used the lens?”

The god of unfinished journeys slid a travel order across the desk. “He has been assigned to you.”

Tal, thirty-six, looked at the name. Oren Kade was forty, with one eyebrow, a stoop from an old spinal injury, and a short beard revealing an old burn along his jaw. His mountain-blue coat beneath a rust wrap was buttoned to the throat. He waited by her shoulder as though he had always resented her.

“I was told the observatory was abandoned,” she said.

“It is not.”

“The astronomer is not the same one.”

“No.”

“Then this favors repayment has acquired a guide.”

“He can read the constellations.”

“He can read anything badly. The distinction is cheap.”

Oren’s voice emerged low and dry, with the cadence of a man who had once been listened to and preferred not to be. “I can read them accurately. The trouble is they are only visible when I lie.”

The god examined his nails. “Ah.”

Tal held out her hand. “Say something untrue about the weather.”

“It is raining silver thimbles where no rain has fallen for nine years.”

His pupils became black eclipses, stars moving through the distance behind them.

“It is raining copper coins,” Tal said.

The stars vanished. “That was also a lie.”

“A dull one. Are you entertaining only before breakfast?”

They left. Tal took the north road. Oren preferred it to the southern swamp, whose willows could hide his stars and whose air smelled of drowned apples. “The swamp is a pleasant corridor beneath noble trees of astonishing height,” he said, and led them onto the black road above the flooded fields.

The Vharn Teeth rose on the horizon, white and terribly steep. Beyond them the sky remained clear except for a pale, fixed point west of the peak. Tal knew the catalogs. No star stood there. The point had not been visible during the last lunar census.

“The favor has found something to hold,” she said.

“Cerren,” Oren said. “Or Ceruleus. It is named differently depending on who is trying not to be frightened.”

“There is no star.”

“There is now.” His mouth tightened. “The charts call it the Burnt Nail. It entered our sky three years ago, falling toward the Athrib Sea until the mountain lens took hold. I reported it fixed.”

“You falsified a chart.”

“I was thirty-seven and fond of employment. After three years, I tried to correct the lie. No one else could see the star, so truth sounded like a peddler selling rooftop lakes.”

The road climbed through pines. By dusk they reached an old goat path folded into a vertical wall. Oren’s leg failed on the first stairs, though his grip on his brass astrolabe tightened.

“An admirably direct staircase,” he said.

“Your leg disagrees.”

“My leg is an unimaginative critic.”

At the top, a narrow ledge crossed a gorge above a fresh white drop. Tal tested each foothold before Oren placed his heel beside it. Halfway across, the ledge cracked.

Oren dropped to one knee, clutching the astrolabe. Beneath him, the mountain opened like a splitting book.

“I am going to die,” he said, and the sky departed from his eyes.

Tal seized his coat. “Lie about it.”

“I am dying because a duckling beneath me has solved taxation.”

The constellations blazed on the gorge’s black walls. Tal followed their line, shifted her grip, and hauled him up as the ledge dropped.

They lay breathless in the snow.

“Thank you,” he said.

“For the tax duckling?”

“For not letting the mountain put an adjective on me.”

At dawn they reached the Vharn Crown Observatory, a crooked stack of stone and copper roofs lashed to the summit. Ice filled its doorways; inside, wool dried over a stove beside bitter tea. Director Ilyra Sen met them in three buttoned coats, fifty-two years old, broad-shouldered, with an iron-gray braid and one hand curled from an old injury.

“Tal Vey,” she said. “Every keeper knows the empty palm. I also know Oren Kade. You are less impressive than the file.”

“The file was written by people with excellent eyesight,” Oren said. His pupils darkened, and something in Sen’s face loosened.

“You still need lies.”

“Truth is undignified.”

Tal placed the Moon’s order on the table. “The hearing begins now.”

At the top stood a circular lens chamber beneath a copper dome. Ceruleus hung over the valley as a hard white mote, neither advancing nor fading. Snow fell below the dome, but no flake crossed the lens; beyond it, the stars doubled and sharpened.

At the center rested the favor, lunar ice around a darkness no candle could warm. It tugged at the chain on Tal’s wrist.

“Bring the founding record.”

Sen brought an iron-bound book. Oren opened it as though handling a wound. The final entry bore his name.

“That was not a lie.”

He read: “The Moon’s favor holds all arrivals at the threshold. Ceruleus waits beyond it. The favor was never meant to arrest an object. To recover it, the object must be permitted to arrive.”

“You knew.”

“I suspected. By the time I proved it, the lens showed nothing under honest observation. The lie was the only sight available.”

“You destroyed your career for a note nobody believed.”

“My career was already a chair being carried toward a cliff. I jumped.”

Tal took the founding record to the lens. The darkness inside the sphere turned toward her. Heat moved through the floor, gentle at first, then hard enough to tighten the leather of her boots. The fixed star began, by fractions, to descend.

Oren seized her sleeve. “It is moving.”

“That is rather the point.”

“The Athrib coast is directly below us.”

“It cannot reach the surface in the days we have.” Tal raised her marked palm. In the center, a black crescent appeared—the collector’s sign, a hole cut in her flesh by no blade. “Moon, I call the favor you lent three centuries ago. Return what is mine to collect.”

The lunar ice cracked. Cold flashed through the room, and Ceruleus slid visibly downward. Somewhere far below, stone boomed.

Oren stared upward. “Ceruleus is a reckless candle dropped by a careless constellation into a black well.”

Stars flooded his eyes. Their light drew lines across the real heavens, and Tal saw the star’s path for herself: steep, unwavering, aimed through the coastal salt marshes into the Athrib Sea. The route left the world’s sky after the lens slowed it. Ceruleus would arrive, but the remaining time could be made to last thirty-six years.

Thirty-six years, Tal thought. Long enough for its burning mass to shed itself into wind and harmless rain.

She knew the moon’s arithmetic. The favor could turn this arrival only against collateral. Tal’s empty palm was the room’s one honest account: not a nation, not an oath extracted from a frightened witness. A collector could borrow. That was how the old laws were made, before borrowing became a virtue only when the lender suffered.

Oren looked at her hand. “No.”

“I have not offered.”

“Your mouth has.”

“The court accepts my mouth.”

“The court can be argued with.”

“The court is me.”

She had spent her life taking choices from others and calling the absence justice. Now the Moon offered the one currency she had never counterfeited: time that was hers to spend.

Oren took her wrist, careful of the silver knots. “Thirty-six years is not the price. It is the length of the flight. The price is whatever thirty-six years costs when you are seventy-two and still alive.”

“Then I shall make a poor old collector.”

He held on. His hand was warm, callused at the fingertips, trembling with cold or fear. Tal could have pulled away. Instead she turned her palm beneath his and touched the scar at his jaw with two fingers. He went still.

“Stay with me through the hearing,” she said. “Not as a guide. As a witness.”

“And afterward?”

She looked through the lens at the bright nail descending toward the world. “Afterward, you will tell me the truth.”

“I warned you about the consequences.”

“Then lie when you need a constellation. I will survive the arithmetic.”

The black crescent in her palm began to spin. The Moon’s voice entered as pressure behind the eyes, cold and clean.

“I will turn Ceruleus around the world for thirty-six years. It will lose its burning heart. I will demand thirty-six years of Tal Vey’s remaining life. Each year of the star’s passage shall take one year from her.”

“Accepted.”

“Tal,” Oren said.

She tightened her fingers around his, then released him. “Accepted.”

The bargain struck. For an instant Tal felt every day she might have had: silver mornings, ordinary grief, the taste of wine on a winter evening, mornings waking beside someone who knew her silence. Then the Moon took one year. Not the memory. The year itself, distilled into weight, entered the broken sphere. Ceruleus curved across the heavens.

The lens collapsed into moon-clear fragments. Tal caught Oren before he struck the floor. When he looked up, lines of age creased her eyes, and an old ache entered her left knee.

Sen called the mountain crews. Settlements along the Athrib coast moved inland. Towers rose to watch the star while Oren wrote the truth in a new record. Each night he still read the heavens through deceit.

“The drowned star rides above the world like a royal barge with a bankrupt flag,” he told her.

She laughed until her new ache protested.

Thirty-six years later, Tal stood in the rebuilt observatory wearing green velvet, because Oren had declared black clothes at seventy-two a moral failure. Her white hair sat beneath a silver cap, and the black crescent in her palm had become a white seam. She could no longer feel debts or make the collector’s sign.

Oren was seventy-six, nearly bald, his curved spine clasped around the brass astrolabe. Ceruleus had vanished at the bargain’s end after spending its final years in the upper air, its last fire dispersed into a green night and rain sweet of iron.

They stood together on the open gallery. Snowmelt ran through the new gutters, and below them the valley shone with the labor of rebuilt roads.

“Anything?” Tal asked.

Oren looked up. His eyes were only his eyes.

He said, “The last light is a coin the sky has spent, and somewhere beyond it a door is opening for better visitors.”

The stars took shape behind him, delicate and white.

Tal watched them wheel over the mountain. “Good lie.”

“Good man,” he said. “Awful liar.”

“I know.” She leaned into his shoulder, and for once let someone else hold her weight.

Below, the world continued beneath an ordinary sky.
