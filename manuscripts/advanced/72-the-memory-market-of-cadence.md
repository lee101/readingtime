# The Memory Market of Cadence

![Cover art: The Memory Market of Cadence](../art/advanced/72-the-memory-market-of-cadence.webp)

The Memory Exchange occupied the top floor of the Assay Office on Fetter Lane, and it was quiet, because nothing in it was urgent. Under the Memory Act of 1802 no person might buy, sell, or pass on a memory of another person without that person’s consent, and the Act did not say when consent had to be given, and the answer the courts settled on in 1841 was that it could be given afterwards, which had turned a prohibition into a filing requirement. Ilse had read section nine on her first morning and had thought it badly drafted every working day since.

The statute book lay open on a lectern by the window, at section nine, because somebody had once asked to see it and the clerk had decided the request could not be refused. In the margin, in a brown hand older than the binding, somebody had written: I do not see how this is to be prevented.

“Fothergill drafted it in 1802,” Ilse said. “He wrote that in the margin, and the printer printed the margin, and it has been in the margin ever since.”

“Does that mean the Act’s no use?”

“It means the Act is honest. It says what a person would want, and then asks them for it afterwards, and by afterwards it is too late to matter and too easy to sign.”

Ruben sat opposite her with his hands flat on the desk. He had a claim ticket, which is what the Register issues when somebody means to sell a memory of their own, and it was the only document in Cadence that a boy of fourteen could hold and be entirely certain of. The fourteenth of June to the nineteenth of September, he said. A summer.

“Who appraised it for you?”

“A man in a car park at Bexley. He said he was an appraiser, and he said summers were fetching two hundred a week in the south this year.”

“And what did he give you?”

“Eleven pounds.”

Ilse looked at the entry. The Register classified by distinctive content, and a summer with nothing distinctive in it was a Class Four summer, and a Class Four summer was eleven pounds, so the man had been right about the market and dishonest about the goods. “The appraisers are licensed,” she said. “There are eleven of them, and all eleven work for one firm, because the Exchange awards the work, and the Exchange is a company, and the company has a shareholders’ agreement.”

“Does that matter?”

“It matters if you were going to do something about it.”

Ruben was not going to do something about it. The money was for a laptop, and the laptop was for a course, and the course was for an apprenticeship he had not been offered yet, and eleven pounds was eleven pounds more than he had. Then he looked at the terminal and asked what a summer was actually worth.

“Two hundred and forty to nine,” Ilse said, “if it had a first communion in it.” She turned the screen. “And under section thirty-one of the Minors’ Transactions Act of 1873, a sale of goods by a person under eighteen is voidable at that person’s election, and a memory is goods, because the Act says goods and means goods, and Ferris v. Bream in 1889 settled it.”

“That’s real?”

“It’s real. It’s in the little red book, which nobody has opened since 1911, and there is no form for it anywhere, and no fee, and you do it in a room downstairs with the magistrate’s clerk and a piece of carbon paper.”

Ruben stood up and thought about it with his hands in his pockets.

“No,” he said. “Eleven pounds is eleven pounds, and if I void it I haven’t got eleven pounds, and I want the course.” He nodded at the open statute book. “Is that your job, then? Being honest about prices?”

“No. My job is that the consent is filed inside twenty-eight days or the transaction is void, and that I never sell to a person who has not got the paper. The Exchange pays four pounds a filing and it costs me about six, so I do nine hundred a year and think about this instead.”

“So which are you?”

“The second one,” she said. “The first one is done by machines.”

* * *

The woman was fifty-one and came in at four o’clock and put a card on the desk, face up, and it was not a claim ticket. It was a purchase enquiry, and the name on it was Enid Marrable, and the memory wanted was the eleven minutes immediately preceding the death of her husband, Colin Marrable, at the Royal Free on the fourteenth of March, of a ruptured aneurysm, which is a thing he would not have known anything about.

“They’re in,” Ilse said. “They’re in, and they’re cheap. A terminal event is a terminal event, and the eleven minutes before one are of no use to anybody, and they have been through the market once and come back unclaimed, and the board price this morning is thirty-one pounds.”

“Thirty-one pounds.”

“Thirty-one, and the filing fee, which is four, and I cannot file for less than the Exchange allows me to charge you, which is also thirty-one.” She wrote it on a pad. “It is the cheapest thing I will ever sell you, and I want you to know that before you buy it.”

The woman looked at the pad for a while. “And afterwards I have it.”

“You have it. You will sit with it in one of the six rooms on the fourth floor with a nurse outside the door, and it takes about forty minutes, and when you come out you will have been there, and you will know exactly what his last eleven minutes were, and they will be the eleven minutes of a man who did not know.”

“That’s what I want.”

“I know what you want.” Ilse turned the terminal around. “I’m required to establish the consent before I take the money, and I always establish the consent, and I’m going to run the provenance, and you are going to watch me do it, because you have as much right to see that file as anybody in this room.”

The Register gave the chain in nine entries and Ilse read them twice. The first was dated 1998 and it was a sale by a person of the same surname, a girl, aged fourteen, of Ridge Road, and the consideration was entered in the field for consideration as nine pounds.

“Your daughter sold them in a bar in 1998 to a man who said they were for a school project, and they have changed hands nine times since, and the ninth owner is a company, and the company holds them in trust, and you cannot buy a memory from a trust.”

Enid Marrable read the entry twice herself. “Nine pounds,” she said.

“Nine pounds. And the consent was filed. That is the part that matters. She consented, and she consented on the proper form, and she consented afterwards, which is what section nine permits, and the certificate is in the file, and it is valid, and it was valid the day she signed it.”

“Then I can’t have them.”

“You can have them if the current owner sells them to you, and the current owner is a trustee, and a trustee selling an asset to a member of the public at a table is a breach of trust, and the trust’s solicitor will say so in the first letter he writes you.” Ilse closed the file. “What I am about to say is not advice and I am not going to say it twice. I have not said it.”

Enid Marrable looked up.

“Your daughter’s consent is valid and the sale is voidable, because in 1998 she was fourteen, and section thirty-one of the Act of 1873 says so, and no consent cures it, and I do not know why that is so, and neither does anybody else, and that is why it is still on the books.”

* * *

The letter from the trust’s solicitor arrived on the eleventh and was four pages long and entirely correct. It was drafted by a man called Mr Prendergast of Prendergast and Vale, who had been a trustee’s solicitor for twenty-two years and who wrote in a manner that made every sentence sound as though it had been read aloud to a committee and then minuted.

The client does not accept that the said document is operative. The client notes that the sale of 1998 was made by a person then of the age of fourteen years, and that no election was made within the period allowed by section 31, and that the client says, without prejudice to the above, that the client could not have known and would not have acted differently had it known, and that the equities therefore do not arise.

“That is four hundred pages of being right,” said the counter clerk.

“Read the last line of it,” Ilse said. “He says the equities do not arise. That is a man telling us the answer is no and inviting us to find a better question.”

“What’s the better question?”

“The 1873 Act gives a remedy, and the remedy is not damages, because you cannot put a price on the only copy. The remedy is restoration, and restoration is what the Act provides, and it is administered by a stipendiary magistrate in a room with a gas fire and no form and no fee.”

“You haven’t thought about this.”

“I have thought about it since the fourth of the month. What I have not done is acted on it, and I am not going to, because it is not a commission, and because I do not sell things, and because the Exchange’s conduct rules say a broker shall not institute proceedings against a member in respect of a matter arising out of a transaction, and I am a member, and I would rather be struck off than be a person who did that.”

“Then what are you going to do?”

“The boring thing,” she said.

Enid Marrable did not leave. “Then I will commission you,” she said. “I will not pay you thirty-one pounds. I will pay you properly, and I will pay you to sit in that chair and fight a trust for as long as it takes, and I will pay it in advance.”

“No,” said Ilse.

“You have just told me the answer.”

“I have told you a statute.” Ilse closed the ledger. “A broker who takes a commission in a matter arising out of a transaction is a broker who has stopped being able to read the register, and the register is the only thing either of us has.”

“Then who is going to do it?”

“I am,” said Ilse, “in about six years, in a gas-lit room, for nothing. And you are going to have to help me, because I cannot get into that house in Sheffield, and you are her mother.”

* * *

The boring thing took six years and most of it was waiting. A voidable sale must be avoided by the person able to avoid it, and the person able to avoid it was a woman of thirty living in Sheffield who did not want it avoided, and Ilse went twice to Sheffield and once to a solicitor in Croydon who had been the family solicitor in 1998 and who produced, from a box, a photograph of the girl at a table in a bar with a man Ilse never identified, and the girl was holding a form, and the form was the consent, and the girl was signing it with the pen the man had handed her.

“I don’t want it back,” Kayleigh Marrable said. “I’ve had thirty years of not having it. I’d have to sit in a room with it, and I’d have been there, and I’d have been there when he was frightened, and I’d have been there when he was bored, and I’d have been there when he looked for the nurses, and I know all of that anyway, because I was in the room, because I did all of it.”

“You were in the room.”

“I was in the room until the machine started. Then somebody made me go out to the corridor, and I sat in the corridor for two hours with a cup of tea, and I have been in that corridor every day since, and I would like it noted that the corridor is the part I mind about and not the eleven minutes.”

“It is noted.”

* * *

So she said yes, eventually, in a solicitor’s office in Croydon, on a form Ilse had had to write herself. The claim went to the stipendiary court. Ashgrove Nominations was dissolved in the first year and re-registered in the second under another name, and the trust moved to Jersey, and the certificate of the trust took two years to obtain and arrived with a stamp on it that a clerk in the Postgate had to ring a solicitor about. The trustees sought to strike the claim out for want of prosecution in the third year. The eleven minutes stayed on the board and were bought four times in that period by people who could not use them and sold them on within the month, and each transaction was filed, and each filing cost four pounds, and Ilse paid all of them out of a drawer that was not an expense and appeared nowhere.

Prendergast wrote at the end of the fourth year to say his client had no objection to the form of the order sought, and that the only matter in dispute was the costs, and that his client’s view of the costs was that they should be borne by the public, and he put it in those words, and he meant it, and Ilse read two other files and found that he had written the same thing in both, and that he was not a liar for it.

* * *

The stipendiary court at Fetter Lane was two rooms over a registrar’s office, and there was a gas fire in the outer one and a bench in the inner one built for a man who had not been a magistrate and had to be sat on sideways, and Mr Dunmore was seventy-nine, and he was delighted.

The claim was Enid Marrable’s, in the right form, on four sheets of paper, and the statute relied on was section thirty-one of the Minors’ Transactions Act of 1873, and Dunmore had not heard of it.

“You have brought me a memory,” he said, “and you have brought me a Victorian statute, and between them they will take the whole morning, and I have had nothing but bail on Wednesdays for a very long time.” He put on his glasses and looked at the fourth sheet. “Section thirty-one. A sale by a minor. Voidable at his election. Yes, I know this one. I used it on a bicycle.”

“Or thereabouts,” said the clerk.

“Or thereabouts,” said Dunmore. “Madam, do you understand what you are claiming?”

“I understand that my daughter was fourteen,” said Enid Marrable, “and that I am fifty-one, and that it has taken me six years and a great many letters to be allowed to sit in a room with eleven minutes that were never hers to sell.”

“That is not a claim to a schedule of damages. That is a claim to a thing.”

“Yes. I have been trying to buy it for six years, and it is thirty-one pounds, and I have a certificate of consent, and it is valid, and my lawyers say it is valid, and I have never once doubted them.”

And Prendergast, who had come to argue costs and had not come to argue anything else, stood up and said that his client withdrew the objection, and that the client wished it recorded that the client had never wished to be in the position of arguing that a man of seventy-eight could not do his job.

“I never wished that either,” said Dunmore, and put his pen down, and read the order, and made it. The eleven minutes of Colin Marrable, of the Royal Free, of the fourteenth of March, were restored to Kayleigh Marrable, and were transferred by her to Enid Marrable, and were filed into the Register at a cost of four pounds, and the Register gave back a receipt with a number on it.

Then Dunmore said, “Madam, before you go. You are going to sit with these, and I have read enough of that file to know that you do not have a proper terminal-mortality certificate for the session, and the nurse’s office is through that door, and if you wait an hour they can do you a proper one, and it takes an hour, and I would rather you did not do it on a Friday.”

“Will it still be there in an hour?”

“Madam,” said the magistrate, “it has been in a filing system since 1998. It will be there in an hour.”

Enid Marrable sat in the room on the fourth floor for an hour and ten minutes, and came out, and went down to Fetter Lane, and at the counter of the Memory Exchange she sold the eleven minutes back into the market for thirty-one pounds, and the counter clerk, who was new, entered it and gave her a receipt, and she put the receipt in her coat pocket.

She bought them again the following March. The clerk at the counter did not know her and did not need to, and the eleven minutes were on the board, and thirty-one pounds was the price, and she paid it, and she filed, and she took them up to the fourth floor and sat with them again for an hour, and came down, and sold them again in the same week. The Register entry that afternoon was a sale from Enid Marrable to the Exchange and a purchase by Enid Marrable, in adjacent lines, in the same hand, and the counter clerk ruled a line between them with a pen, and then, after a moment, took the pen away and ruled it out again, because there was no rule requiring a line.
