# The Republic of Lanterns

![Cover art: The Republic of Lanterns](../art/advanced/79-the-republic-of-lanterns.webp)

The Republic of Lanterns stood on a slope, and the slope was the city, and the city was nine hundred steps from the river landing to the top of the ridge, and at dusk on the eleventh of October the Steps were black from the bottom to the top. They had been black every evening for two hundred and seventy-four years, which is to say they were black until a man came up them with a lamp on a pole, and then they were gold, and the Republic began.

Salvo Enna came up them at a quarter past six. He was forty years old and had been coming up them since he was nine, first with a pole and his father’s lamp, and then with the ring of nine thousand keys on his belt, the oil can on his hip, and the ladder on his shoulder, and for twenty-two years the Fourth Ward had been his alone, and the Fourth Ward alone carried two thousand one hundred and eighty of the Republic’s nine thousand two hundred and forty-one lights. He went up the way he had always gone up, which is slowly, and he did not look at the river, because the river was black and had been black the whole of his life and was not a light.

At the foot of the Steps was a painting nine feet high and eleven across, put on the wall in 1738. The founder of the Republic stood in it in a plain brown coat with a wick-trim in his right hand and a single lamp in his left, and behind him the painter had put the whole city lit from the soles of the steps to the top of the ridge in one stroke of yellow, the stroke a man makes when he has been told what a painting is about and believes it. Salvo had looked at that painting every evening of his life and had never once asked why the founder of a Republic of Lanterns was a man with a wick in one hand.

Above the painting, cut into the limestone in letters a foot deep, was the first clause. The Republic is constituted by the light. Four lines below it, cut on the same day in the same hand, the Republic is that which the light falls upon. Then: a person is one who holds light given freely, and keeps it, and answers for the flame. Then: no lantern in this Republic is common. Then: the lamplighter is an officer of the Republic and takes no part in its government. Six lines, one clerk, two hundred and seventy-four years, and the whole constitution of a city of nine thousand citizens fit into a doorway and still fitted.

He lit the first lamp at the seventh step and the second at the eleventh, and by the fortieth the Steps were doing what they always did, which was to turn the town above him into one warm shape with a hundred edges. You cannot light a city one lamp at a time. You can only start somewhere and go, and the going is the thing: nine hundred steps, two thousand one hundred and eighty stops, an hour and a half up and an hour of coming down in the dark inside your own light, which is a strange place for a man to be.

* * *

The Register had six columns and had had them since 1720. NUMBER. CITIZEN. KEEPER. WARD. LAST LIT. And a sixth, added in 1734, headed EXTINGUISHED. The NUMBER column ran to nine thousand two hundred and forty-one. The KEEPER column was kept perfectly, in a fair hand, because a keeper is a person and persons are what a Register is for. LAST LIT was the best-kept column in the Republic and had never once been a lie. The CITIZEN column was empty.

Not empty in the way a shelf is empty. Ruled in 1720, ruled again in 1790 when the old ink ran out, ruled again with new ink, four ruled lines to every written line on the page, down nine thousand two hundred and forty-one rows, and in two hundred and seventy-four years not one name had gone into it. Forty-one names were in the EXTINGUISHED column. Salvo had read that column perhaps a hundred times and had never once thought about the other one, because a Register of Citizens with nothing in the CITIZEN column is still a Register about citizens, and the column headed CITIZEN is the column for names, and the name goes on the row, and the row of a lamp that has never been extinguished has no name on it. The first name in the EXTINGUISHED column was his father’s, given in 1763, and he had never looked at it.

“Why does the Office keep a column for a thing that never happens,” said the young woman behind the counter, “when the Office keeps a column for a thing that has never happened in two hundred and seventy-four years?”

Salvo put the key ring on the Register, which he did not do. “Your column is not empty. Your column is the reason this city knows what it owes for.”

“I am Registrar Sarn. I have held the office two years and I have the fair copy, and the fair copy is the same. I have read the judgement of 1741, pasted into the front of the Register by my predecessors, and the reasoning is sound, and if you went to the upper house about it this afternoon you would lose.”

The Registrar was thirty-one and her collar was new and the brass had not been polished, and she had a rule she worked by, which Salvo understood inside a second, because he had one too. The Office is funded by the levy. The levy falls on the citizen. The citizen is a lamp, lit at the Office’s cost and paid for at the Office’s cost. If the citizen were a person, the person would buy the oil. The Registrar did not say this. She did not have to. It was in the fourth book, the oil book, three columns and a total, up nine years running because the wicks were tired, and a solvent Office has not wanted this question for a hundred years.

* * *

The Fourth Ward had four lanterns with a placard nailed to the shutter, and only one of them mattered, and it was the two hundred and seventeenth in the order of the ward, and its name was Perrin, and its keeper was a woman of seventy-nine called Wenna Kell, and Perrin had been lit every night of its life, and in the spring of this year Perrin had been given, freely, to Wenna Kell’s granddaughter.

That was the whole of the campaign. There was no programme and no committee and there were not two men in the city to carry a banner, because a banner is a thing a person carries and a lantern does not, and the placard on Perrin’s shutter was a board with a number on it and one sentence under it, and the sentence was: the register is the proof.

Wenna Kell sat in the window of the house at the six hundredth step with a shawl on her knees, because it is cold on the Steps after six and always has been. She had kept Perrin for thirty-one years, and it had been given to her by her mother, and her mother by a man who carried oil up these Steps before there was a Registry. People came and stood in her light and did not sit down in it, because in this city it is a thing not to sit in a neighbour’s light.

“Wenna. Is it true about the column.”

“It is true about the column.”

“Two hundred and seventy-four years, Wenna.”

“Yes. Since before my mother was given it.”

And the thing the ward could not get over, the thing that made a banner unnecessary, was that it was not a scandal. It was a form. At the Lamp Office, on the level, in the room where transfers were done, for two coppers and a stamp, a person could declare that a lamp was given freely to a person, who then kept it and answered for the flame, and the clerk wrote the keeper’s name and the date in the KEEPER column. Nobody instructed anybody. The Office did not profit by the gifting and did not lose by it, and the clerks stamped the forms because stamping forms is what an afternoon is for.

There had been eight thousand and forty gifting forms in the Republic in eleven years, and nobody had ever counted them but a clerk doing the oil, and every one of them had turned a lamp from common into kept. The sum of given lamps was nine thousand two hundred and six, and it was the only figure in the whole of the lamp trade that was going up.

“Wenna,” said a woman at the back, “if Perrin is a person, what is my son?”

“He is a keeper,” Wenna Kell said, “of a person,” and she said it kindly, and she had said it four hundred times, and the woman in the doorway put her hand over her mouth and went up the Steps with the light of Perrin going over her from the side all the way to the top, which is what everybody in this city can do and nobody ever mentions.

* * *

The ladder came down on the nineteenth at seven in the evening and the Fourth Ward came out onto the Steps, and the Registrar came out with her, and a boy after her with an office lantern, which has no name on it and no keeper, and the Registrar observed that aloud, to nobody, in the voice of a woman reading her own sentence.

“Salvo Enna. I will do this in order and in the open. One: I have read the judgement of 1741, the reasoning is sound, and I am not asking the Republic to overrule it. Two: the levy falls on a citizen, and while the citizen is a lamp the Office is paid by nine thousand lamps, and if the citizen is a person the Office is paid by nine thousand households, and I want it recorded that I said that before I asked and not after. Three: the Fourth Ward has lodged a resolution to hold a test count on the last night of the quarter, and I am required to attend with the extinguishers.”

“I know what you attend with,” Salvo said.

“You do. The Office’s standing to extinguish is the oldest in the Republic and has never once been used, and it has never been used because in two hundred and seventy-four years no light in this city has ever gone out, and I want you to hear the shape of that sentence, because it is a sentence about you. The Office can strike a person only where a person can be put out, and that power has never been tested, because there is one man on the Steps and he has never dropped a lamp.”

“You want me off the Steps,” Salvo said. “You have dressed it in a book.”

“I want the Register read.”

And a man at the back of the crowd, on the fourth step, hands in his pockets, seventy-four years old, who had carried a lamp up these Steps for forty-one years before Salvo Enna was given the ward, said, “He is right about the book and he is wrong about the Steps, and both of those are mine and not his, because I am the one who taught him. The lamp in that boy’s hand is not a citizen and not a lamp, and I have been wrong about the difference for forty-one years and have never once been wrong about a wick. Put nine thousand names in that column and then what.”

“Then the Republic will have citizens,” the Registrar said, “and will have had them for two hundred and seventy-four years, and will not have to be told twice.”

“No,” Toval Marrow said. “Then the Office will have to be told once and nine thousand times, and the ninth thousand time is a woman of eighty-four on the top step whose oil comes in a cask she cannot lift.”

He was right, and she was right, and the ward went up the Steps in the cold, and the Registrar walked down to the flat, and Salvo Enna stayed where he was until the last lamp of his round was alight and then went down into his own light, the only light in that city with a name on a collar.

* * *

His father’s light had gone out on the ninth of March, 1763, in the fourth hour of the morning, in bad weather, and the Office struck the Enna name out of one column of the Register and wrote it into another, and the boy of nine was made to look at it, because a boy ought to know the name of the thing that happens when a light goes out in this city. The clerk said, that is your father, that is a citizen struck, and the boy said, is my father a citizen, and the clerk said, your father is on that line, and the boy said, that is not what I asked you, and the clerk did not answer.

He had never let a light out. Twenty-two years, two thousand one hundred and eighty stops, and on the eleventh of October, 1873, he stopped at the lamp numbered one thousand nine hundred and eighty-four, at the four hundred and sixth step, and did not light it, as he had not lit it in nineteen years and eleven months. There is a rule for that house. Rule fourteen: where a keeper is in arrears beyond two years the lamp is held in common again, and a lamp held in common is lit at the Republic’s cost, and the cost goes on the roll, and the roll goes in the oil book. The woman in that house is eighty-four and could not pay and did not say so and did not move.

“There is no page in this book with my name on it,” Salvo said, to the shutter at the four hundred and sixth step, on the last night of the quarter, “and there are two thousand one hundred and eighty pages in this ward alight tonight because I have carried a lamp up nine hundred steps since I was nine.”

He said it once, and he said it to a shut window, and then he took the lamp off the pole and set it on the sixth step and sat down on the stones beside it, and he did not light it. By eight o’clock the Fourth Ward was black from the bottom to the top, for the first time in two hundred and seventy-four years.

* * *

Doss had carried the ladder since he was thirteen and had been seventeen for four months, and that night he carried it down, set it against a shutter, went home, slept, and was woken at midnight by his sister, and the two of them stood in a doorway on the one hundred and twelfth step and looked up.

The first was Perrin, at the six hundredth, and that was not a surprise, because Perrin had been lit every night of its life. The second was not Perrin. The second was the house above Perrin, and then the house above that, and between the second and the twentieth Salvo understood, sitting on the sixth step with his hands on his knees, that he was looking up the Nine Hundred Steps at windows, and that they were alight.

The Office had put Notices of Count on every shutter in the Republic on the tenth, with a man on a ladder and a pot of size, because a notice is a notice and the Office does not have to like what it issues. The lamp at the one thousand nine hundred and eighty-fourth was lit at ten past seven by a girl of nineteen with a ladder of her own, and the old woman behind that shutter did not come to the window, and did not need to, and the Office marked the lamp, and the Registrar wrote the name.

The lamps came up the Steps out of the flat from half past six, and out of the other eight wards at the same hour, and the Office bell rang the half hour and Salvo listened to it from the sixth step and did not get up. The man at the counter looked down the length of the Register and then out at the black Steps and then back at the column headed CITIZEN, which was empty, and he was fifty-four and had been a clerk for thirty-one years and his wife was in the Fourth Ward at the eight hundred and eleventh step, and he said the thing that was in front of him, which was, “It is going to take a very long time,” and by four in the morning the Registrar had four pens going in four columns and had not once left the counter.

* * *

The first name went in at twenty past one and it was Perrin, and Sarn wrote it herself. The second was the lamp at the eight hundred and eleventh step, and then they came faster than four pens, and a clerk at the top of the Steps read the tallies down and a boy carried them up, and by eight the CITIZEN column had two thousand one hundred and seventy-one names in it, and the Registrar’s hand had begun to go, and she put the pen into the fair copy and signed the count, and thirty-five lamps had gone out and been struck, and those thirty-five were the first names entered in the EXTINGUISHED column in thirty-one years, and Sarn entered them herself, one at a time, and did not hurry any of them.

Toval Marrow came down at ten to enter his own name, and would not have the date of a gift, because he had never been given anything, and the Registrar said a name could be entered on a keeper’s oath, and he swore it with his hat in his hand, and the clerk wrote it, and he looked at the six letters of it a long time and then went up the Steps, and nobody asked him what he thought, because in this city a man is not asked what he thinks of a lamp.

At noon the Registrar closed the fair copy, and the Register was carried down to the top of the Steps on a board and set on the counter in the wind with its weight against the sill, and the count was on the top leaf in four hands, and the column headed CITIZEN had nine thousand two hundred and six names in it and the column headed EXTINGUISHED had seventy-six, and both were ruled down nine thousand two hundred and forty-one rows, and the ink on the six hundred and nineteenth line was still wet.

Salvo Enna was still on the sixth step. He had been there all night, and the Registrar had sent a boy down twice to tell him the count was taken, and the boy had come back twice without saying anything, because boys who carry a lamp up a staircase for four years learn to read a man from the doorway. She came down herself at two in the afternoon and stood on the sixth step and looked at the lamp standing on the stones beside him, unlit, wick trimmed, chimney clean, pole laid flat, and then she looked up the Nine Hundred Steps, at two thousand one hundred and seventy-nine lit windows going away from her in a line to the top of the ridge.

“You will be cold,” she said, and he said that he had been colder, and she said, “Then come and stand at the top of them,” and he got up, and picked up the lamp, and carried it, and did not light it, and climbed the nine hundred steps of the only street he had ever lit, in the dark, between two rows of burning windows, with the whole of the Fourth Ward looking down at him from its own light, and not one person in that ward more surprised than he was.
