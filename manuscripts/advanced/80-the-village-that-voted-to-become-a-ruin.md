# The Village That Voted to Become a Ruin

![Cover art: The Village That Voted to Become a Ruin](../art/advanced/80-the-village-that-voted-to-become-a-ruin.webp)

The count finished at eleven forty on a Thursday in the back room of the Longthwaite school, and the room smelled of nine hundred people who had voted in the same four hours: wet coats, paraffin, and tea drunk too fast out of paper cups. Ida Ruskin had been at the trestle table since six, and after nine she stopped announcing the tallies.

The returning officer read the declaration himself, because the declaration was a document and Ida did not trust a document to anybody else. Mr. Denby, a retired pharmacist who had counted ballots for thirty-one years, took off his glasses and put on the other pair, which was the only ceremony the village had ever devised for itself.

“Against the Riverside Logistics Park: seven hundred and eighty-four,” he said. “In favour: seventy-seven. Spoiled: three. Polling stations: four. It is recorded that the majority against is ninety-one per cent of the votes cast. I am required to state that the majority is clear, and that no provision of the Representation of the People Act is engaged by the question put, and I am told to say so for the peace of the room.”

Somebody laughed at the word clear, and the laugh stopped short of the people who had wanted the park. Royde Pethick, who ran four trucks and had been shown a haulage contract in the spring, sat with his arms folded and did not move again.

Ida carried the last bundle out to the car park, where a man with a stanley knife was cutting the good words off the sign that said VOTE YES ON THURSDAY. Nobody had voted to hang that sign. The bus would stop at the bottom of the lane in an hour, and nothing whatsoever had happened.

* * *

What had happened happened in week three, in a room above the butcher’s, because Ivo Standish would not drive into a village that had refused him, and Ida decided to take that as a courtesy and did not believe it.

He was forty-five and apologised before he sat down. “I am not going to insult you,” he said, and set out a folder. “Ninety acres. An unconditional consent granted in March, at an examination where everybody said yes. Consent is not a hope. It is what you get when everyone has already said yes, and everyone did, twice, in writing.”

Eleven million of works. A hundred and forty jobs, twenty-two of them for people within four miles. “I voted against it,” he said. “I have four children. I bought the site because the ring road goes past it, and the ring road is not a matter of opinion.”

Then he opened the folder, and Ida understood that everything before it had been the part of the meeting he had wanted to enjoy. “Section four of the Ruins Act 1911. A ruin is any place the permanent population of which has been diminished by the collective decision of its inhabitants. A ruin is scheduled, at the end of twenty-one days beginning with the entry of the decision in the register of elections. Your county entered you on the thirtieth, and it did not ask anybody, because the Act does not permit it to.”

“So it is not a vote against me,” Standish said. “It is a vote against the existence of the site, and I am not here to say they were wicked. I am here because the site is now a scheduled ruin, my funder’s facility is repaid at the end of the quarter, and I am a man who has signed things.” He closed the folder. “No repair. No new building. No alteration. No removal of an inhabitant. I do not find them obscene. I find them four words long, and I cannot get around them.”

* * *

Nell Cragg was twenty-nine and had been in the Historic Environment Record for six years, which meant she had learned that a building is a set of facts and a story is a set of opinions, and that the record admits the first politely. She had listed a flax mill with a bell that had not been rung since 1931, and had put her hand on it without ringing.

The entry came down on a Tuesday in a folder. Ruth from the desk stood in the doorway without coming in, which she had never done before, and said, “There is a whole place in, Nell. Longthwaite. Nine hundred residents. It is a ruin.”

There were three things to do and only twenty-one days. The map, from the Ordnance Survey. The schedule, four prohibitions printed as four lines on a card. And the population schedule, which was the one that made her stop: a page of names, headed INHABITANTS PROTECTED, and a note that no inhabitant was to be removed without the written consent of the office.

“There is no repair in it,” Sowerby said. “There is no build in it, and no get-out. Three of the four are about the building and one is about us.”

“I have read them three times.”

“Then what is it for?”

“For keeping a place exactly as it is,” said Nell. “In every case I have ever filed, the thing being kept is stone, and stone does not have opinions about the winter. In this case it has nine hundred people in it, and I have signed a document promising not to change it.”

She wrote the description at the foot of the entry in the register’s flat style. RUIN. Population permanently diminished by collective decision. Nature of interest: collective. Then she telephoned Ida Ruskin, whose name was on the schedule of inhabitants, and who was not the first name in it.

“Ms. Ruskin, I have to tell you what your vote did.”

“It was a refusal,” said Ida.

“It was a decision,” said Nell. “Section four does not ask what you were refusing. It asks whether the inhabitants of a place have decided something together, permanently, and the answer is yes, on the ninth, by ninety-one per cent, and the word for yes in that section is collective, and I entered it on Tuesday, and you are now a scheduled ruin. I am the person who did it, so if you would like to be angry at somebody, please make it me.”

* * *

By February the valley had discovered what a schedule does, which is nothing, everywhere, all day long. The water tank over the allotments lost a roof panel in a gale in October, and a temporary cover was an alteration and a permanent one was a repair, and the county used both words in the same paragraph.

The worst of it was the wall. The retaining wall below Ash Row came down in January and left a hole in the ground at the edge of a public road, and the hole could not be filled, because filling it was a repair. The highway authority owned the road up to the boundary. The boundary was defined by the schedule. The schedule defined the boundary by reference to a map of 1911, and the map of 1911 showed the road as it had been before the road was built.

In April Sowerby said the thing he had been not saying since December. “Four of my reports a week say a thing is dangerous. Dangerous does not have a clause. Dangerous is not in the Act. I have all four words of that Act memorised, and I have never once made one of them do the thing I needed it to do.”

Nell sat with the file open in front of her. “Everything I do is to keep something as it was. That is my whole trade, and in six years nobody has asked me to keep something as it was while it was doing that.” And at the end of the meeting Sowerby mentioned that there was a provision at the back of the Act about maintaining a ruin in its ruinous condition, and a grant attached to it, invoked twice in a hundred and ten years, both times for abbeys. Ida Ruskin wrote the subsection number on the back of her hand and put her hand over it.

* * *

The Longthwaite Estate was a charity with one asset and a deed of 1889, drafted by a man called Prebble, and which nobody in the village had ever read, because nobody had ever needed to, which is the ordinary condition of a document that has worked for a hundred and thirty-five years.

The trustees shall hold the freehold of the village of Longthwaite in trust for the maintenance of the settlement as a working settlement, and shall not sell, charge, or alien the same. And then amenity was defined: the right to build upon, to repair, to alter, and to occupy. And where amenity was diminished, the trustees should pay the beneficiary the value of the diminution, and if the value were nil, they should convey the land in satisfaction.

“They have not been wicked,” Fennimore said, because Ida had been about to say that they were. “Nobody in this county drafted it. A ruin has no amenity. Not diminished amenity. None. You cannot build, repair, alter, or occupy, and the definition of the thing the trust was protecting is a list of the four things that have been made illegal. This estate has been sitting on that contradiction since 1889 like a bird on a wire.”

The reversion had been bought in 2019 for one hundred and forty pounds and a lunch. It had been bought again in 2021 by a holding company belonging to Ivo Standish, and nobody had told him it was the most valuable thing he owned. The sale was advertised for the twelfth of April at the Bell Inn, one lot, reserve one pound, and the reason it could not be anything else was that a scheduled ruin is not a body corporate. It cannot borrow or sue, which is a clause in a finance act of about 1988 that Ida Ruskin found on her telephone in a coffee shop.

The notice went into the post on the second of March. It was four paragraphs long and entirely accurate. The village had not been sold. The village had been conveyed, in satisfaction of a debt of nil, to a person who had bought a piece of paper for a hundred and forty pounds. Royde Pethick read it in the post room at the haulage yard and put it on the pinboard, and at four o’clock he took it down and put it in his cab.

* * *

In April Nell Cragg was asked, by her own office, whether she intended to defend the entry. She laid it out in order for Halloran. The entry was made on the thirtieth of September in consequence of a line in the register of elections. The Act required the office to protect a place. It did not require the office to defend its own handwriting.

“You are the author of the entry,” Halloran said.

“I am.”

“And you apply to have it removed.”

“The entry was made correctly. The removal is correctly available. Both are true at once, and only one is any use to anybody.” If I tell a panel that Longthwaite matters because it is a scheduled ruin, then in nine years there will be a second place claiming the same, and after that the register is a register of places that were frightened into being frightened. That is a rumour with a stamp on it.”

The hearing took two hours on a Tuesday in June. Nell gave her evidence second. “The office has no evidence of interest in the buildings, and none is disputed. It is a consequence of a decision taken by the inhabitants, lawfully, on a clear majority. I am the officer who made the entry, and I cannot support it on the evidence. The Act does not require me to defend a decision. It requires me to protect a place.”

Ida Ruskin, in the second row of the hearing, in a shirt she had bought for a wedding, understood in the plain way a person understands a sum they have been avoiding that the statute had made the valley safe. The man with the reversion in his coat pocket was the only person in Longthwaite with a plan that worked, he was owed money, and she was the last person in the valley to say she did not know what to do next.

The entry came off the register on the eleventh of June, and the reason was written into the register in the same hand as the entry, because in ninety years somebody will want to know. The prohibitions lifted in the same week. The sale completed on the nineteenth, for one pound, and the only bid in the room was the bid of the man who had set the reserve. The first thing Standish did with a valley that could now lawfully be built on was to commission a topographical survey, which is not vandalism. It is a Tuesday.

* * *

He gave them fourteen months, and he gave them the reason without being asked. The lender’s facility had to be drawn or repaid, and it was not his money, and it was not a threat. “I am not going to move anybody,” he said. “I am going to build forty acres of yard, and there are nine hundred people living in houses I am not going to touch. People will take money for houses they are not going to be sad about for years yet, and then they will be sad, and both of those are true at the same time. I have a hardship fund with four and a half million in it. I have already given Sian Pethick eleven thousand of it, and I am telling you now so that you cannot tell me afterwards that I did not.”

The bus went twice a day and would go once, and the market cross was a scheduled monument, and none of it was any use, because the people of Longthwaite were not a party to the conveyance and never had been.

The meeting on the sixth of December was chaired by Bronwen Trefryth, who had been a governor for twenty years. She asked Ida Ruskin, as the last person in the village who had run anything, what the campaign’s advice was, and Ida stood up and said the thing she had not said to anybody in nine months, which was that she did not know.

“I have nothing,” she said. “I have a year of my life and a campaign that got what it wanted, and I have been extremely clear about what I was against, and nobody ever asked me what I was for. I am the last person in this hall who thought the Act would tell us what to do, and I have been wrong about that, and I would rather say it out loud in this room than in a letter. And if anybody wants to be angry, there is a man in Newport with a lorry and a good reason.”

* * *

The file arrived on a Tuesday morning in the second week of January, and it was Nell’s fault entirely. It was the county’s own practice note on scheduled ruins, and on the cover, in ballpoint, it said: You asked me in November what the rules actually were. Here they are. It is not much, and I am sorry. Page four was headed MAINTENANCE AND RUINOUS CONDITION, and the second paragraph said that where a ruin had been maintained in its ruinous condition for ten years, the office should apply for a maintenance grant, should enter it in the register of maintained ruins, and should certify the condition annually. It had a form number, on a printed card in a drawer in the second-floor office.

She read it twice in the school library, and then telephoned Nell Cragg, who said, “I know. I sent it on the fourth. The honest reason is that I have spent five months in a room by myself and I could not make it go away on my own.”

“You are telling us to take our roofs off.”

“The Act requires a ruin to look like a ruin, has required it since 1911, and nobody has ever claimed it. If you claim it, the water is not your problem and neither is the slate, and the indemnity comes with the grant, which is the only reason nobody has ever wanted it.” A pause. “And the school has to go. A ruin may not be a place of assembly. That is the only part of that Act I have ever been glad of.”

The meeting on the twenty-second of January was attended by six hundred and forty people, and the fire authority prohibited assembly in the old hall. The winter was the difficulty, and the answer to it was a portable school in the fair field and a list of named exemptions, read out from a trestle table in the yard in January.

Pethick said, “You want me to take my roof off.”

“I want us to have a say about the shape of our own lives for the first time in two years,” said Ida. “It is a terrible idea. It is the only one on the table that is not a person selling us something.” Pethick voted against, and said so when the vote was called, and voted against again in the second count because Bronwen Trefryth misread the first. Standish came for the last count, and said, “I would advise against it, and I am aware how that sounds. I am advising against it for a planning reason. A lodge at the valley mouth has a perfectly ordinary future, and you are about to give it up for a grant.” He left before the count.

The order was made in April, the money came in June, and the maintenance began in September, and Longthwaite took eleven months to become what it had been obliged to be. Twenty-two houses went open to the weather to the height of a man’s shoulder, their chimneys taken down level with the eaves, their doorways hung with old lintels set square and left unpainted, in the manner of a building that has agreed to stop. Nine hundred and four people did not go anywhere. A woman on Ash Row, whose bathroom had been refused for three years, had a flush and a door that shut, because the Act forbade the building of a thing and said nothing about the water already underneath it. By the tenth winter there were two entries in the register of maintained ruins, and Sowerby signed the annual review in a hand that had got worse since 2023 and had not got worse out of dissatisfaction.

The survey of Longthwaite made in the eleventh year describes a settlement of nine hundred and four people in a valley eleven miles from a ring road: its dwellings standing open to the weather to a mean height of one metre and one hundred millimetres, one dwelling at the north end retaining a roof by election, its owner being in it. There is no scheduled building in the settlement, one scheduled monument, and a lodge at the valley mouth, which is not a ruin and never was. The plate is new. It was cast at Cratterford in the same week as the first twenty-two chimneys came down, and it was bolted to the door of the old school, which is no longer a school, and which is the largest roofless room in the valley, and which will remain so for as long as the Act stands: RUIN — AREA SCHEDULED — MAINTENANCE OF RUINOUS CONDITION REQUIRED — REPAIRS PROHIBITED.
