# The Astronomer's Daughter and the Vanishing Star

![Cover art: The Astronomer's Daughter and the Vanishing Star](../art/advanced/78-the-astronomer-s-daughter-and-the-vanishing-star.webp)

The register of the Meriden Observatory lies open on the desk in the plate room every morning of the working year, and for eleven years the first thing Nura Vance had done on arriving was to find the line for Gn 9-114 and read the magnitude off it, and the first thing she said, before she had taken her coat off, was the number.

“Nine point three one,” she would call down the length of the floor, and somebody would answer with the number from the previous month, and the difference would be a small negative quantity, and that was the morning. She was twenty-nine and she was the most junior instrument-operator on the staff, by appointment, and she had wanted to be in the theory room from the age of nineteen and was not in the theory room.

The star had faded. It was a northern star of the ninth magnitude, catalogued in 1790 by a man with a different instrument in a different century. From the autumn of 1882 to the present it had lost seventeen hundredths of a magnitude, in a line, without a wobble, and the line was the entire subject, and four explanations stood behind it in a row like four people who all had a ticket.

The first was dust. A dark cloud of the kind that dims what lies behind it had drifted in front of Gn 9-114, and the Observatory had measured reddening on that patch of sky since 1870, and there was no reddening, so the first explanation was nearly dead but not dead, because the reddening measures stop at magnitude 9-4 and Gn 9-114 is fainter than that. The second was the star itself: a spot cycle, a blotch of its own surface turning slowly round to face us, on a period nobody can measure inside eleven years.

The third was distance: a parallax baseline set down badly in 1790, the star four hundred light years farther than we think, everything about it correspondingly reduced. The fourth was Ilya Ferrant’s, and the fourth was the beautiful one, and the fourth was wrong.

* * *

Ferrant was thirty-four and had been in the theory room three years and had a way of arriving at an argument that made it look as though it had been waiting for him. He had taken the residuals, the hundredth-of-a-magnitude differences between each month and the straight line drawn through the whole series, and had found a structure in them: a slow undulation of period four years and one tenth and amplitude six hundredths, which was not noise, which he had tested, and which he laid out on the graph paper of the front room for three afternoons while people stood behind his chair. “A straight line in magnitude with a four-year ripple on it is a body being covered,” he said.

“That is a companion, in a long orbit, crossing the disc, eating the light. And the reason we have not seen it is that a companion crossing a faint dwarf at this range is a hundredth of a magnitude, and the plate is a hundredth of a magnitude, and the eclipse is the same size as the plate.”

Nura liked him and did not like the fourth explanation, and she had a professional reason for disliking it that she could state in one sentence and that she stated often. “It is a hypothesis about a body,” she said. “The other three are hypotheses about a measurement.” And Ferrant said, “I am aware of that, and it has never once made a hypothesis weaker,” and put his finger on the ripple, and said that a straight line with nothing on it was a telescope’s suspicion and a ripple was a thing you could publish, and that the department had one telescope’s worth of time to allocate next spring and no way of asking for it.

The Director came down at four most days and was sixty, and had appointed Nura to her post when she was twenty-three out of a list of nine, and had told her in a corridor that he could not put a young woman in the theory room with Haldane Sree and could not put her anywhere else, and that the instrument post was the work and the theory room was the credit. Five years later she had thanked him for it in a note, and had meant it, and had gone on wanting the other thing.

“Your father wrote the first one,” the Director said to her, once, in the first month, in a tone he did not use again. Nura said that she knew. The Director said, “I want you to understand that I do not think that is a disqualification, and I want you to understand that I have watched you check his arithmetic for eleven years and never once check his premise,” and then he went up, because he had a plate waiting, and because he was a decent man who had said the true cruel thing and would not improve on it by saying it twice.

* * *

Bess Ocklund was eighty-three and had washed plates in the basement of the Meriden Observatory for fifty-one years, at the board’s old rate because the board liked her, in a line of sinks under a wooden rack with a smell of hypo that never entirely left her apron. She washed the Gn 9-114 plates, all 2,140 of them, keeping the years in order, which nobody else had ever asked her to do. In March of that year she said to Nura, without looking up, that there was a thing about the star she did not think Nura would want to hear.

“Then say it.”

“I have washed this star for fifty years.” Bess turned a plate in the light of the window, a wet grey oval with its grain standing up. “I am not an astronomer, I will not pretend to be one. But I have held every plate of this star in this room, and stars do not go out the way candles go out. They go down and they come up. They have a temper. This one has been going down at the same rate every time for eleven years, and no thing in the sky has ever done that to me.”

“It is a long series. A straight line can happen.”

“Yes,” Bess said, and put the plate on the rack, and wiped her hands on her apron, which was the end of it. “But not a very straight one. Not a cook’s straight one.”

* * *

Nura had seven weeks of the instrument hands’ round in front of her, because instrument-operators cleaned. The twenty-six-inch required the outer surface of the objective, the finder objective, the four eyepieces, the plate-holder spider, and the field stop, which is the brass ring at the focus that holds the illuminated field to a circle of exactly forty-one arcminutes, and which is held by four screws and has never been adjusted, because the handbook at page 118 says the field stop is fixed and is not to be worked upon except by the makers.

She had the plate holder out on the bench with the stop ring in her two hands when she found the notches. Eleven of them, on the inside of the ring, at one point of the circumference, each one a bright scallop of filed brass a few millimetres across, each one cut in a different year, and each one slightly deeper than the last, and each one cut from the inside, which is not how a man files a stop he is trying to hold still.

She put the ring down and picked it up again and put it down. Then she went to the maintenance book, which records instrument work by month, and read back through eleven years of the twenty-six-inch, and there, in October of 1882: Stop, field, cleaned. And in the same month in every year after it, the same line, in the same hand, eleven times, by an instrument hand whose name she knew and who was not a liar, and who was not a man you asked about a stop every autumn for a decade.

“Cleaned,” she said, to nobody, in an empty room, and then she went and found the works-order book, because a man who files a stop files a stop, and filings are work, and work is ordered and signed, and the works-order book is the oldest book in the building. The 1882 order was her father’s. She knew the hand before she had read four lines of it, because she had learned it off a mantelpiece at the age of seven.

Field stop to be drawn in, three millimetres, to reduce the external illumination, the cell having been re-floated at the works and returning a faint blue flare at the edge. Countersigned, Director. And the note under it in the Director’s hand: Ordered. And after that, ten years of nothing, no work orders at all, and eleven lines in a maintenance book in the wrong tense.

* * *

The Drive Book was on the shelf behind the dome drive, in a different binding, brown, and it was the book Nura had never once been asked to look at in six years. It was the log of movements of the instrument, and the rule at the front, in a hand older than anybody living, was the rule she had been taught in her first week and had not once thought about: that the person who operates an instrument does not keep its work log.

The idea was separation of duties, which is a good idea, and which had been in the Observatory’s standing orders since 1831, and which meant that the hand that turned the wheels was never the hand that wrote down where the wheels had been.

Eleven entries. Each one in October. Each one in the same three hands and a fourth, and the fourth changed in 1887, in 1890, and last year, so that the fourth wheel had been four different junior operators in eleven years, and the entry said nothing, and the junior turned what he was told to turn, on a moonlit night, for a plate nobody wanted.

“Twenty minutes twenty-four seconds of declination, out,” the entry said, in the hand of Haldane Sree. “Plate, eleven minutes. In, twenty minutes twenty-four seconds.” And beneath it, in the same hand, a signed line for a relief, with a name against it, and a name against that one, and a name against that, and Nura read four names down the column and none of them were hers and all of them were twenty-two or twenty-three years old.

She stood in the plate room with the Drive Book open on the bench and worked the arithmetic, because she was an instrument operator and arithmetic was the one thing she had never once doubted. Eleven years at twenty minutes twenty-four seconds, which on the declination screw of a twenty-six-inch at that hour of the night is just over twenty arcminutes, and the field stop has forty-one arcminutes of diameter, and at twenty arcminutes off axis the aperture is vignetting a ninth of the light away, and a ninth of a magnitude is about a fifth, and a fifth is four times the annual fade. The star was not fading.

The star was being photographed outside the middle of the field, by four people, on purpose, in October, for eleven years, and the register’s setting column was written forward by 2.8 seconds of arc each time so that the position would creep across the plate catalogue the way a star creeps, and nobody plots position off a refractor that is known to be well set.

* * *

She went up to the Director’s room at nine and put the Drive Book on his desk open at the 1882 page and said, “You knew.”

“I knew in 1890,” Saye said. “Sree came to me in the spring and told me, and I have been the Director since 1888, and I did not stop it, and I wrote down the reason you are about to object to, and I will read it to you if you like.” He turned a sheet round.

It was four lines in his own hand, dated the fourteenth of April 1890, and it said that the drive was a beacon programme; that a ship had left the Cape in 1854 and had not been heard from since; that Gn 9-114 was the only fixed object in eleven light years that every instrument on the continent could see; and that the programme would move the written position of that star by sixty-two seconds of arc over twenty years, which at the range of the ship is a lane of sky a hundred and eighty astronomical units across, and a ship is five hundred metres long. “That is the case,” the Director said. “The case against is on the other side of the sheet.”

“Every astrometric catalogue on this continent will now disagree with every catalogue before 1890, for four hundred years, and if the ship is not there, or went somewhere else in the first thirty years, then it was a piece of work we did for nothing and a number that is wrong forever. I have two thousand years of positions behind me and I have spent ten of them signing things that put a lie in the first column.”

“You could have told somebody.”

“I could have told the Board, and the Board would have consulted, and the consultation would have taken four years, and the movement is a hundred and eighty astronomical units wide, and it is thirty-eight years into a forty-year flight.” He looked at her. “I want to say something to you and I want to say it once and I do not want you to answer it. You are about to say that I had no authority. I want you to consider, before you say it, that I had no authority, and that I was the only person in the building who had none, and that having none was the only reason I could hold it together for three years.”

* * *

Ferrant was in the front room with the residual graph pinned to the wall when she came in, and he did not turn round, and she told him the whole of it standing up, and when she had finished he asked the first sensible question, which was what the four-year ripple in his residuals was doing if not a companion. She said she did not know yet. He said that the filed stop was the whole of it, and that a bite which grew by one notch a year and gave back a bite a year, on a period of four years, was a plate-holder and a season. He said, “You have told me the mechanism.”

“You have not told me the arithmetic, and I will not throw away a beautiful series because a woman has come in with a filing.” And she said his residual period was the plate-holder being re-floated every four years, and that the works had a card for it. He said, “Show me the card,” and she showed him the card, and he read it a while, and then he said, “I do not believe you. Put that on the record between us, because in about a year I am going to have a child, and I will want to be the sort of man who did not change his mind for a rumour.”

“But I will check it, and I will find you are wrong, because the part I cannot accept is not the filing. It is four people with a wheel.” And he unpinned the graph and rolled it up and gave it to her, and said, “Keep that. If I am wrong you will want to show somebody what I was sure of.”

* * *

Nura Vance did not go to the Board. She told the Director that she would report it, and that her report would be true, and that it would be addressed to him, because a true report is a true report whichever way it is sent. Saye listened to the whole of it and thanked her, and did not try to make her feel small, and when she had finished he said that he was entitled to one more thing, as an officer who had held the office for eleven years and would be relieved of it in the spring. He put his hand flat on the closed Drive Book.

“The question was never whether the star should be moved,” he said. “The ship is out there or it is not, and the marker works or it does not, and nobody has shown me the harm in a number that is slow and patient and true for two hundred years. The question was whether four people should move it in secret, and you have established that they did, and I have not argued otherwise and am not going to.”

“I gave Haldane the legal case in 1890 and I gave Adela the moral one, and I sat in this room for three years making sure the two cases did not meet. They met. You have found that, and it is the only thing I have got wrong, and I am not young enough to be relieved of it, so I will hand it over with the book.”

The dome was cold on the eighteenth of December, cold as the hill itself, with the wind finding the shutter gap and the tube standing at the altitude of a hand’s width above the horizon on its two bearings. The drive was a machine for four people and it was not improved. One wheel governed hour, one governed declination, and two men sat on the fine screws, and a fifth hand worked the plate holder, and Nura took the fourth wheel because there were four wheels and there were three of the men who had used it for eleven years, and the fourth had been a boy of twenty-two every year since 1887, and had never once been told what he was turning.

Haldane Sree showed her where to stand and how to take up the weight of it, and Adela Krimm showed her the reading, and Ivo Oster put the plate in and took it out, and the great tube moved eleven hundred tons of brass and glass by a third of a degree, slowly, with a noise like a rope under load, and stopped when he said stop.

Then, in the light of the plate-room lamp, with the shutter closed and the sky shut off and the object glass at the temperature of the yard, Sree opened the Drive Book on the ledge and wrote the night’s line in his own hand, which is a different hand from every line in the observation register on the desk behind them, and which is the hand of the man who ordered the movement, and beside it he wrote the fourth wheel, and beside that he wrote Vance, and Nura Vance read it, and did not say anything, and put her hand on the brass rim of the wheel and felt it still warm from her own pushing, and the page lay open with eleven years of it above and one more beneath.
