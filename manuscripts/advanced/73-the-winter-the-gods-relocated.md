# The Winter the Gods Relocated

![Cover art: The Winter the Gods Relocated](../art/advanced/73-the-winter-the-gods-relocated.webp)

The census of the southern circuit was a job of four weeks in February, and Ilse Bhattarai had done it four times, and the fifth was the only one that ever mattered. The Bureau of Locations kept a god the way a land office keeps a field: as a set of measurements, taken at intervals, in a form, against a fixed trig point on the Moor, and the warden of a circuit walked the settlements, took the returns, brought them back in a tin box, and entered them in a ledger, and everything anybody knew about a god came out of that ledger.

The Manual of Locations, 1871, was short enough for a coat pocket. Rule four said that a god is a place with powers, and that a place is any area enclosed by a boundary, and that a boundary is a line drawn on a map, and that a line drawn on a map is a legal act. Rule nine said that a god cannot leave its boundary without ceasing to be a god. Rule fourteen said that a congregation is constituted by residence, and that a god cannot lawfully have a congregation it has left.

That rule fourteen had governed four settlements on that circuit alone, being Nether Sallow, Over Winder, Harrow Gill and the township of Sallow Bridge, and between them they held eleven hundred and six souls, and every one of those souls was a member of a congregation, and a congregation requires a god, and the god’s location was fixed at the last census by an entry in Ilse’s ledger that said, in her own hand, THE SEA THAT CALMS, ASPECT OF SALT, BOUNDARY AS PER SHEET FOURTEEN, and sheet fourteen was in a drawer in Annexe C, rolled round a tin, and had not been out since the previous census.

* * *

Temma Ruddock was nine years old and had been in the returns of Nether Sallow since the day she was born, because her grandmother filled the form, and her grandmother filled every form, and one of the forms was the birth schedule, and the birth schedule requires the name, the date, the settlement, and the god, and the grandmother had put the god in the box marked GOD because the box asked, and she was not a fool.

“You are not on the god box,” Ilse said, on the second morning, in a schoolroom with a bad stove. “You are on the soul box. Name, date, settlement, and then the god, which is a separate line and a separate signature.”

“They’re the same line.”

“They are two lines and two signatures, and one of them has a person on it and one of them has a god on it, and I cannot enter a god on a line that is signed by a person.” Ilse turned the form round. “Now. What is the god of Nether Sallow?”

“A thing that comes up the road,” Temma said.

“That is not a god. That is a description of a visit. What work does your god do here?”

“It can’t do anything. It can only be the place where it is.” Temma said it without embarrassment, the way you state the height of a wall. “It stands in the churchyard and it’s the shape of the sea, and if you say what it’s for, it stops being that and starts being the thing you said.”

Ilse put down her pen. The Manual had four hundred pages on the powers of gods and eleven on their persons, and the eleventh was the oldest and the shortest and had been written by a warden in 1890 who had clearly been frightened of his own handwriting. Never describe a god to its face. A description is an instruction, and a god will do what it is told, and there is no appeal, and the reason is section twelve, and section twelve is a name.

“Then how do you know it’s a god?” Ilse said.

“Because I can see it,” said Temma, “and you can’t, and your book says you can only see it if you are inside it, and I have never once been inside it, because I have never been on the god line.”

* * *

On the Thursday of the third week the circuit meeting at Nether Sallow had four hundred and eleven people in it, the whole of the living parish over twelve, and it had been called to hear the warden read her returns, which took eleven minutes, and then took the rest of the day. It was Ilse’s habit to take questions, and the first came from a man called Ferris, who wanted to know why the schedule of the tithe had changed, and she read him the amendment, and he wrote it down, and then a woman at the back said that the leaves of the sycamore by the churchyard wall had gone grey on the north side, the side that faced the sea, and this had not happened before.

Ilse asked whether anyone else had seen it. Thirty-one hands went up. The leaves of eleven trees on the north side of eleven walls, in one morning, in one hour.

“That is the aspect showing,” said a man at the front, with the air of a man who had been to a lecture. “It does that. The aspect shows when the congregation is unsettled.”

“All right,” Ilse said. “I will note that the congregation is unsettled.”

“You’ll note it in the ledger.”

“I’ll note it in the ledger, and the ledger goes to Annexe C, and Annexe C will compare it with last quarter, and if it is a sign I will be told.” She looked at her page and then at the girl, who was at the front on a chair, because the chair was for her, because the grandmother had arrived an hour late and been given the chair for having come to the meetings since 1951. “And while we have the whole parish in the room, is there anyone here who can tell me what a god is?”

The room laughed, and then stopped, because the question had been asked in the tone of a woman who had asked a question she intended to have answered.

Nobody answered for a while. The stove ticked. And then Temma Ruddock said that a god was a description that enough people agreed on, and that it was kept in a drawer in Annexe C, and that the drawer was the god, and that when people stopped agreeing it went on being a description, and that this was not the same as going away, and that everyone in the room was currently arguing about whether it was true, and that a god being argued about could not be a god, because a god could not be indoors arguing.

The nine-year-old is the one who did it, in the sense that she told the truth at a village meeting on the Thursday of the third week, about what a god is, and what a congregation is, and about a census, and four hundred people believed her, because she was nine and it was plain and it was what they had all been half knowing since Monday, and something that had never once in nine thousand years been told what it was heard itself described, and left.

There was no sound. What there was, afterwards, was eleven bells, and the tide-mark on the north wall of the schoolroom went out like a light being turned down, and four hundred people saw it go out, and the girl saw eleven things go out of the room and go north, at the speed of a person walking.

Then the wire brought the returns.

* * *

The Comptroller was called Anselm Deane and had held the post for nineteen years, and he was at the central office by the following morning with the returns of every circuit on the desk, timed and logged, and the log showed that all of them had come in between 14:11 and 14:14, and every one of them was displaced by one hundred miles due north, and every one was signed by a warden who had written, in eleven identical hands, no change observed.

“The simultaneous returns are the proof,” he said. “Nobody moved them. If a man had moved them he would have had to move four thousand, and a man who could do that would not have written no change observed eleven times in three minutes.”

“What do I do?”

“You do the census.” He turned the ledger round so that she could see the column of figures, and the column was in his own writing, done overnight, in pencil, correctly. “Rule nine: a god cannot leave its boundary without ceasing to be a god. Every god left its boundary. Therefore, as of 14:11 on Thursday, there are no gods in the southern, western, eastern and northern circuits, and there are four thousand and six settlements standing inside a boundary with nothing in it.”

“Nine hundred and eleven in the four circuits I have counted.”

“Nine hundred and eleven in the four circuits you have counted,” Deane said, “and eleven hundred and nine across the country, and the count is not the problem. Sit down, Warden, and hear the problem.”

Rule fourteen said that a congregation is constituted by residence, and that a god cannot lawfully have a congregation it has left, and it followed, without any argument at all, that a settlement inside a departed god’s boundary was a congregation without a god, and that in a congregation without a god no marriage could be effectuated, and no burial permitted, and no will proved, and no tithes apportioned, and no boundary drawn, because drawing a boundary is the act of a god’s presence and there was no presence.

“Nine hundred settlements,” Deane said, “in which nothing may be signed, nothing may be buried, and nothing may be measured, and the only exit from the condition is to draw a new boundary around each settlement, and each new boundary requires a god, and there is no god inside any of the nine hundred, and the Manual has no procedure for producing one.” He put his hands flat on the ledger. “You are to continue the census. I am aware of how that sounds. The census is the only instrument in this country that can make a boundary legal, and if you stop, the void becomes permanent, and if it becomes permanent, then by 1871’s rules these people are not in a congregation at all, and a person not in a congregation has no god, and no burial rights, and no claim on anybody.”

* * *

The god of the southern sea was at Mere Sallow in the third week of February, and the lake was four miles north of the village, and the god had been a hundred miles inland for four months and had not once been asked to do anything.

It was sitting on the north shore with the water behind it, in the shape it had always used, which was a standing wave about nine feet high that did not break and did not move, and Ilse had walked four miles up the frozen road with a sled of forms because the lake had no track and no name on any sheet before February, and it turned to look at her, and said it was glad of the visit and asked her to sit down, and then asked whether it was supposed to be there.

“You are at Mere Sallow,” said Ilse. “The boundary was drawn on the ninth. The schedule says so, and the return says so, and the return is signed by a warden who was not me and who was, I am glad to say, correct.”

“I do not object to the boundary,” said the god. “I want to know what I am for.” And it told her, patiently, and in order, which was the first thing about gods that Ilse had not had explained to her in thirty-eight years. It calmed the sea. It held the salt where the salt should be. It took the drowned, which was a function and not a punishment, and the sea was two hundred miles south of here, and so were the people on it, and it could still do all of it, and had been doing all of it four times a day since the ninth of November, and the Manual said it had no power outside its boundary, and so every one of those four times had been done by a god that was not lawfully there.

“Then stop,” said Ilse.

“I cannot stop,” said the god. “I was described as the thing that calms, and there is a great deal of shipping on that coast, and I do not have anything to describe myself as instead.” It turned the flat white front of itself towards her. “What should I do? You are the warden. You have the Manual. Tell me what to do.”

“The Manual says you do nothing,” Ilse said. “That is the answer, and it is the whole of the answer, and I am sorry it is such a poor one.”

“I have read the Manual,” said the god. “I read it in the first week, when the warden’s man left a copy on the reeds, and I have read all four hundred pages, and I have read the eleven pages about persons twice, and I have had nothing to do since but read, and I would like to ask you a question, and then I will let you work.”

“Ask it.”

“Is it true,” said the god of the lake, “that a god cannot be indoors?”

* * *

The method was the thing Ilse had been carrying round the circuit since November, and it did not work, and she had carried it round eleven times, and it worked on nobody. To draw a boundary you must attest it, and the Manual provides one instrument for attestation, which is the testimony of a person living inside the boundary, given to a warden and entered in the return. Every one of the nine hundred settlements was inside a boundary from which the god had departed. The settlements were still standing and their inhabitants still resident, and a resident inside a void is a resident inside nothing, and the 1871 draughtsmen had assumed that every person on a schedule was inside something.

“Testimony from inside a void is void,” said Deane, on the last day of January, over the telephone, from four hundred miles away and in the voice of a man reading a decision. “I have looked for it for a month. I have had eleven wardens look for it. There is no such procedure and no form, and if you invent one the Comptroller’s office will strike it out, and if I strike out one invented provision I shall have to strike out the lot, and I am not going to be the man who put a second thing wrong with this country.”

“Then I will go and stand in a village and ask a person,” said Ilse, “and you will tell me that what they say is not evidence.”

“I will tell you that it is not admissible evidence,” said Deane. “Those are different sentences, and I have spent nineteen years learning the difference, and I have never once wished to.”

She had a list. She had made it out of the returns herself, in the margins of the March ledger, in pencil, and it was a list of every person in the four circuits whose name was not on any schedule, and the list had one name on it, and the name was in a grandmother’s hand, on a line that said GOD, and the grandmother had written it because the box asked.

* * *

There were one thousand and nine settlements in the four circuits, and the name was Temma Ruddock, of Nether Sallow, aged nine, born in the interval between the returns of two successive Febembers, entered by her grandmother in the column for gods because the column for gods was the one on the form, and entered nowhere else at all, and therefore not a person in the record, and therefore not inside any boundary, and therefore the only lawful witness in England.

“I am not going to ask her to do it nine hundred times,” Ilse said.

“Then do it once,” said Deane.

“That is the same thing with a smaller number on it.”

“Warden Bhattarai,” said the Comptroller, “you are going to ask a child of nine to attest nine hundred boundaries in four months, in the middle of the worst winter since 1871, and every god in the country will hear the description of itself, and section twelve is the reason we do it this way, and I have read section twelve more times than you have, and it says, and I will give you the words, it says that a god which has been correctly described cannot be asked to do anything it has not already been described as doing.” He let that sit. “And if it cannot be asked to do anything, then it cannot do harm, and I have been trying for four months to find any other instrument and there is not one, and I have been wrong about this once and I have never been wrong about it twice.”

* * *

The work took the rest of the winter. The returns were timed and logged, and the log became the record, and the record became the precedent, and the precedent was that a person in the record may attest a boundary from outside it, which is a small door in a large wall, and it was built in four months by a girl of nine who did not entirely understand what she was doing, and who was given a chair, a pencil, and a sheet per settlement, and who said on the first morning that the drawing was the easy part, because a place is a place, and the hard part was the aspect, because the aspect was what the thing was for.

She did it properly. Eleven hundred and nine boundaries, drawn to a scale of one to the fifty thousand, each one signed by a person in the record who lived outside it, each one entered in a return, each one lodged in Annexe C, and the gods in them legally resident, with their aspects stated and their congregations constituted, so that a marriage in Sallow Bridge in the last week of February was effectuated before a warden with a schedule and a god present in the room, which had not been possible since the ninth of November.

On the last afternoon the roof of the census hall at Sallow Bridge came in under the wet snow, and it came in onto four hundred sheets of schedule and eleven hundred sheets of return and one map, and the map was four yards by three and it was on a board and the board was propped against the north wall, and the wall held.

* * *

The hall had no roof after that and the walls stood and the snow came in through the gap where the trusses had been, and the map was still on the wall, because the map was the one thing in the building nobody had thought to move, and there were nine hundred and eleven new boundaries on it in Temma Ruddock’s pencil, and every one of them was correct.

Ilse sat down on a desk that was still a desk. Beside her, in the shape it had always used, which was a standing wave about nine feet high that did not break, the god of Mere Sallow settled against the wall and looked at nine hundred and eleven boundaries in a child’s handwriting and said nothing for some time.

“You wanted to ask me a question,” Ilse said.

“I asked you one in February,” said the god, “and you gave me an answer that took four hours, and I have thought about it every day since, and I have a better one.” The flat white front of it turned towards the map. “If a god is a description that enough people agree on, and I am the description that a coast agrees on, then the coast agrees on me because of where I am. If I am not where I am, the coast does not agree on me, and there is nothing in me at all, and what I have been calling myself, since November, is a habit.”

“Yes,” said Ilse. “That is more or less it, except that the habit has a boundary, and the boundary is what is holding you up, and what is holding you up at this moment is a line drawn in pencil on a board in a shed, by a girl who thinks she is talking about a river.”

“I would like to be told,” said the god, “what a congregation is. Properly. I have read the definition and I have read the eleven pages and I do not believe either of them, and I have been at this lake for four months with nothing to do, and I have thought about nothing else.”

“A congregation is a settlement inside a boundary,” said Ilse, “and it is constituted by residence, and that is the whole of the definition, and it is true, and it is also the least interesting thing anybody has ever said to you, and I am not going to stop there, because you asked properly, and because you are a legal person and I am not permitted to be inexact with a legal person, and because in four hundred years nobody is going to read this file and I am going to have to say it now.” She turned on the map, so that the line of the northern lakes was in front of both of them. “The reason your definition is unsatisfying is that it is a description written by people who were frightened, in 1871, about a god that heard itself described, and everything since has been an attempt to make sure that nobody ever has to say what you are to your face.”
