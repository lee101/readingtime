# The Girl Who Argued About the Parallax

![Cover art: The Girl Who Argued About the Parallax](../art/advanced/180-the-girl-who-argued-about-the-parallax.webp)

Allegheny Observatory stands on a hill above Pittsburgh, and in February the hill is grey and the inside of the dome is colder than the outside of it. Nell Arkwright worked on the floor of that dome, at a measuring engine, on glass plates that other men had exposed months earlier.

She was twenty-nine. Her hair was dark and pinned up with a steel pin. She wore a scarlet wool coat with the sleeves pushed back past her wrists, because the coat was for the stairs and the sleeves were for the engine.

A computer was not an astronomer. A computer was a person who did arithmetic for an astronomer, at an hourly wage, on a schedule that let the astronomer sleep. The plates came up out of the bath and went to her desk. On her desk was the engine, and on the engine was a carrier that would hold one plate at a time under two crosshairs, and each crosshair could be moved a thousandth of an arcsecond at a time.

Her winter's work was one field in Boötes, forty degrees off the brightest star in the sky, containing the fastest star in the sky.

* * *

The star was three years old as a number. Barnard had put it in the sky in 1916, comparing a plate exposed at Lick in 1894 with one exposed at Yerkes in 1916, and finding that in twenty-two years it had walked further than a thousand full moons laid side by side.

It was a red dwarf, ninth and a half magnitude, and it moved ten and a third arcseconds a year, very nearly due north. It was the only star a person could watch walk across a background of other stars with their own eyes, from a city, on a night with no moon. Its catalogue number was BD plus four degrees thirty-five sixty-one, out of the Bonn Durchmusterung, printed in 1875 and used as a fixed point by everybody in that room.

How far away it was, was the argument. Nothing moves that fast unless it is close. Close to what, is not a thing you can look at. It is a thing you have to measure. The measurement is called the parallax, and it comes out in fractions of an arcsecond, and for this star the whole of it is about five hundred and forty-seven thousandths of an arcsecond.

* * *

Before the number there was the glass.

Each plate was a square of plate glass smaller than a tea tray, with a film of silver salt dried onto one face. Light had fallen on that face for forty minutes in the dome, and had knocked the pieces of silver apart in a pattern that a chemist in a cellar had then developed into something you could put under a microscope.

What she looked at were dots, a hundred and forty of them in the field, most so faint that you found them by putting your eye to the microscope and sliding the plate until the dot came up out of the black. Then the crosshair went across it, the drum went round, and she wrote the number down. Every number on that sheet was an estimate, and by the eleventh plate she was tired, so she put the worst in a different ink and came back to them at four in the morning.

Glass plate does one more thing, which is to shrink. It shrinks over decades, and when it shrinks everything on it comes closer together, and a plate that has shrunk a tenth of a per cent will report every distance a tenth of a per cent small.

She checked. There was a ruled grid printed on the 1917 film in the same batch, it agreed with the plate's own known size to a part in three thousand, and that is nothing at all on a bow of half an arcsecond. She wrote it in the margin with a tick against it and never had to think about it again.

* * *

Here is how the parallax is got.

The Earth goes round the Sun once a year. A star is so far away that from one end of that orbit and the other, it looks like the same place. Almost the same place. It is not the same place. It is displaced a little, and the direction of the displacement depends on where in the year you look from.

How much displaced depends on where the star sits relative to the Sun. The displacement is largest when the star is a quarter of the way round the sky from the Sun. It is exactly nothing twice a year, once behind the Sun and once on the far side, because then the Earth's motion is straight along the line of sight and a motion along the line of sight does not change the angle.

At the latitude of Pittsburgh, Barnard's star is in the quarter position in late June, in the evening, and again in late December, in the morning. By the first week of March it is three quarters of the way round, and in the first week of April it is on the far side of the Sun, where its parallax is nothing whatever.

That is not a subtlety in the theory. It is the reason the archive has three folders and not one. The June and December plates are good plates. The March plates are not bad plates. They are plates where the answer costs four times as much to get wrong.

The parallax sits at the flat top of its yearly curve in June and December, and the top of a curve is flat, so a minute of clock error in June is worth almost nothing. By the first week of March the star is well round from the flat top and the curve is on the way down, and the same minute of clock error is worth four times as much. A March plate is worth taking. It is only worth taking carefully.

That is the whole reason the observatory had a rule about June and December. The rule was old and nobody had written down why. What it never covered was the third folder, because a man working alone takes whatever the sky gives him at eight o'clock on a Tuesday, and the sky does not consult a rule.

* * *

The box had eleven envelopes in it. Eight were June and December. Three were March.

The work was this. In one year the star walks ten and a third arcseconds. Its parallax is five hundred and forty-seven thousandths of an arcsecond. The entire answer is one part in nineteen of a single year's travel. If you take two positions a year apart and draw the straight line between them, you have not measured the parallax. You have measured the walk.

The parallax is the bow in the path. Half an arcsecond of bow, up in one season and down in the next, sitting on top of twenty arcseconds of travel, and it has to be pulled out of the walk the way you pull a curve out of three points.

She did not draw anything. She made numbers and gave them to a man to add up, which is what the wage was for.

* * *

The standing value in the ledger for that field was five hundred and thirty-one thousandths, with an error of seven. It had been put there the previous winter by a man who is no longer at Allegheny, and nobody had touched it since.

Nell's numbers, off the eleven envelopes, came to five hundred and forty-nine, with an error of nine.

She read them twice, checked the arithmetic on the back of a catalogue page, which she had to rub out because the ink did not lift, and did it again on the flyleaf of a pocket book.

The two values did not agree.

* * *

She took it to Anson Pike, who ran the astrometry and who had been doing it since before she finished school.

Pike looked at the two numbers for a while. Then he said that a parallax was not got by taking her word for a comparison star, and that her comparison star was the eighth magnitude one at the north-west corner of the plate, and that the catalogue put its parallax at seventy-six thousandths.

Nell said yes. She said that was exactly the number she had used.

Pike said, then, that if the catalogue is wrong about the comparison star, her answer is wrong, and there is no way on that floor to find out whether the catalogue is wrong about the comparison star, because every parallax anybody has ever measured is a difference between one star and another, and the smallest parallax you can measure is the difference between the two smallest, and somewhere at the end of that chain there is a row of zeros and no telescope.

He said it without unkindness. He said it the way a man says a thing he has said before.

* * *

She went back down to the plate room with the envelopes in a cardboard box tied with string, and she read them again, not the numbers this time but the handwriting.

On the June and December envelopes the mid-exposure time was in the observers' hand, in pencil, always to the minute. The March envelopes had times too, but in a different hand, upright and small, and in purple ink where all the other ink on the box was black.

The March times were not the times the shutter was opened. They were the times the plate went into the carrier, and the difference between the two was however long it took a man to walk back round a dome in March with the dark slide in his hands.

She went through the dome log and matched the entries. Three March plates. One of them, the fifth of March, had hung at eleven twenty-one in the writing on the envelope and had actually been open from eleven forty-nine to twelve and one.

* * *

That was twenty-eight minutes out, which is seven degrees of sky.

Seven degrees costs nothing where the curve is flat and a great deal where it is not. On the fifth of March Barnard's star was three quarters of the way round from the Sun, seven degrees is a fifth of the whole parallax factor, and the answer moves by about twenty thousandths of an arcsecond.

She redid the solution with the corrected time. It came out five hundred and twenty-nine, with an error of eight.

Which was two thousandths away from the ledger.

That was the joke of the winter. She had gone looking for the reason the ledger was wrong and had found a plate somebody had carried to the carrier at the wrong time on a Thursday in March 1918. When she fixed it, the ledger was right.

* * *

The meeting was on a Thursday and it was not so much a meeting as a reading.

Pike put two numbers on the slate and did not say which was whose, because everybody in that room had been up in the dome that winter and knew which envelope came off which desk. He asked for the objection to the new value, and she gave him the March envelope, and the dome log page, and twenty-eight minutes.

He read the log page twice. Then he said the error was real, that the observer had been careless in March 1918, that the observer was in Cleveland now, and that the question would have to be put to him by letter, and the letter could not go before the spring mail.

The new value therefore rested, he said, on a log page in a room, against a value six seasons of this floor had built up in the ordinary way. He did not say she was wrong. He said that a number you cannot swear to is not yet a number.

Nell said the log page was the observatory's, that it had hung in the dome room since 1914, and that it was in the same hand as every other page in it.

Pike said he knew that.

It took three weeks for the argument to be worth nothing at all.

The five hundred and thirty-one had been printed in the annual report in the spring of 1918, and the report had gone to eleven libraries and two societies, and the value was also on a card in a card index in Boston that people sent to each other by post. You do not correct a card index. You issue a new card saying the old one was wrong, and then somebody has to go and find everyone who ever sent for the old one.

Pike could do that. He had done it once, in 1916, over a value of four thousandths, and it had cost him a whole autumn and one unhappy letter.

There was the other half of it. If the ledger was wrong then every distance in that catalogue was wrong with it, because they had all been built the same way, in the same building, by the same handful of men, hanging off the same row of zeros. Eight thousandths on one star is nothing. Eight thousandths across the catalogue is a change in the size of the sky, and there is no way to ask anybody who used the old number.

Then he gave her the plate-washing rota for the spring. Half past four in the afternoon, under running water, at sixty degrees, three hours a night, eleven nights.

The water in that building came out of the tap cold enough in February to take the skin off the inside of your wrist, and at sixty degrees it was hot enough to be a mistake on purpose. The plates went in on wire trays, forty to a tray, and came up one at a time to be tipped, drained and set on the rack with the coated face up, and the room was full of steam and you could not see the rack at the far end.

She did the algebra in her head, which was the hard part, because numbers do not slow down when your hands go numb. By the fourth night she had caught herself twice putting a term in with the wrong sign. Both times she caught it because she had written the running totals in pencil on the sleeve of the scarlet coat and had to take the sleeve off to read them, which nobody in that room had ever done before her and everybody in that room was doing by the eighth night.

* * *

She was back at the engine in the autumn, on the same field, on the same plates, because the field had not finished with anybody.

The standing value was five hundred and thirty-one. Her corrected value was five hundred and twenty-nine. She still did not believe it, but she had stopped arguing about it, which is a different thing and not much better.

So she did the thing nobody had asked her to do. She measured the comparison star.

The comparison star was eighth magnitude and it was in the plate, and she measured it exactly as carefully as she had measured Barnard's star, on the same plates, on the same engine, under the same crosshairs, at the same hour on the same nights. What she could not do was get its parallax out. She could only get how far the two of them moved apart, and that is a difference of two parallaxes and not either of them.

To measure the difference of two parallaxes you need a second comparison star. The field had one, ninth magnitude, two inches to the south-east. The catalogue gave that one forty-four thousandths and the eighth magnitude one seventy-six, so the difference should be thirty-two.

She ran the same bow on that pair. She got twenty-four.

She sat with that a while.

Everything in the ledger was a difference. Barnard's parallax was a number minus seventy-six, and the seventy-six was somebody's difference between that star and another, and that other star had a number of its own, and if she had measured that one too she would have found a third number, and it would have been a difference as well, and there would have been a fourth.

There was no end to it and there was no first term. The floor she was sitting on was a difference from something she could not name.

* * *

Thirty-two against twenty-four is eight thousandths of an arcsecond, which is less than the width of the line the crosshair draws on the plate, and which is one and a half parts in a thousand of the distance to the fastest star in the sky.

It was also, if it was an error, an error that had been sitting in that catalogue since before she started at Allegheny. Twenty-four was measured with this engine. Seventy-six was not. Seventy-six was made of two earlier measurements by two earlier men, one of whom had died in 1911 and the other of whom had left in 1914.

She could not find out which side of the eight thousandths was the wrong side. The catalogue's seventy-six had been made on that floor, on that telescope, from plates measured on that engine by a man who had left in 1914. Her twenty-four had been made on that floor, on that telescope, on that engine, by her. Both had come out of the same room, and there was no instrument in the building that could look at that field and say which of them to believe.

* * *

On the last night before the winter set in she put the plate of June 1917 into the carrier, and then the plate of June 1918, one after the other, and put her eye to the microscope.

Barnard's star had gone where it had gone. She wrote down the two positions and subtracted, and got the difference of the two parallaxes, which was solid, which was the best thing she made all winter.

Then she added the catalogue's seventy-six to it and got five hundred and twenty-nine, and she wrote that in the column headed Parallax, in the units the column was headed in.

Afterwards she took the pocket book out of the coat pocket and opened it at the flyleaf, and wrote 537 there instead, which is what you get if the catalogue's seventy-six ought to have been eighty-four, and which nobody in that building was in a position to find out.

Neither number was right. One of them was eight thousandths of an arcsecond out, she knew the size of that exactly, and she had no way of telling which direction it went, and the column she was working in did not have anywhere to put that either.
