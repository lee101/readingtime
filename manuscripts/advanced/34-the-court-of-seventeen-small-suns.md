# The Court of Seventeen Small Suns

![Cover art: The Court of Seventeen Small Suns](../art/advanced/34-the-court-of-seventeen-small-suns.webp)

The summons reached Tamsin Vale as a black sunrise folded inside an envelope.

She was thirty-two, a solar cartographer with ink beneath both thumbnails and a burn scar along her right palm from an instrument that had once mistaken her hand for a shadow. She wore a long coat of bleached blue wool, fastened at the throat by a silver sun, and carried a folding brass astrolabe in a leather case. The summons said only this:

COME TO THE PALACE OF SEVENTEEN SMALL SUNS. BRING THE MAP THAT SHOWS WHAT IS MISSING.


The palace came into view above the salt flats, a ring of white stone and colored glass orbiting a central black spire. Seventeen small suns moved around it in slow, unequal arcs. Some burned gold. Some were blue enough to cast shadows at noon. One, at the far edge, was no larger than a coin and flickered as if someone were repeatedly closing its eyelid.

A brass voice spoke from the palace gate.

“Cartographer Vale, you are late by one morning.”

“I traveled by daylight.”

“Daylight is a local courtesy. The palace keeps the official kind.”

Beyond the gate waited seventeen heirs in robes marked with their sun's color. They stood in a loose circle around a table of white stone. At the center sat Regent Celestine Vey, a tall woman in a scarlet coat and a crown made from seventeen narrow rays. She wore no jewels. The only ornament on her coat was a brass key hanging over her heart.

Tamsin set down her astrolabe case. “Which sun is missing?”

Celestine raised one hand toward the smallest, flickering light.

“The Ninth,” she said. “Its heir is Nial Vey.”


“He carries a living day,” Celestine said. “So do the other sixteen. At dawn, each heir releases the day they carry into the corresponding sun. The sun gives the day to the palace. The palace turns, and a new day enters the calendar.”

“And the heir?”

“Remains.”

Tamsin studied the black spire. “That sounds like a punishment described as a duty.”

“It is the oldest compact.” Celestine spoke without heat. “A day cannot be owned. A day can be witnessed, carried, given, and remembered. The heir is not its master. The heir is its gate.”

Tamsin opened her case and unfolded the astrolabe. Its brass rings cast a little shadow across the table. “Then why is the Ninth dark?”

“Because its day is gone.”

“Gone where?”

Celestine looked at the other heirs. “The calendar is missing ten years.”

The words moved through the circle. Nial lowered his eyes.


“Exactly.” Celestine’s fingers tightened around the key. “The Ninth has drifted beyond its permitted arc.”

Nial spoke for the first time. “The court says the drift began ten years ago.”

“And the court is?” Tamsin asked.

Nial looked around the circle. “Accurate.”

“Most courts are not.”

“The first court was,” he said. “The court that signed the Calendar Severance.”



“The missing year is not gone,” she said. “It is somewhere else in the orbit.”


Celestine stepped forward. “The Calendar Severance removed Year Eight Hundred and Seven. Ten years of days were cut from the public calendar and stored in the Crown so the suns could hold their lawful arcs.”

“You cut ten years away to stop the suns from falling.”

“We cut one year,” Celestine said. “The Crown multiplied it into a decade of stored force.”

Tamsin pointed to the ninth hole. “The hole is still there.”

“The Crown is a promise, not a vault. Each year, one heir gave a day to keep the remaining suns aligned. The Ninth received nothing, because its heir’s day was used first.”

Nial’s hand dropped from his chest. “I gave the first day.”

“You were fourteen.” Celestine’s voice softened. “You did not know.”

“I remember it.”

“Year Eight Hundred and Seven ended with the Ninefold Dark,” Nial continued. “The court cut the year before dawn. My father told me to hold the day until the palace was safe, then entered the spire. He did not return. I have carried the first safe day for ten years.”

Tamsin looked at the darkening sun. “The day after the severance?”

“The day after. The Ninth has been waiting for a calendar to receive it.”

Celestine lowered her head. “The court proposes to cut ten years from the future. The new absence will match the old one. The orbit will close.”

“It will close because you have removed another piece of the sky,” Tamsin said. “You will not restore the Ninth sun. You will make ten more years disappear, and the remaining suns will spend themselves trying to follow.”

“Is there another way?” asked the heir in green.

“Yes. But it will not be convenient.”

* * *

The palace’s Hall of Orbits was beneath the spire, a round chamber lined with seventeen windows. Each window looked onto one sun. The Ninth window showed a gray sky with a small orange ember at its center.


“The laws of the court are precise,” Tamsin said. “One heir carries one day. One day may be given only once in a calendar cycle. No day may be taken from another heir. A sun cannot accept a day that has already been given to it.”

“Then the Ninth is lost,” said an heir in a red robe. “It has waited ten years.”

“No. The Ninth has been given a day.” Tamsin tapped the gray window. “It has not been given a date.”

Celestine stepped closer. “Explain.”

Tamsin picked up a black thread and placed it across the chart.

“A living day is a promise that time can happen. Nial’s day is real, but the Crown took the date that made it visible. The sun knows the day exists; the calendar refuses to know where it belongs.”

“Then restore the date,” Nial said.

“Restoring it would force every debt, death, and promise made during the Ninefold Dark into the present. We cannot invent a past to satisfy a light.”

“So the Ninth remains dark,” Celestine said.

“The Ninth receives the safe day. The lost years remain outside the public calendar.”

“That is not law,” said the red-robed heir.

“It is older than the law you wrote,” Tamsin replied. “A day may be given without a date when heir and sun witness it.”

Nial walked to the Ninth window. The orange ember brightened, then sank.

“My father is inside the spire,” he said. “If I give the safe day, will it release him?”

“No,” Celestine said. “His day was lost with the severed year.”

“Then I kept the wrong thing.”

“You kept what kept the court from falling.”

A crack crossed the Ninth sun’s face.

* * *

At midnight, the Regent called for votes. Seventeen colored cushions ringed the Hall of Orbits; the Tenth was empty, its occupant gone to the lower vaults.

Celestine stood before the throne. “The motion removes the next ten years from the calendar. Every heir who votes yes gives one day of consent. No heir loses it; the day returns when the orbit closes.”

Tamsin stood beside the chart. “That is not consent. It is a contract written in a language only the Crown speaks.”

“It is the oldest emergency compact.”

“It is an emergency compact for a court that has forgotten the emergency.”

The First Heir, an old man in copper, raised his hand. “If the Ninth falls, the others will follow.”

“Not if the Ninth is anchored correctly,” Tamsin said. “The orbit is a chain with seventeen links. Each sun is held by the day its heir carries. The Ninth is not asking for ten years; it is asking for the right to begin again.”

Celestine’s fingers closed around the key. “A date cannot be made without a witness.”

“Then let every heir witness one instant,” Tamsin said. “Each heir gives one day to the next sun in sequence, ending with the Ninth. No sun gives to itself. No day is taken or counted twice.”

Nial looked up. “The chain would be seventeen suns wide.”

“For one dawn.”

“And after that?”

“The days return to their heirs at the next sunrise.”

“Why not do it every dawn?” the green-robed heir asked.

“Because the court would become one body,” Tamsin said. “One chain can restore a broken link, not become the ordinary shape of the sky.”

The old First Heir took the first cushion. Nial approached the last. Celestine remained at the throne. “If the chain fails, the first sun will darken.”

“It will not,” Tamsin said. “Unless someone breaks the order.”

Twelve hands rose for the chain. Four abstained. Celestine’s did not move.

“The motion passes,” the First Heir said.

Celestine removed the key. “The chain may be attempted.”

* * *

The court stood in the Hall of Orbits with one foot on each of seventeen colored tiles. The tiles were narrow, brass-edged, and warm beneath their shoes. At the center, the Ninth sun’s window opened onto a sky that no longer contained a sun. Only a small orange mark trembled there, waiting.

Tamsin stood beside Nial. She held the astrolabe open. Its rings no longer pointed outward. They turned around the black thread, each ring finding the next.

“Begin with the First,” she said.

The old heir placed his palm on the first tile. The First Sun flared white. He drew a breath and gave the day he carried into the light. The day was not visible. It entered as heat, as a widening of the shadows, as a note too low for human ears.

The Second heir stepped forward. The First Sun dimmed by one shade. The Second brightened.

Sun to heir. Heir to sun. Heat to heat. Day to day.

The red-robed heir gave his day to the Third. The green-robed heir gave hers to the Fourth. One by one, the court spoke the date they carried, even when the date was inconvenient. A birth. A flood. A failed harvest. A door locked from the outside. A treaty signed in a language no one present could read.

The chain crossed the room.

At the Tenth tile, the missing heir returned dust-covered, carrying the Crown Clock’s hidden plate.

“It was under the throne,” he said. “The year opened itself.”

Tamsin saw ten years of tiny marks pressed into the metal, circles crossed by lines. The days had continued to happen while the court called them impossible. The Tenth heir gave his day onward.

By the seventeenth tile, every sun but the Ninth leaned toward the others, forming a bridge of gold through the gray.

Nial stood before the last cushion.

“Give the first safe day,” Tamsin said.

Nial closed his eyes. For ten years he had kept the day beneath his heart, waiting for room in the calendar. He opened his hands.

The safe day rose as a white thread and entered the Ninth window. The orange mark flared. The crack widened, and everyone felt a lock turn in a distant room.

The lost year was not restored. No history became less painful. The first safe day found a place in the present, held by the witnesses of the living.

The Ninth sun opened. Nial fell to one knee, looking younger by ten years and older by a century.

Celestine Vey watched him. “The court’s motion is fulfilled.”

“No,” Tamsin said. “The motion was a promise made with the wrong unit.”

The Regent turned. “The Ninth sun is restored.”

“A single day anchored the chain. The future remains untouched. The court has no authority to remove it.”

“The court has always had that authority.”

“Law is not a habit. You may amend the emergency compact, but you may not call every disappearance necessary.”

The old First Heir looked toward the Ninth window. “What amendment?”

Tamsin pointed to the plate in the Tenth cushion. “Record the missing year as a wound, not a blank. Let the people who lived through it speak without restoring the false order. Let the Crown hold no day without a named witness. Let every heir give and receive only by consent. And let the Ninth sun keep its first safe day in the present, where it can be seen.”

The court debated until dawn. Some wanted the severed year restored, others the Crown Clock burned. Nial waited until every voice had spoken, then faced the throne.

“Do not restore the date,” he said. “Restore the knowledge that the year happened.”

“You have no authority to forgive the court,” Celestine said.

“I have authority to speak for the day I carried. It says the court was wrong, and the day still belongs to the living.”

Nial turned to the other heirs. “Will you speak with me?”

The seventeen stood and named the locks, the dark, the missing people, the children born during the absent years, and the songs that survived. The names did not restore the calendar. They made a living record, held by voices that could correct one another.

The Ninth sun climbed higher. The other sixteen followed it in their unequal arcs. The palace moved around them, and for the first time in ten years its shadow did not fall on any one of them.

At midday, Tamsin packed her astrolabe. The brass rings were warm from the chain.

Celestine met her at the gate. The key still hung over the Regent’s heart, but she no longer wore the crown.

“Will you stay?” the Regent asked. “The court could use another cartographer.”

“I have a salt flat to map.”

“The salt is blank now.”

“Blank is not empty.” Tamsin closed the case. “It only has fewer lies in it.”

Behind her, Nial stood beneath the Ninth window. The sun touched his shoulder, and he did not flinch.

Tamsin walked out beneath seventeen small suns. The black envelope in her pocket had become blank paper. She left it there, a map of what no one needed to own.
