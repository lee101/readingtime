# The Cartography of Small Deaths

![Cover art: The Cartography of Small Deaths](../art/advanced/89-the-cartography-of-small-deaths.webp)

Aldermere published its small-death rate on the first working day of every month, and on the morning of her thirty-ninth birthday, Dr. Adaeze Nwankwo signed the figure for the forty-seventh consecutive time, and the figure was zero.

The rate counted deaths among the people of Aldermere who were not ill, in circumstances that did not require a coroner, at ages under sixty-five. It was measured by an instrument, the Cadence Table, a brass-framed plate of black glass standing on a marble plinth in the corner of her office, which refreshed itself on Sunday nights without being touched and admitted of no adjustment by any person in the city's employment.

Her office published the figure. Two hundred and eleven organisations subscribed to it: insurers, who priced risk from it; employers, who set the payroll levy from it; landlords, who set the continuance surcharge from it; school boards, who ordered their waiting lists from it. Four thousand two hundred copies of the monthly return went out, and the twelfth line of every copy said SMALL DEATHS, WEEKLY MEAN, and the twelfth line said zero.

She had drawn the audit sample herself at eight, because the manual required one and because she did not sign what she had not looked at. Twenty returns came up from the sealed drum at random: nine lodging houses, four tram sheds, three parish registers, two harbour books, two employers' night logs. She checked each against the house it came from, and the house confirmed its return, and the return confirmed the plate, and the plate was the instrument, and the instrument was the truth.

She signed at a quarter past nine. The nib had begun to squeak in the spring and she had not replaced it, because a pen was a discretionary expense and the office was not discretionary.

* * *

Detective Ines Havelock had been forty-six for two years and a detective for twenty-two, eleven of them in the coroner's liaison unit, which nobody requests, because its work is to reconcile the inquest calendar against the Registrar's returns so that no family is asked the same question twice.

The Coroner's Office kept the other list. A coroner was required where death occurred in custody, on the works tramways, in a common lodging house of more than twenty beds during a declared heat, in a licensed conveyance, in a municipal bathhouse during a boiler inspection, at a licensed common during scheduled fumigation, or where the person attending the body had no standing to certify it. Every one of those categories had stood outside the small-death rate since 1911, on the sound ground that a coroner had already established the fact of death and the Registrar's office had no business establishing it twice.

"Nineteen this year," said Mr. Fenn, who was sixty-eight and had been the coroner for twenty-six of them. He set the page down and squared it. "Nineteen is a very good year."

"It was nineteen the year before the rate went to zero."

"It was," Fenn said. "And the small-death column that year ran close to six a week, and I have thought about those two numbers together more than I have thought about most things, and I could not make them fit, and I am not paid to make them fit." He said it without heat. He was a man reporting a difficulty he had no jurisdiction over. "Section nine is the oldest section in my book and the only one never amended, and I have never seen a reason to amend it, and I would like it noted that I said that in writing in 1998."

Havelock copied the exemption into her own book and thanked him, and on the tram home she worked out that the coroner's list for the last twelve months was four hundred and twelve, and that four hundred and twelve appeared on the front of no report in the city.

* * *

Nwankwo went to the Empress herself, on the Sunday, because the lodgers belonged to her office and she would not send the clerk. The Empress was a common lodging house of twenty-six beds over a shop in Railway Row, and the householder was a woman of fifty-eight called Ada Kelsall who had kept it nineteen years and who came to the door in a cardigan with a marker in her hand, because there is always a marker in a householder's hand.

"I know what you have come about," she said, and did not invite her in until the authority was produced, and then she invited her in and made tea and was entirely composed. "Nineteen returns from this house this year and not one of them a small death. It is a good year. It has been a good four years. I could show you the ledgers back to the spring of 2019 and there is nothing in them for anybody to complain about, and I have looked, because there is nothing else to do on a Tuesday."

"How many people have you buried out of this house since the first of January?"

Ada Kelsall put the marker down on the table, which Nwankwo understood to be what a householder does when she is about to do sums. "A hundred and eleven," she said. "Thirty-one in the last month. Twenty-two of the thirty-one were resident in March, and I have their dates and I have their sheets, and eleven of the twenty-two had a certificate from the parish saying they were comfortable." She picked the marker up again. "I write what I am given. If you want a thing not to be true, do not ask a householder. Ask a doctor, because a doctor signs it, and a householder puts on the form exactly what a doctor signs."

Nwankwo asked what the house was like. The house was full, and had been full for four years, and the wait for a bed was nine weeks, and a bed in a common lodging house of more than twenty beds was the only address a certain number of Aldermere citizens had, and the parish relief office could not send anybody anywhere without an address.

"So the house is full," Nwankwo said.

"The house is full," Ada Kelsall agreed, "and the Table says there is no reason for me to be anxious, because there is no rating on this house at all, because there is nobody in it to be rated."

* * *

The Registrar's office kept the Sunday plates in a flat file, one a week, four years of them, and the plates were not confidential, because nothing was confidential that the Registrar's office produced, and the flat file stood against the wall where anyone with a business involving a death could stand in front of it for as long as they liked.

Havelock stood in front of it on a Thursday with a stool and a week-by-week tally sheet, and she was still there at eleven, and Nwankwo found her at half past, holding a plate flat on both hands like a tray of something breakable.

"You cannot carry these out of the building," Nwankwo said.

"They are public. Section twelve, sub two, records of an instrument in the Registrar's office are not withheld from any person, on account of the instrument's function or otherwise."

"You have been in here five hours."

"I have been reading the wrong column, I expect."

The plate was the size of a school exercise book and thicker, and it carried four columns. The first three were the ones everybody saw: resolved, unresolved, returned. The fourth ran down the right-hand edge in smaller figures and had no heading, and beside each entry in it sat a household number and a name.

"What is that."

"The provisional column." Nwankwo said it the way she said most things, flatly, in the register of a person reading out a schedule. "It lists the households whose projection resolves inside seven days. The Table publishes it on the Sunday and it clears on the Wednesday. No office has ever asked me to explain it because no office has ever been interested in it. It is a projection. People find projections boring."

Havelock turned the plate over. The reverse carried the ledger date and the officer of record, and for every Sunday for four years the officer of record on the provisional column was the same man, a claims assessor named Nabil Sugrue, and against his name, in the small hand of a scheduling clerk, the word SOLE.

They took nine plates to the Coroner's Office and laid them out in date order on Fenn's table, and Fenn looked at them for a long time and then asked for the pencils, because he wanted to mark rather than trust, and Havelock let him.

"Eighty-one of these," Fenn said, at two o'clock. "Eighty-one deaths in eleven weeks, in the exemption categories, and the exemption has been the same since 1911, and not one of these eighty-one appears in the Registrar's returns, because they are not small deaths." He put the pencil down. "They are the only deaths the city is having."

"Who else could have written them?"

"Nobody," Fenn said. "The Table does not write. The Table reports. The person in the corner who has been looking at this every Sunday for four years is the person who knows, and he has been extremely careful, and I have to say to you, Inspector, that a man who wanted a city to die is not careful like that."

* * *

The Continuance Rating was the reason the number mattered, and it was set out in nine pages of the manual that Nwankwo could recite, because she had read the manual in her first month and had read it again in her fourth year, which is when the zeros started, which is when she first understood what the zeros were for.

A household's rating was computed from its projected small deaths. Above the threshold of one point four per decade the household paid a continuance surcharge, which the landlord passed on, and its children fell to the back of the school board's list, and its insurance was declined at renewal, and its employer's levy rose by a step. In the four years since the rate went to zero, three hundred and eighty-eight people had come off the marginal band and stayed off it, and the office had measured each of them, and the measurements were not arguable: the flats were still tenanted, the jobs were still held, the places at the school of St Ambrose and the Nautical College were still filled.

Three hundred and eighty-eight, and Nwankwo had a folder for each of them, because the Registrar's office is required to keep a file for every household whose rating has changed in either direction.

On the Thursday she took the detective to a kitchen in Railway Row, where a woman of fifty-four named Vera Osei had been paying a surcharge of nine pounds a month on a two-room flat above a chandler's shop, and the surcharge had gone to nothing in the month the zeros were signed, and had stayed at nothing, and in the four years since she had kept the flat, kept her job at the biscuit works, and put her granddaughter through two years of the Nautical College.

"That is not a coincidence," Vera Osei said. "You people did that. I do not care how you did it. The rent came down and the college came open and I have the letters."

"It is not a coincidence," Nwankwo said. "It is a consequence of the rate, and the rate is a measurement, and the measurement has not been falsified, and the file is in my office with your name on it, and you are welcome to read it."

"You are welcome to read it," Vera Osei agreed, "and I have read it. It is four pages. It says my rating is zero. It does not say why."

* * *

Sugrue worked on the fourth floor of the Claims Annexe, in a room with eleven desks, and he had asked for the Tuesday slot because the plate refreshed at four on Sunday and he wanted to be in the building on Monday morning when it landed, and nobody had ever wanted the Monday slot, and so he had it.

He was thirty-one. He came to the interview with a folder under his arm and offered to make tea first. He was polite, unhurried, and entirely willing to talk, and he talked for two hours, and he was right about everything he said.

The sister was Rhoda Sugrue, who kept a room over a laundry on Cobb Street and died in a common lodging house of twenty-six beds, aged fifty-eight, in February, of a fall. She had not been ill. There had been no coroner. She had been the one line in the small-death column that month that everybody in the building had felt, and her death had gone into the district's projection, and the projection had moved the whole postal district up a band, and eleven other households in Cobb Street had gone above the threshold in the same quarter, and the surcharge had taken eleven families' flats, and six children had been taken off the school list and sent to the warders' rooms.

"In the manual," Sugrue said, "on page one hundred and forty-one, there is a paragraph. It says that where a household rating is computed from a projection, and the projection is not realised within the projection period, the rating shall be computed on realised occurrences only. The whole rating scheme rests on a table of things that have not happened yet. She was the only person in Cobb Street who happened."

He opened the folder. Inside were the eleven years of Sunday plates, his own working marginals, four years of a second list, and a single sheet of ledger paper on which he had set out two columns in a very small hand.

"In the twelve months to March, the Table's provisional column resolved four hundred and twelve times. Every one of those is in the Coroner's Office, and I arranged every one, and I am not going to sit here and be coy about it." He looked up. "I took them from the marginal band. I took them because they were in it, and the Table itself had marked them, and the Table is a government instrument, and if the city cannot be bothered to care which of its own residents it expects to die this week, then neither can I."

"And the three hundred and eighty-eight."

"Three hundred and eighty-eight, in the same period, off the band and staying off it. That is the trade. It is not a good trade. I have never once pretended to anybody that it is a good trade." He squared the folder. "You want to know whether I think about the four hundred and twelve. I set the figure up. I did it in a room with a plate on a table."

* * *

They did not prosecute, and the reason was not sentiment, and Deputy City Attorney Teodora Ranz wrote it down in two paragraphs so that nobody in the building could later pretend that it had been a feeling.

There was no charge available. No return had been falsified; every weekly return in the city was true. No instrument had been altered; the plate was black glass and could not be altered. No record had been destroyed; the plates were public, the ledger was public, and the whole of the four years stood in a flat file against a wall where anybody with a business involving a death could read it. The city had been lied to, comprehensively, by a true number, and there is no statute in the world for prosecuting a man for arithmetic.

"The disclosure will do the damage instead," Ranz said. "Section four of the Rating Act: where the small-death rate is restated, every rating in the city is recomputed on the restated figure, on the first working day of the following quarter. Three hundred and eighty-eight households. That is a real number, and I have had it modelled, and it is not a quarter of them, it is nearly all of them, because the surcharge is a step and the step is what was holding the flat."

Havelock had worked out the same thing in November and had not slept well in December, and she said so, because she was a police officer and the file recorded what police officers said. "I have spent nine months," she said, "believing that a number that cannot be wrong is the worst kind of number there is. I am not willing to be the officer who puts three hundred and eighty-eight families back on a list to prove that I was right."

The Registrar sat with her folder and did the thing she had done every month for four years, which was to check the figure, and the figure checked out, and she had verified a sample, and the sample was accurate, and a measurement is not falsified by being true, and there was no clause in the manual that covered a registrar who was right.

* * *

What they commissioned instead was an instrument, which was the one thing all three of them had in common, being people who did not trust a conversation.

A Schedule of Concealed Circumstances, to be prepared by the officer with standing, motive and access, which was Sugrue, and to include, without exception, every occurrence the small-death rate has excluded since the first of January four years ago, arranged in date order, with dates, circumstances, names, and the mechanism of each. It was to be published in the Aldermere Gazette in weekly instalments, unabridged, under his own name, and the Registrar's office was to countersign each instalment, which made the city a party to it.

He asked for two things, and he asked for them in writing, and the second of them is the reason he signed. He asked that his name appear. And he asked for an interim schedule, seventeen clauses, which froze every rating in the city at its present figure until the Schedule closed, so that the disclosure could not be used to reprice the three hundred and eighty-eight while it was being read.

"That second clause is not for us," Nwankwo said, when he had gone. "It is for them."

"It is for the document," Sugrue said. "An audit that changes the thing it audits is not an audit, it is an event, and events get argued about. I have spent four years being argued about." He took his folder. "I would rather be printed."

* * *

The first instalment ran on a Thursday in the spring, four columns of Gazette, eleven names on the first page, and by the fourth Thursday the paper had a waiting list at the print works, because people wanted to buy the paper and then keep it, which is not a thing anybody has ever wanted to do with a municipal gazette.

The city held it. The council debated the interim schedule for two months and extended it, and read the four hundred and twelve names into the record of the chamber, and a woman whose sister was on the fourth page read them at a church hall in Cobb Street with the register in her hand. The insurers wrote to the Registrar asking for the restated rate. Two hundred and eleven organisations received a letter from the Registrar's office with a Schedule of Concealed Circumstances in it and no restated rate, and eleven of them subscribed to the Gazette in the following quarter, which no one had predicted and which the Registrar's annual report noted, once, without comment.

On a Thursday a year in, in a room above a chandler's shop in Railway Row, Nabil Sugrue, who was thirty-two by then and would stay thirty-two for a while, sat at a table with a proof sheet, a compositor's pencil, and the Thursday plate, and set the columns by hand because he did not trust the machine with a name.

On a Friday in the same month, on the other side of the city, in an office on the first floor with a marble plinth in the corner, Adaeze Nwankwo, thirty-nine, read instalment fifty-two with a ruler down the margin, checking the arithmetic of another man's confession against an instrument that could not be argued with, and the black glass on the plinth turned over at midnight, as it had every Sunday for four years, and told the truth.
