# The Empire Built on a Sleeping Debt

![Cover art: The Empire Built on a Sleeping Debt](../art/advanced/66-the-empire-built-on-a-sleeping-debt.webp)

The Vashen Empire was founded on a loan, and the loan had been made in the ninth year of the calendar then in use, by a people called the Tenu, to a man called Gavros Vashen, a timber factor at the mouth of a river four thousand kilometres from anywhere the Empire would later stand. The Empire had been solvent for nineteen hundred and fourteen consecutive years, and the number that proved it had not changed since the day of the loan.

Marguerite Ancel read that number into a brass horn on the first working day of every fiscal year, in the Hall of Nine Columns in Teshkar, before one representative of each of the nine departments, and she was thirty-four and had held the office of Chief Accountant of Obligations for two of those years. The number was four million, six hundred and twelve thousand, nine hundred and forty crowns. She read it the way her mother had read it, unhurried, with the figure broken into its groups so that a man who had been drinking could keep up.

“Nine departments have heard it,” said the Herald of the Reading, who was a boy of about fifteen with a wax tablet and a bad cough. “Seven, chief. The Ministry of War did not send anyone.”

“The Ministry of War sends nobody when the Saline Account is presented,” Marguerite said. “Enter it as heard in absentia. It is heard.”

* * *

Behind the Hall of Nine Columns, in a room with no windows, there was a cabinet of oak with glass doors, and inside it a file nine feet tall, and the file was called the Covenant File, and it had been the most heavily consulted object in the Empire for two thousand years in the sense that everybody knew exactly how heavy it was and nobody had ever opened it. The measures taken to preserve the demand from being made were kept in a second cabinet, in nine volumes.

Marguerite had read all nine volumes in her first year, because her mother had told her to. The first dynasty had been false, installed in the four hundred and eleventh year so that the original succession could be presumed to have ended without an heir; the Ash Plague had been a quarantine certificate, drafted to stop a claimant from reaching the capital in time to make an argument; the Saline War had been fought against the Ninth Tharsenites, an enemy invented to absorb a surplus that had begun to look, in the annual comparison, like a debt.

Every one of those measures was lawful. That was the part her mother had made her understand before she let her sign anything. The Empire had never committed a crime against the Tenu; it had simply declined, nine times over nine generations, to answer a letter.

“You will be asked,” her mother had said, “what you would have done differently. Say that you would have done it sooner. That is the whole of the answer, and it is true.”

* * *

The boy came in on the eleventh day of the month, and he came to the public counter, which is where anyone without an appointment is required to come, and he was seventeen years old and very thin, and he put a bundle onto the counter wrapped in oilskin and tied with cord, and he unwrapped it, and inside it was a piece of paper that had been folded and refolded until the folds had gone through.

“Demand,” he said.

Marguerite was behind the counter because on the day of a public presentation the Chief Accountant takes the counter herself, and this is not humility, it is procedure, because the Act of Continuance holds that a demand upon the Empire must be received by the officer of the Continuing Obligation or by nobody.

The clerk beside her, Osdar Ruhl, a filing clerk for twenty-six years, wrote the word into the day book and then stopped, because there was a column in the day book headed RECIPIENT, and there had never been anything in it.

“What is your name,” Marguerite said. “For the receipt.”

“Ilkai Tenu,” the boy said. “Last of the line. I am the last of the line. My grandmother died in the winter and there is nobody after me, and the paper is not mine, it is the copy, and I have carried the copy, and I have walked two thousand kilometres, and I would like it entered.”

“Entered where?”

“In the Register of Obligations,” Ilkai Tenu said. “Under your covenant.”

Ruhl looked up. He was fifty-one and he had never once, in his professional opinion, been surprised by anything, and he was now surprised, and the surprise showed as a small movement of the eyes. “Chief,” he said. “There is no such column.”

“There is no such column,” Marguerite agreed. “Write it in the margin in pencil. He is here, he is of age, and he has asked.”

Marguerite did not dismiss him and she did not accept him, and the reason she could not decide between the two was that neither instrument was built for a boy standing at a counter in the ninth month of a year that had been dry. The Act of Continuance provided that a demand might be received, and the Standing Instruction of the Office of Obligations, issued in the eleven hundred and fourth year by the seventh Chief Accountant, provided that the covenant should not be re-opened.

“There is a reason for it,” Marguerite said, and the boy nodded, as though she had been arguing the other side. “You have not read it.”

“No,” he said. “I have read the other one.”

* * *

They took the long way, because the boy wanted to see the buildings, and because Marguerite found that she did not want to arrive at the Chapter House with the argument already made. The Tenu cut came up from the eastern boundary eight hundred years after the Empire had stopped paying for it, and it was paid for in a currency that had not existed when the covenant was drawn, the same currency the child buggies take.

She walked it with him in the long afternoon, past the arsenal and the flats and the provincial bank, and he told her that his grandmother had kept the papers in a tin box under the floor and had told him, when he was eleven, that the debt was theirs and not hers, and that a debt is not a shame but a job.

“I have read the covenant,” he said, “the same as everyone who has ever been angry at a government. It does not help. It is a good covenant. It is fair on its face, and every copy of it in the Empire is sealed, and the sealing is what the people who govern you will point at, and the sealing is done by the office of my mother’s employer.”

Marguerite stopped walking. “The Chapter House at Ander is maintained by the Office of Obligations.”

“It is maintained by the office of your employer,” Ilkai Tenu said. “It was built for a copy of the covenant, and the copy is the only one in the Empire that is not sealed, and I have been reading it in the cold for four months, and it is four pages, and you are going to ask me what is on the fourth page, and I am not going to tell you.”

“Why not?”

“Because it is not mine,” the boy said. “It is yours. It has been yours for two thousand years.”

* * *

The Chapter House of Ander was a very old building on the Vashen plain, one storey, with a roof of lead and a refectory that had been the dormitory of a dissolved college, and the library at the end of it had a stone floor and forty-one books, twenty of them liturgical and nineteen a water-damaged account of a mill. The copy of the covenant lay on a reading slope under a sheet of oiled paper, and the two monks who lived there had known a boy was sleeping in the refectory since March and had said nothing, because he was polite about books.

Marguerite read it, and she read it well. She was the Chief Accountant of Obligations and she had been trained from fifteen in a dead language that nobody spoke, and had never had cause to use it for anything.

“The fourth page is folded,” she said, and it was.

The fold was not a fold. It was the original fold of a document that had been rolled when it was wet, and the fold had been made in the first year, and the page had been joined to the third by a strip of linen-sized parchment with a seal on the join, and the seal was Gavros Vashen’s, and it was the only intact Vashen seal in the world.

“That is not a seal over the fourth page,” Marguerite said. “That is a seal over the join. I know this seal. It is on the receipt. It certifies the whole instrument. Which means somebody broke it.”

Ilkai Tenu said, “I did not break it. The first copy is in your vault and the second is in the charter house and the third is under the floor of a house in Sef that I have never seen. You have three. I have one. The monastery has one. Five. Somebody has to account for the other, and I have stopped caring, because I have read them, chief. All four pages.”

“Whose hand is that?”

“Mine,” the boy said. “It is my hand. I wrote the fourth page. I copied it, and it is in the dead language, and I did it in nine weeks, and I did not know what it was until I had it out, and then I could not put it down, and I read it again four thousand times.”

* * *

In the nine hundred and twentieth year the salt wars had put eighty thousand people on the eastern road, and the Tenu had not gone home, and the Second Office of Assizes had issued a certificate that no claimant of the line of Gavros Vashen survived, and the certificate was true in the way that a certificate is true, which is to say that nobody had looked. The Ninth Tharsenites came up that autumn and killed the people on the road, and the Empire paid for the war, in a province nobody had visited.

Marguerite did not say any of this. She took the fourth page down off the slope, and she got the linen join with the seal, and she asked the monk to bring her the reading lamp, and she said, “You will have to stop me if I get to the fourth page.”

“I am seventy-one,” said the Prior of Ander. “I could not stop you from a chair.”

It said that the said loan was made by the Tenu out of their own lands, their own waters, and the whole of the territory between the two salt rivers, and that the Empire received it not in coin but in the possession of that territory, and that the principal of the loan was therefore the land and not the sum, and that the sum in the schedule was the valuation and not the obligation. And it said that the loan was interest free, and bullet, and payable on demand, and that on demand it was repayable in kind and not in coin, and that the repayment in kind was the return of the said territory, entire, to the said lenders and to their line, and that a return of territory is complete when it is held by them and not by any successor.

“It was always the fourth page,” Marguerite said.

“Yes,” the boy said.

“The first three pages have been read in this Empire for two thousand years. I have read them. My mother read them. The Arch-Chancellor has the first three pages in a frame in his office because they are beautiful.”

“They are beautiful,” Ilkai Tenu said. “I have copied them four times. I can recite the third page. It is the fourth page that they seal, and I know why, and it is not because of the four hundred and eleven years. It is because of the word territory, and the word line.”

She stayed in the Chapter House for two days, and the monks gave her the room at the end that the boy had been sleeping in, and on the second morning the boy came in with a sheet of paper in his own writing and said, “I am not going to explain it to you. I am going to let you explain it to me, and then you can decide what your office is.”

So Marguerite sat in a cold room on the Vashen plain and read out the fourth page of the Covenant of Tenu Harbour four times.

The first three times it came out in the dead language and she stumbled in the middle of the long word for territory and the boy said nothing at all. The fourth time the Prior of Ander put his finger on the first line and said the sentence with her, slowly, and it took eleven minutes, and at the end of it the Prior said, in the ordinary language, that the dead tongue is a very good instrument for making a promise, because it cannot be improved on, and then he went out and shut the door.

The Arch-Chancellor was seventy-one and had been in the service for fifty-one of those years. He came to the Chapter House on the second afternoon, and he read the page, and he said, “Four hundred and eleven years.”

“Eleven generations,” Marguerite said.

“Eleven generations,” the Arch-Chancellor agreed. “I have read the first three pages in that frame, and signed four instruments that refer to the covenant, and never in my life had occasion to ask which page was under the join, because the join is where the seal is.” He put the page down. “I am not going to resign. I want that on the record. I am not going to say that the Empire was founded on a fraud. It was founded on a document that four men in one room read out loud to each other, and then everybody after them was busy.”

* * *

She slept for one hour in the refectory and then went down to the Hall of Nine Columns at the fourth hour, and the clerks were there, because clerks are always there at the fourth hour, and there were nine of them and a records officer and a lawyer of the office, named Perev Koll, seventeen years in the post, who had never once asked to see the covenant.

Marguerite put the fourth page on the table in front of her, and then she said, “Read it out, please. I would like it read.”

“If the Chief Accountant says a thing, it is written down, and if it is written down, it happened,” said Perev Koll. “That has been the practice of this office since the office existed.”

“That has been the practice of this office since the office existed,” Marguerite said, “and the practice is wrong. The practice is the reason there has been a Standing Instruction for nine generations, and the Standing Instruction is the reason there has been a file, and the file is the reason there has been an Empire. If I say that the sea is dry, then the sea is dry, and there is no instrument by which anybody is ever corrected, because the only instrument is me. That is a person with a stamp.”

Nobody said anything for a while. Then Perev Koll said, “What does the Chief Accountant propose?”

“The Chief Accountant proposes that the office stops answering for the covenant in the next three minutes,” Marguerite said, “and that we find out what the Empire is when it has answered it.”

* * *

The Empire was dissolved in eleven days. It was dissolved by a memorandum, and not by a war, and not by a famine, and not by an invasion, and there was not one death in the whole business except the four the monks had buried at Ander in the eight hundred and twentieth year, and Marguerite Ancel had them reinterred in the proper ground at the proper expense out of the Saline Account, which is the only use she ever found for the Saline Account.

The Saline War was stood down on the second day, and the provincial garrisons were paid their arrears, and the Ninth Tharsenites were recorded in the year book as an error of the ninth Tharsenite enumeration, and the Ash Plague certificate was revoked, and a quarantine that had lasted eight hundred and sixteen years was lifted in four lines, and the Office of the Assizes was instructed to reopen the succession claims it had certified dead, two hundred and forty of them founded on the certificate.

The Plain of Ander was held in common from the first of the month. The four thousand kilometres were not moved and could not be, and the boy did not ask for them, and the answer was that he could not have them, and that he had known it when he set out, and that the point had never been the ground.

“It is never the ground,” Ilkai Tenu said. “That is the whole misunderstanding. The ground cannot be returned. Nobody is owed ground. You are owed a duty, and a duty is discharged by a person and not by a county.”

He wanted to be a person. That had been on the table since the second day in the Chapter House, laid out with the same flat competence as everything else he said, and the empire had gone in the middle of it, and the two of them were sitting on a step outside the Hall of Nine Columns with the memorandum in Marguerite’s coat.

“If everything I was owed is a duty and not a piece of ground,” Ilkai Tenu said, “then I have no idea what to do with the rest of my life, and I have been walking towards this for two years, chief, and I can go back, but I cannot go back, because going back means the tin box under the floor, and the tin box means the roof over my head, and the roof over my head is the only thing anybody ever gave me that I did not have to be angry about.”

“I know,” Marguerite said. “I had an office.”

“You had a lock,” he said, “and you have lost it, and you are going to want it back, and I am not going to pretend to you that you will get it, because nobody who loses the thing they were good at ever gets it back, they only get a different thing, and it is always worse, and it is always theirs.”

“And what is mine?”

“A debt of your own,” Ilkai Tenu said. “You are going to be the officer who says what the Empire is, and you will have to say it in a room, and there will be no Act, and the minutes will be a thing you write, and one day somebody will come and ask you whether it is true, and you will have to look at the paper.”

Marguerite said, “That is not a comfort.”

“It is not meant to be,” said the boy, “it is meant to be a job.”

* * *

The discharge was executed at the Hall of Nine Columns on the ninth day of the twelfth month, in the presence of the nine departments, the first of which was the Ministry of War, and it sent a man this time, and he had walked to Teshkar and had come in dusty and stood at the back.

Marguerite read the fourth page aloud into the brass horn. She read it in the dead language, and she was bad at it, and she had not practised it in front of anybody, and the Hall of Nine Columns, ninety feet from end to end, heard every error she made and let her finish. Then the Provost asked her to read it again, and the Prior of Ander, who had come down from the plain and was sitting in the third seat, said it with her the second time, slowly, and when she came to the long word for territory she took it out of his mouth, and she went on.

The last of the line was given the paper to sign as creditor, and the Ministry of War’s man asked him whether he was sure, and the boy said that there was no version of this in which he was sure, and that signing was not the same as being sure, and that his grandmother had told him that a receipt is a thing you give a person who has been waiting, and the waiting was hers and the paper was his.

The form was one page, and it had a heading, and the heading was the same heading that had been on the covenant for two thousand years, and the two of them were on the same page at the bottom of a hall ninety feet long with nine departments watching and a brass horn on the table with the cap off.

Marguerite Ancel, Chief Accountant of Obligations of the Vashen Empire, the last office of that name, countersigned below him, in a small upright hand, and the first signature above it was in a round unlearned hand with no flourishes in it at all.

Under the two signatures the page was otherwise empty, and the words that should have gone there were the words neither of them said, because one was the last officer of an empire and one was the last of a line, and the thank you was the one that would have been a transaction, and the goodbye was the one that neither of them would say first.
