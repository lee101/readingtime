# The Girl Who Inherited a God

![Cover art: The Girl Who Inherited a God](../art/advanced/144-the-girl-who-inherited-a-god.webp)

The shrine at the Weaver's Turn is a stone box the size of a bread bin with a slate lid and a green lamp on a spike, and it has been on the verge road since before the road was metalled, and it is the only building in Halversgate that has never once been repaired.

It does one thing. It finds a lost thing, and it tells you where the lost thing is, and it does not do that for nothing.

Each answer costs it a page of its own account, taken out of the book in order, from the front, and it has been doing this for two hundred years, and there were eleven pages left in it when she took it over.

* * *

Anwen Rudd is twenty-two. She has dark hair cut straight to her jaw, a grey coat she bought at a charity shop in Corve, and a habit of reading the small print on things nobody has asked her to read.

She has been the clerk at Barnett and Slyne, solicitors, of Halversgate, for two years, and the office is above the chemist on Weir Lane, and the work is conveyancing and probate and the twelve pounds a week that goes with it.

On the second of April a solicitor called Cass Ardell, who is fifty-three and wears a brown waistcoat she has never had cleaned, gave her a folder and told her to put it in her bag, and said, “You should not open it here.”

* * *

The folder is a sheaf of twenty-eight pages in a brown board cover, and the first page is a schedule of a smallholding at Cove Head that nobody has owned since the war, and the second page is a name.

The name is Aldis Vane. He was seventy-one and he died in a nursing home in Corve on the twenty-ninth of March, and he had lived alone in a council house on Ferrier Row for nineteen years, and he had no family, and he left no will.

“There is no estate,” said Cass Ardell. “Eleven hundred and forty pounds, most of it in a building society account that has to be closed before anything goes, and no beneficiaries, and a donor.”

“A donor to what.”

“To the shrine at the Weaver's Turn.”

* * *

Anwen read the twenty-eight pages in the office that afternoon, because she could not not.

The third page was a letter in a hand that got worse as it went. It said that Aldis Vane had held the shrine for nineteen years, that he had taken it from Daveth Ollerton, who had taken it from a woman called Marda Quill, who had taken it from a man whose name had been struck through and written again and was not legible.

The fourth page said what holding it was.

Aldis Vane had done it on Tuesdays and Fridays, when he was not at the bowls club, and he had kept a ledger, and the ledger was the fifth page, and it was two hundred and nine entries in a hand that got worse as it went, and every entry had the same shape: a thing, a date, and where it was.

* * *

A dog, west field at Cove Head, under the second gate. Tuesday. Fourteen rows.

A wedding ring, the shingle at the Turn, thirty yards below the wall. Friday. Two rows.

A door at number four Ferrier Row, which was locked and had been locked for six weeks. Tuesday. Three rows.

A boy, the church at Draymoor, walked out on the Sunday morning. Friday. Nine rows.

A boundary stone between Tarnwick and Ninebridge. Tuesday. One row.

That is what it does. Not a lost dog in the abstract. The place where the thing is, to the yard, with a row count, and no opinion about who put it there.

The rota on the sixth page says when it works. Tuesdays and Fridays, half past seven in the morning to half past seven at night, and any other time if somebody writes to the box at Halversgate post office in an emergency, which happens about four times a year.

* * *

The seventh page is a budget.

Aldis Vane paid the electricity at the shrine out of his own pocket for nineteen years and had claimed it back from nothing. The shrine's cost is one bulb and one bulb costs nineteen pence, and the ledger has a column for the nineteen pence and the column has been filled in on every fifth entry since the year Marda Quill held it, which is the year the columns were added.

The eighth page is the schedule of the rota itself, and against it a second column headed ATTENDED BY, and against Tuesdays and Fridays there is a series of initials going back to a woman whose initials are M.Q.

There is no money in it. That is what takes a girl of twenty-two eleven minutes to understand in a solicitor's office above a chemist: the whole of this is one old man's time and one light bulb, and there is no fund, no trust, no deed, no priest, and nobody to hand it to.

* * *

She went out to the Turn on the Saturday because it was four miles and she thought the walk would help.

The stone box has no door on the front. There is a slot at the bottom the width of a hand and a quarter deep, and inside it there is a stub of pencil and, in a tin, about forty pounds in coins and one-pound notes in date order.

There is a lamp on the spike. Aldis Vane had left it off.

On the front of the box, cut into the stone and weathered almost smooth, there is a figure. It is not a saint and it is not a god and it is not a person. It is a wheel with four spokes, and beside it, cut smaller and newer than the rest, there are two sets of initials, M.Q., and D.O., and A.V., and a fourth set that had been started and abandoned.

Nobody in Halversgate can say what the wheel means. The Advertiser did a piece on the shrine in the June four years ago and got the same answer from eleven people.

* * *

She did the first one on the Tuesday because she could not sleep and there was nothing else to do with it.

The entry in the ledger is in her hand and it says: spectacles, left on the number 9 bus, Tuesday eleventh of April. Row eleven of the bottom step. Nineteen pence.

The spectacles were on the bottom step, in the middle, under a folded piece of newspaper that was not yesterday's.

She took them to the bus station and gave them to the driver, and he said the man had come in on the Friday and said they were in his coat and then had stood at the counter for ten minutes looking at them, and had gone away without them, and had come back at four o'clock.

He had not seen who left them. Nobody on that bus had seen who left them.

She came home and sat down and looked at the ledger for a while and worked out what she had done, which was that a page had gone.

* * *

The eleventh of May.

The third of June.

The twenty-second of June.

The ninth of July.

She did them from the entries in the ledger, because that is what Aldis Vane had done, and because it turned out to be the whole of the job, and because on the ninth of July a woman asked her about a ring and did not ask who she was.

The book the answers are paid in is a different book from the ledger. It is the shrine's own account, ruled forty lines to a page, and it runs from a hundred and forty-one to a hundred and fifty-three because somebody numbered the first hundred and forty in one year and went on.

She counted the entries backwards from the last page and did it four times. Two hundred years at two days a week is over twenty thousand lines, which is five hundred pages, and there is no money in any of it, and nobody has ever wanted there to be.

* * *

What nobody told her, and what she found out on the fourteenth of July, is that you can write to it.

There is a slot at the bottom of the box and about four times a year somebody writes to it, and Aldis Vane's rule was that an emergency goes in and gets done before the next scheduled day, whatever it is.

In July she got one from a house on Marsh Lane: cat, missing since Sunday, will not answer to name.

She went on the Tuesday. The answer is a place and a row count and no opinion, and she wrote it on the card and left it in the letter box of the house on Marsh Lane at about six in the evening, as Aldis Vane had done nine hundred times.

On the Friday the woman telephoned the library to say the cat was in the porch behind the water butt and had been there since Sunday.

She had asked a friend. The friend had put it there.

* * *

That was the thing that stopped it, and nobody in Halversgate has ever been told that it was stopped by that.

On the twentieth of July a man from the county came out to the Turn on the strength of a letter, because a woman who runs the post office in Corve had written to the council to ask whether the stone box on the verge was a listed structure and who maintains it.

His name was Daveth Ollerton, and he was sixty-eight, and he had held the shrine from the year Aldis Vane's aunt died and had handed it on because he was going into a home in the March, and he had not been near the Turn since and had expected a great deal to happen in his absence.

He sat on the verge with his back to the road for two hours and read the ledger.

Then he asked Anwen Rudd what she thought she was doing.

* * *

“I have not done one since the twenty-second of July,” she said. “It is the eleventh of August.”

“There is a page missing,” said Daveth Ollerton. “There are five pages missing out of the front of this book. I counted them on the Monday. Somebody has taken five pages out of it.”

“They are spent,” said Anwen Rudd.

“They are spent on what.”

And that is where she made the mistake, and she has said since that she was twenty-two and that somebody in her position would have made it, and that she was three weeks out of a solicitor's office and had had a folder in a brown board cover five months before that.

“It is not a god,” she said. “It is a rota with a budget and a list and a lamp, and somebody has to do the Tuesdays and the Fridays, and the pages go when it is done, and that is all it is.”

* * *

Daveth Ollerton went through it in eleven minutes.

He asked her what she thought was on page five hundred and twelve, which is what the arithmetic gives you. She said she did not know.

He asked her who had made the wheel. She said she did not know.

He asked her what the last two hundred and nine entries were for if not somebody being answered.

He said the last thing she has repeated back to herself since, and he said it in a flat voice on a verge in the sun with his back to the road.

“My aunt did the Tuesdays for thirty-one years,” he said. “She did them for a lost dog and a wedding ring and a door at number four Ferrier Row and a boy who walked out of the church at Draymoor, and you are telling me it is a mechanism.”

“It is a mechanism,” said Anwen Rudd.

“Then get somebody else,” said Daveth Ollerton, “because I did not hand it to a mechanism and I am not going to be the man who says so at a hearing.”

* * *

That was in the second week of August. The rest of it was the winter and the spring and it went the way those things go.

The requests did not stop in a week. They stopped over eleven weeks, and the reason, which Anwen Rudd worked out afterwards from the card in the tin at the back of the box, was that you have to write to it, and the person who wrote the cat letter had told the woman who runs the post office in Corve, because she was the one who posted the Tuesday cards and she had asked.

There were nine written requests in the ledger for the year. There were two in the following year, and none at all in the year after that, and in the year after that the post office in Corve stopped getting cards to go out on a Tuesday because there was nothing to go out.

The man at the county came back in the September and put a paper notice on the box, and it said that the structure was not listed and that the council had no responsibility for it and that the parish council had asked whoever was keeping it to contact them.

* * *

The scheduled work did not stop for that. Nobody writing a card knew anything about the notice.

She did the Fridays for another seven months on her own, because the answers were in the ledger and doing them took forty minutes on a Friday and it seemed a waste not to, and she went out on the Tuesdays with a notebook and did the ones she could see.

A handbag, Ferry Row, under the seat of the bench at the harbour end of Dock Street. Tuesday. One row.

There is a bench at the harbour end of Dock Street, and it is the one place in Halversgate that people sit on facing the water.

She found the handbag, and there was a fish-and-chip wrapper in it and eleven pounds, and she gave all of it back.

A straying collie, the reservoir road, half a mile past the turning for the lock-up. Friday. Four rows.

A wedding ring, Cove Head beach, in the sand about nine feet below the tide line. Friday. It was not retrievable and she wrote that, and that is in the ledger, and it is the only entry in four hundred that says a thing was not done.

* * *

In the April the two people in the world who could have explained any of it were both unavailable for most of it.

Daveth Ollerton was in a home and could be got on a good day only, and had said he would not say anything more about the stone box and had meant it as a kindness and it was not received as one.

Cass Ardell at the solicitors had eleven wills to write and no interest in a stone box, and had been entirely correct, and had said in the January that the smallholding at Cove Head still had to be dealt with and that it had been dealt with twice already by mistake and once properly.

The sixty-two pounds that had been in the tin was got into the parish account in the March by a form, and Anwen Rudd did not sign it and would not explain why.

* * *

The thing she did was on the ninth of May, and she has described it since, when she talks about it at all, as looking.

She sat in her car at the Turn for an hour and ten minutes with the ledger open on the passenger seat and went through two hundred and thirteen entries, four hundred years of Tuesdays and Fridays, and read what they were, which was: a dog, a ring, a door, a boy, a stone, a spectacles case, a straying collie, a scarf, a hedgehog in a garden, a set of keys, a false tooth.

Not one of them in two hundred years asked what it was for.

That is what she found at the end of the hour and ten minutes. She had gone looking for a god and a machine and a fraud, in that order, and what was in the book was a list of lost things and the places where they were, kept by people who could not read and did not need to, for two hundred years, for nothing.

* * *

The next Friday she went down at half past seven and did the first one of her own.

A dog, west field at Cove Head, under the second gate.

She had not put it in the ledger and she did not know the row count and she walked the fence line twice and found it under the second gate, on the boundary between one field and the next, and it came when she called it.

Then she went back to the box and took out the front section of the account, which is the six pages that are left of it, and read the entries on them.

It is not a god. It has never been a god, and there is nobody in two hundred years of it who thought it was, and M.Q. and D.O. and A.V. were not priests of anything at all but three people who could not read in a county where that was common and who were each very good at counting rows.

The wheel on the front of the box is a mill wheel, because it is a turning on a road that used to be a mill road, and somebody cut it two hundred years before anybody thought there was anything to remember.

* * *

She does the Fridays and she does the Tuesdays when there is a Tuesday. There were a hundred and six entries in the first year and a hundred and nine in every year since, and there is a second person with her now, because a boy of nineteen from Corve had come to fix the lamp and stayed, and his name is Fenn Carrow and he can read perfectly well.

The eleven pounds and forty pence a month that came out of the smallholding at Cove Head went into the parish account and comes back out of it twice a year in the form of a cheque for the bulb and the bulb holder, and the cheque is made out to A. Rudd and has never once been queried.

The front section ran out on a Friday in the second February. She ruled a new one herself, forty lines to a page, and wrote the first line in it, and the first line is a thing and a date and where it was, and after it she wrote the word ROWS and then crossed it out because there was no room for a row count and nobody had been asking for one for ninety years.

* * *

A stone box on a verge with a wheel cut on it and three sets of initials is not a church and never was, and it will be found one day by somebody who reads the small print and takes it apart and cannot understand a word of it. The person who is holding it now is twenty-three and has a folder from a solicitor and forty lines to a page and no god in the arrangement at all, and the arrangement is the same arrangement it was the day Marda Quill took her turn at the box, and it goes on for as long as there is somebody in the county willing to keep the Friday.
