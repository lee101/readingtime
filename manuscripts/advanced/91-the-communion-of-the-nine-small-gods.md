# The Communion of the Nine Small Gods

![Cover art: The Communion of the Nine Small Gods](../art/advanced/91-the-communion-of-the-nine-small-gods.webp)

The notice was nailed to the door of the cathedral on a Tuesday in October, and it said that the cathedral of St Eudo would be deconsecrated on the fourth Sunday of Advent, and that the nine minor jurisdictions of the diocese would be consolidated into one at the same time, and that Dean Peter Ault, as registrar of the cathedral and custodian of the Book of Bounds, was the officer responsible for the carrying out of both.

Ault was fifty-four and had been dean for thirty years and had never believed in a god, and he was the only person in the diocese who was legally permitted to touch the Book of Bounds, because the Book is the cathedral's founding instrument and the dean holds it in cure, and because the ninth schedule of the Book lists the boundaries of nine gods and only a registrar may alter a schedule.

The Book ran to two hundred and eleven pages. The ninth schedule was a list of nine, and Ault could say all nine from memory in the order they were founded: ASH of the millrace, LOWWATER of the quay, PESTIS of the sealed room, EQUAL of the weighbeam, TARR of the bell, HOLLOWHEART of the orchard, LONGWATCH of the workhouse lodge, STONE of the boundary hedge, and GUTTER of the candle.

Eight of them had held their territory for a thousand years. The ninth had held it for nine hundred and forty, because the bell had been recast in 1606 and the god had gone into the new bell and had not come out of it, and the parish of St Tarr's-in-the-Fields had paid for the recasting and never missed a service, and the god had been unfashionable and small and entirely reliable for four centuries.

The cathedral itself was not impressive, which was in the schedule. It was a building of the fourteenth century with a west tower that leaned, a roof repaired eleven times, and a body that Fenwick would later certify in writing as honest. It had held nine jurisdictions for a thousand years without ever having had a god of its own, and nobody had found that odd, because a god of a territory is at home anywhere he is licensed, and the cathedral of St Eudo was licensed to nothing at all.

The resolution that consolidated the nine had been carried by the Consistory in the spring, by nine votes to none, and Ault had not been a member of the Consistory and had not been consulted, because a dean is a custodian of instruments and custodians are not asked for their view of the instruments. The Consistory's grounds were sound. Two vicars were short. The nine parish churches cost more to open than the diocese collected in the offertory, and Ault could not remember the last year he had been able to describe the arrangement as affordable.

* * *

The engineer's report came in from Ilse Fenwick in November, thirty-eight years old, chartered, six weeks on site, and it was a good report in the sense that nothing in it was wrong. It had a schedule of the fabric, a schedule of the defects, a schedule of the loads, and an appendix of everything in the building that was not the building.

The report had been commissioned because a building cannot be deconsecrated until somebody has certified in writing that it is safe to be a building with no god in it, and the Consistory had instructed that the survey be complete before the fourth Sunday of Advent, and Fenwick had written to the diocesan solicitor to say that she would not sign a deconsecration schedule for a building with an uninvestigated load in its north bay, and the solicitor had read it twice and asked her to itemise the objection.

She was good. She had done the roof of a hospital at Cardiff and a viaduct at Nantwich, and she came to a cathedral the way she came to a viaduct, with a hammer, a paint pen, and a notebook ruled down the margin, and she had found four faults in the lead work in eleven days and written to the cathedral about all four, and the dean had agreed with three and lost the fourth in a manner he found restful.

"Item fourteen," she said, in the Chapter house, with the page turned down. "Bell chamber, north bay. Additional load, forty-one kilograms, not scheduled, not lead, not iron, and cold. I have assumed it is a repair, a chair, a bell frame stay, or a lie, and I require to know before the scaffold goes up, because if it is not on your schedule and I have not found it, then somebody has been carrying something up there for a hundred years and not telling me."

Ault took the page. He had been in the north bay of that bell chamber at fourteen, and at nineteen, and at twenty-six, and in the last year of every decade since, and he knew the shape of the cold in that corner, and it was a cold with an opinion.

"There is no additional load," he said. "You have your answer and you may put it down as a repair."

"Dean." Fenwick did not move the page. "I am going to write additional load, unaccounted, and I am going to number it, and if you would rather I wrote something else you should tell me now, in front of the Chapter, and not afterwards when I am on a scaffold in February."

* * *

The Chapter minutes were kept in a press in the Chapter house and were read by nobody, and Ault had been the only member of the Chapter under sixty for eleven years, and had therefore been the only person alive who read them.

The minutes record that on the ninth of March, 1994, the Chapter received a communication from the god of the millrace, and that the god declined, in the terms set out in the Book of Bounds, at the third reading. The minutes record that on the fourteenth of November, 2003, the god of the quay was consulted as to a boundary and answered at low water only. The minutes record that in 2011 the god of the candle was asked whether a proposed extension of the graveyard would take its territory, and answered, in the hand of the Chapter's own minute-taker, to say that it would, and the extension was moved forty feet east and never mentioned again.

Nobody believes a minute. A believing person would be told that the Chapter of a diocese contains no god, and that these were the words of an old man being polite in Latin.

He knew precisely what he was, and he had known for a long time. He had set four empty years of a bishop's stall between himself and the building and gone on being a dean, and gone on being the man who settles a boundary when the boundary is disputed, which is work a person can do for a diocese that is crowded with gods and for a diocese that is empty of them.

The Book, at the end of the ninth schedule, carries a rubric in a seventeenth-century hand which says that where the candle burns, the god is told. It does not say what the god is told, because a rubric that has been read four hundred years by a man who does not believe it is a rubric that has been left alone.

In the study behind his house there was a candle stub on the writing desk, four inches, and Ault had put it there in his thirtieth year because the parish of St Eudo was the territory of GUTTER and because his mother had been ill in that year and had got better, and he had lit it, and he had prayed, and he had not believed a word of it, and he had kept it lit for thirty years and it had burned down to an inch and a half and he had never once put it out.

* * *

The councils met at the Chapter house in January, nine tables, nine chairs, and Peter Ault in the middle with a file, and he won, because he was right.

His case had four parts and it took him ninety minutes. The diocese could not support nine priests of the peculiar and was two vicars short. A god of a territory could not be made general without ceasing to be the god of anything, so consolidation would not promote nine gods, it would end eight of them and give the ninth a large address. The cathedral's heating, at ninety-one thousand pounds a year, cost more than the nine parishes had spent on their churches together in the last audited year. And the one jurisdiction that could be consolidated without loss was the one that already had no god in it, which was the orchard parish, whose apple tree had been felled by resolution of the parish council in 2014 and whose entry in the ninth schedule had been annotated PENDING since, because a schedule may not be annotated by a registrar and only a seat may be vacated by a registrar.

"Eleven minutes on the orchard," said the chair of the marsh parish council, a woman of sixty-one called Ruth Teague who had chaired for nine years and did the accounts herself. "You have had nine minutes on the orchard in front of this meeting, and I have not interrupted, because I have a list and the list says the apple tree went down in a storm with the rest of them and that is the end of it."

"The tree went down in a storm," Ault said. "The resolution that felled it is dated the ninth of June and it reads *health and safety*, and health and safety in June is a thing councils say, and I have never in thirty years met a council that felled an orchard in a storm in June for health and safety."

"Then what is the seat?"

"There is no seat," said Ault, "because the ninth schedule has carried the word PENDING against the orchard line since the Tuesday after the tree came down, and a registrar may not annotate a schedule and a registrar may not vacate a seat, and a seat cannot be vacated by a council, and I have been stuck behind my own penmanship for eleven years."

The theological objection came at the end of the hour, from the chair of the mill parish council, and she made it well, and Ault could not answer it.

"Dean, you are telling us that eight of these are going to stop. Not be forgotten, not be mourned, not be moved to a shelf. Stop. And you are telling us that on the authority of a form and a schedule, and a form and a schedule is precisely what a man like you would produce, because you are the man who makes the forms and keeps the schedules, and a person who has spent thirty years on the paperwork of a thing he does not believe in becomes very good at that paperwork." She closed her folder. "I am not saying you are lying. I have no reason to think you are lying. I am saying that I do not live where this is true, and I have to make my decisions in the place I do live in, and in this place there is a god of a millrace, and a resolution I passed, and a woman who rings the bell on a Wednesday whether anybody comes or not."

"And her bell-ringer is forty years of your money," said Ault.

"She is," said Ruth Teague. "And she is the only one of us who has never needed me to be right."

* * *

He went out that night with a theodolite, a plumb line, a tape, and a folding rule, and he did the whole diocese alone, in the dark, in six hours, because he had done most of it before and knew where the cold was.

Ash was in the millrace, at the third stone from the sluice, and the millrace had been culverted in 1951 and the water was under a car park, and Ash was under the car park. Lowwater was on the quay steps and was there at low water and no other time, and it had been there at four in the morning when he got to it, standing on the third step in the shape of a person. Equal was in the weighbeam at the cattle market and the beam was eleven ounces light, and had been since 1741, and the market had made allowances since 1790.

Pestis was in the sealed room above the vestry, in a locked chair, with a lock that had not been turned since the year it was put there, and Ault put his ear to the door and heard nothing, and that was new, because in 1994 he had heard it breathe.

And TARR of the bell was in the belfry, in the new bell of 1606, and was eleven years old, and had been bored for two hundred years and was delighted to have somebody.

He got the bell rope at two in the morning, and the top of that stair was four degrees colder than the theodolite had said it would be, and the north bay was where the report said it was, and there was nothing in the north bay, and the cold came out of the bell, and it was the ordinary cold of a bell in January and it was also somebody standing in a bell looking at him.

"You are the registrar," said Tarr.

"I am the dean. There has not been a registrar of this cathedral since 1888, the post was abolished, and I have kept the title because the Book does."

"Then you are the one who does the forms." The god was sitting inside the sound with its arms round its knees. "I do the forms. Every year, since I got in here, one of the deans comes up with a form, and I sign it, and nobody reads it, and then a year later a different one comes up." It said this without any complaint whatever, in the voice of a child reporting the weather. "I would like a different form. I have been in this bell since 1606. I am eleven. Have you got a form with something else on it?"

"There is a form with nothing on it," said Ault, and got the Book out on the bell frame and put a knee against the frame, which took his weight the way a bell frame takes weight, and Tarr read the ninth schedule upside down for a while and then observed that PESTIS was not at the millrace, which was correct, and that GUTTER was in the candle, which was also correct, and then asked whether anybody was ever going to come up and look at the bell, and Ault said that he would come on Sunday and bring the Chapter.

"You will bring the Chapter," said Tarr, "and they will want to know my age."

"They will want to know whether you have a parish."

"I have got a parish," said Tarr. "It is the only thing I have got. The parish is not the bell, Dean. The bell is only where I am."

"There are nine of you," Ault said, on the step with the report, and the Chapter house was full of chairs and coats and a fire that had gone out, and every chair in it was occupied by a parish councillor who had come to be told that their god was to be merged into a larger one. "I am going to say this once and you are going to tell me I am a man who does not believe in you, and you will be right about your own reasons for saying so, and I am not asking any of you to change your mind, and I am asking you to hold your noise for nine minutes."

Nobody made a noise. The chair of the mill parish council had a hand over her mouth, not from belief, and had it there so as not to be rude to a man on the floor.

"They are not a metaphor," said Peter Ault, dean of a diocese that had not believed him for thirty years, to nine parish councils and a Chapter fire that had gone out. "They are located. Each of them has a place, and a competence, and a boundary drawn by a deed in this Book, and the one in the bell is eleven years old and has been bored since before Shakespeare and would very much like to know what is happening. Where a territory is extinguished, the person is not made smaller. The person is discharged." He put the Book on the table with the ninth schedule facing up, and the room looked at it, and it is only paper. "I have been registrar of this diocese for thirty years and I have never once asked a council to believe a word of me. I am asking you to read a schedule, and to notice that one of the nine lines has been PENDING for eleven years, and to ask yourselves who put it there."

* * *

The resolution was carried in the Chapter house in March, and it is a short document, and the instrument behind it is a form that has been in the statute books since 1611 and has never been used, and Ault had found the form himself, in a drawer, in the second year of his deanship, and had not shown it to anybody.

A god of the ninth order: a parish, no cathedral, no diocese, no seat in the Chapter, one building, small, listed, kept. Not a cathedral and not a see and not a jurisdiction of the second or third order, but a god with a boundary, a competence, a seat, and a building that the parish is obliged by the listing to maintain and nobody is obliged to fill.

Nine of those. A belfry. A weighbeam house with a beam in it. A sealed room with a chair in it. A workhouse lodge. A boundary cottage. A mill store. A vestry, a quay shed, and a room over the Chapter house with a candle in it.

It cost one page of costing, and the total was sixty-eight thousand pounds, and the cathedral's heating had been ninety-one thousand the year before, and Ault put the two figures side by side on the wall of the Chapter house on a Thursday and left them there, and the nine parish councils came and looked at them and one of them laughed.

The one objection the councils had left was money, and it was a real objection, and Ault answered it with the listing rather than with an argument. A building in the parish of a god of the ninth order is a place of worship, and a place of worship in a listed building is repaired out of a fund that does not exist for ordinary property, and the parish itself contributes nothing, which was the thing the chair of the marsh council had wanted and had not believed she would get. Ruth Teague read the schedule of the fund twice at the second reading and asked one question, which was whether the candle was included, and Ault said that the candle was included, and that it was the cheapest listed building in the diocese, and that it had a fire risk rating of one, and that he had looked into it.

* * *

On the fourth Sunday of Advent the cathedral was deconsecrated, and the one god of the whole diocese was seated at the crossing by a man from the Consistory who read a form, and the Book of Bounds was carried out of the building in a tin box by two clerks, and the book has never been opened by anybody who was not the dean, and the dean was fifty-four and had done it properly.

The old lectern was in the boiler room, because the boiler room was the only place with a door that locked, and the new god had no use for a fourth-century oak lectern with a two-headed bird on it, and so it stood against the brick with a folded hymn sheet under one leg to keep the foot steady.

It was exactly eleven o'clock, and the boiler was off, because the boiler served a building that no longer had a congregation, and the pipes were cold, and Peter Ault sat down on the step below the lectern with his back against the brick, the way he had sat every night for thirty years, and the cathedral was dark, and the candle in the room upstairs was four inches and burning, and he was there.
