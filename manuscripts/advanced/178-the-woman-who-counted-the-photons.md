# The Woman Who Counted the Photons

![Cover art: The Woman Who Counted the Photons](../art/advanced/178-the-woman-who-counted-the-photons.webp)

Marama Ngata counts photons one at a time, which in March at Siding Spring means sitting in a dark room with a red lamp strapped to her left wrist and a black instrument box on the table in front of her that she talks to in numbers. In the box is a glass tube the length of a pencil. Light from a star comes down a telescope, through a glass filter, onto the flat end of that tube, and every so often a photon lands on the coating at that end and knocks one electron loose. The electron goes down the tube and gets multiplied a hundred million times on the way, and arrives at the wire as a pulse of current you can measure. One photon, one pulse. Marama counts the pulses.

The count she is keeping tonight is the count for a red dwarf called WX Microscopii that has a planet candidate around it that somebody else found. The star is fourteen and a bit on the magnitude scale, which means its photons arrive at the top of the atmosphere at a rate she could work out on the back of a hand, and then the number you actually get in the tube is that number times the area of the mirror times the fraction that survives the mirrors, the air and the glass.

The arithmetic is not hard and it is not forgiving. A zero-magnitude star drops about eight million photons a second on a one-metre mirror. WX Microscopii is fourteen point two, and every magnitude is a factor of two and a half in brightness, so you take away fourteen point two times that factor and the star is about two million times fainter than a zero-magnitude star. Eight million divided by two million is about four photons a second arriving at the mirror, and about one in five of those gets all the way into the tube and out of the other end as a countable pulse.

So. Three photons a second. That is what she has been sitting with since eight o'clock.

The candidate planet was announced in March of last year from survey photometry. The signal was a dip of four tenths of one per cent in the light, repeating every two days and about five hours. That is a small planet, or a large one, or nothing at all. Four tenths of a per cent is the size of dip that a group of starspots rotating into view will make on a red dwarf, and red dwarfs are covered in starspots, so the announcement came with the sentence that every such announcement comes with, which is that ground-based follow-up is required.

Marama's job is the ground-based follow-up. Her job is to measure that dip well enough that it is a dip and not nothing, and one part in three is what that takes.

To do that she has to count enough photons, and here is the rule that governs her entire working life. If you count a hundred photons you know the number to about ten, because the photons do not arrive in a tidy fashion. They arrive at random. A thousand photons and you know the number to about thirty. Ten thousand and you know it to a hundred. To get the number to one part in a thousand you must count a million. To get it to one part in seven hundred and thirty you must count half a million.

One part in seven hundred and thirty is what four tenths of one per cent wants, because four tenths of one per cent divided by three is about fourteen hundredths of one per cent, and fourteen hundredths of one per cent is one part in seven hundred and thirty.

She needs the ratio of the target to the comparison star to that. That means half a million photons off WX Microscopii and half a million off the comparison.

She is getting three a second off the target and thirty-six a second off the comparison. Half a million photons at three a second is one hundred and seventy-seven thousand seconds, which is forty-nine hours of straight counting on the target alone, and forty-nine hours is two days and two nights, and she has four nights of allocation that are not contiguous.

Four nights. Three hours and four minutes of transit on each, because the transit of a two-day planet comes round every two days and five hours and only crosses the meridian in the small hours, when the star is high and the sky is black. Four nights gives her twelve hours and sixteen minutes of the object being in front of her. At three a second that is one hundred and thirty-two thousand photons on the target, and one and a half million on the comparison.

One hundred and thirty-two thousand is short of half a million by a factor of four.

She has known this since the proposal went in. She wrote the number in the proposal and underlined it, and the referee wrote back and said the number was correct and asked whether she had considered the three-point-nine-metre, and she had, and in November she put a request for two and a half hours on it and the allocation came back in February with the time granted and a note about the slit.

The three-point-nine has fifteen times the area of her one-metre, so fifteen times the photons at the same integration. Two and a half hours on the three-point-nine is nine thousand seconds, which at three a second and fifteen times the area is four hundred and five thousand photons, and the slit takes a tenth off that before it gets to the star at all.

Four hundred and five thousand is better than a night of her own. It is short of half a million.

The bigger telescope does not help her enough, because what she has run out of is time and not light, and time on the three-point-nine is scarcer than time on the one-metre. She wrote that back. The reply thanked her for the observation.

* * *

The second night is the one she loses, and she loses it to nothing anybody could have forecast.

The forecast was clear. The forecast was clear for all four nights, which is why she took all four. At two in the morning on the second night a band of thin high cloud came over the ridge from the west and by half past it was sitting over the observatory and it did not look like anything on the forecast, because it was too thin to look like anything. Through it the stars did not dim. Through it the sky stayed black. But the star stopped being fourteen point two and became fourteen point three and a bit, and it did so by about a per cent, and it did so differently from the comparison star because the comparison star is a different colour, and because at one and a fifth airmass the thin cloud is not neutral.

One per cent is twenty-five times the dip. A signal of four tenths of one per cent sitting on a one per cent cloud drift is a signal nobody can find.

She could take the night anyway and let the comparison star take the cloud out. That is what the comparison star is for, and on a night of high thin cloud it mostly works. It did not work, because the cloud was drifting through the atmosphere and the two stars were nine arcminutes apart, and nine arcminutes at the height of the cloud is not nine arcminutes of cloud. The target was under the thin part and the comparison was under the thicker part, and a comparison star cannot correct for a difference it cannot see.

She got four hours and eleven minutes out of that night and threw all of it away at four in the morning. Writing the night's file name and the reason on the back of the log in the observatory's standard format, which is one word for cloud and the date, and she has used that word eleven times in fifteen years and has never once liked it.

That leaves three nights. Three nights gives her nine hours and twelve minutes, which is ninety-nine thousand photons, which is one part in three hundred and fifteen, which is a dip of four tenths of one per cent with an uncertainty of about a third of one per cent, which is not a dip.

* * *

The first problem is the sky, and the sky has to be subtracted, and the sky is made of photons too, so the subtraction has its own error in it.

Siding Spring on a moonless night in March has a sky of about twenty-two magnitudes per square arcsecond in the visible band. That number is the sky's brightness, and brightness in this work is a count rate like any other, so the sky is putting photons into the same tube at the same time as the star is. She measures the sky separately, from patches of the same field with no star in them, and subtracts, and the count she is left with is the star's.

But the sky she subtracted was not the sky that was there while she counted the star. Over three hours the sky at a good site changes by a few per cent, and it changes because the temperature of the air above the telescope changes, and because there is a band of high cloud two hundred kilometres east of the observatory that gets smaller or larger.

That is fixable with a comparison star, and the comparison star is a K dwarf nine arcminutes away in the same field, and the comparison star is measured on the same mount through the same filter at the same time, so almost everything that happens to the sky happens to both of them and cancels in the ratio.

Almost everything. The comparison star is nine hundred times brighter than the target, and it is a K dwarf and the target is an M dwarf, and the light of an M dwarf is redder than the light of a K dwarf, and the air between here and there is not neutral. It takes about a tenth of a magnitude out of a red star for every extra unit of airmass it passes through and takes rather less out of a yellow one. Over her three hours the airmass goes from one and a fifth down to one and a tenth and back out to one and a half, a swing of about four tenths, and that swing turns into four thousandths of a magnitude of colour term, and four thousandths of a magnitude is four tenths of one per cent of flux.

Four tenths of one per cent. The size of the dip she is trying to measure.

She can correct it, because the size of the colour term is a known function of how much redder the target is than the comparison, and the target's colour is in the survey data. She corrects it. What is left after the correction is a residual of about three hundredths of a per cent, and three hundredths of a per cent comes out of the difference between an M dwarf and a K dwarf and how far the two wavelengths at which you can measure a colour term are apart.

It is not a mistake. It is the floor. She has spent her career learning that floors are not mistakes, and she has learned that you can spend three nights and a telescope and eleven years of somebody else's education discovering one, and then spend the next night writing it down so that the next person does not spend three nights.

* * *

The third night is the one that saves the observation, or most of it, and it does it by accident, in the last hour, in the difference between the target out of transit on one night and the target out of transit on another.

She is not looking for it. She is checking that the comparison star has not gone off, because comparison stars do sometimes go off, and a comparison star that has gone off by four tenths of a per cent produces a dip of four tenths of a per cent, and she has checked it two hundred times before. So she plots the target's own light against the comparison star for all three nights and lays the three traces on top of each other, and they do not lie on top of each other.

They undulate. They go up and down by about half a per cent with a period that she can read straight off the plot, because the three nights are spread over a week and the period is a bit under two days, and the third night lands where the first two nights say it should and not where the first night alone says it should.

The star is rotating. It has a starspot group on it, the spot group comes round every one point nine four days, and as it comes round it takes between zero and a half of one per cent off the light. Half a per cent is larger than the planet. The planet candidate is four tenths of a per cent. The starspot is half a per cent, and the starspot has a known period and the planet candidate has a different one.

Two point one nine two days for the planet. One point nine four days for the star. If the dip were a starspot crossing it would come round every one point nine four days, or every three point eight eight days if the spot went round twice, and it comes round every two point one nine two days.

Two point one nine two is not one point nine four. Two point one nine two is not three point eight eight either. It is one and a bit of one point nine four, which is not a whole number of turns, and a spot cannot cross the star in one and a bit of a turn.

She writes it on the back page of the notebook. She writes it twice. She reads the referee's letter from March of last year, which had said the signal was not a harmonic of any published rotation period for the star, and she sees that the referee had said published, and that there was no published rotation period for this star, and that she has just measured one to a third of a per cent with three nights of photometry she was not using for rotation.

The signal is not a spot crossing. The signal repeats every two point one nine two days and the star turns every one point nine four, and those two numbers cannot both be about the same thing.

* * *

The paper comes off the table in August.

She did not take it off. The author of the announcement, who is at a university in the north of England and who has been nicer about every email than she had any right to expect, withdrew it. The reason given in the withdrawal is the size of the uncertainty on the transit depth, which is correct, and which is thirty-two hundredths of a per cent on a dip of forty-one hundredths of a per cent, and which is the reason.

The depth she can report is zero point four one per cent, plus or minus zero point three two. The number has to go into the paper as a null result, because zero point four one plus or minus zero point three two is not the same as zero point four four, and the difference between those two is the difference between a planet and a planet slightly smaller than a planet. It is not enough to say there is something there. It is only enough to say she has not found out what.

She keeps her three nights. They are hers, they were allocated to her, and she spends two of them again in September on a star with no planet candidate, because the instrument is the same instrument and the technique is the technique and she is the only person at that telescope who can run it at night now. The student she trained in 2031 runs it for the person who took the paper, and the paper gets a null result in it too, and her name is on that paper and not on the one that would have had a planet in it.

The last page of the notebook has the arithmetic on it in her own hand, because she writes things down when she cannot make them come out right. Half a million photons needed on the target. Ninety-nine thousand counted. Three a second against thirty-six a second. One and a third of a per cent of light gone to high cloud. One point nine four days. Two point one nine two days. Zero point four one, plus or minus zero point three two.

None of those numbers agrees with any of the others, and every one of them is right, and she has not found the mistake in any of them because there is no mistake in any of them.
