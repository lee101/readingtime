# The War of the Under-Servers

![Cover art: The War of the Under-Servers](../art/advanced/83-the-war-of-the-under-servers.webp)

Under Marrowgate there was a second city of nine hundred thousand people, and it had run the first one for four hundred years on a promise that had never once been written down.

Every main above the twelfth level belonged to the people underneath it. They patched it, sleeved it, argued about it in shifts, and sent the bill up. The surface paid through an office called the Ground Levy Office, and the money came down the Stile Street shaft in a sealed iron box with two locks, and nobody below the fourth level had been allowed near it.

Wren Addo kept the run-board on the fourteenth level. He was nineteen. His grandmother had kept the seventh, his mother the thirteenth, and the name Addo sat on the depth roll in Wren’s own hand, which meant something underground and nothing at all on a street.

The board was four metres of slate ruled in chalk. The rightmost column was headed SURFACE REF, and it was the only column nobody below the fourth level could read. When a job needed the surface, somebody wrote a number there, and it went up in a runner’s mouth, and the answer came back down in a runner’s mouth, and the board never learned what it said.

“That is the war,” Wren said, and wiped the chalk from his palms. “Four columns and a number, and the number has not meant anything since the sixteenth year.”

The payments stopped without anybody meaning them to be permanent. The notice was one printed page referring to an interim suspension of scheduled ground support pending review, and the review date was not mentioned again in any document Wren had handled. The answer from below, drawn up at an assembly in the Kiln Hall and called the Notice of Self-Management, said that the undercroft would govern the undercroft. It did not say that the undercroft would leave, and that omission was why the surface went on treating it all as maintenance.

“They have been fighting this for eleven years,” Wren said. “They have never once been below the fourth level. Their insurance forbids it and their forms do not exist down here.”

“They are not fighting anything,” Tallow said. “They are fixing a pipe. That is the entire reason they win.”

She was fifty, had been in the war thirty of its forty years, and had started it by refusing to reopen a certified seam. She set the file on the slate, which was the only table in the room.

“I told the Kiln Hall in the sixth year that we would hold the Stile Street shaft by the twelfth,” she said. “I have been losing since the fifteenth. I look for the week every year and I have never found it, and that is the part I cannot explain to nine hundred thousand people.”

“Then we are not losing the war,” Wren said. “We are losing the schedule.”

Tallow looked at the SURFACE REF column for a while. “Yes,” she said. “That is worse, and I would rather not have said it in front of the level.”

* * *

On the surface, the Fourth Works kept a depot on a street where nothing much happened, and the depot kept a day’s sheet, and the day’s sheet governed the entire city as far as the water was concerned.

Marek Drasch was forty-four, a regional engineer, and he read the sheet the way other men read a newspaper. At four in the morning he signed out a party of five, a lift, a marshal, and a permit for the manhole at Twenty-One, and he carried his own wrench, because the sheet said the stores opened at six and he never trusted a store that had not yet been opened.

Down the Stile Street shaft in the cage, past the fourth level, the air changed: not the heat, but the texture of it, and the smell, which by the twelfth was a thousand people breathing in one volume. His helmet lamp found a face on a landing looking up at him, and the face did not move, and Marek understood that it was not startled but confirming an item.

They worked six hours on the main at Twenty-One, a re-seat rescheduled twice, and were three-quarters through the clamp when a runner came down the ramp with a lamp and a length of three-inch pipe and said that the level was closed for an inspection.

Marek asked, quite reasonably, by what authority. The runner turned the lamp so the light was on him rather than on Marek, and said the authority was clause four of the Notice of Self-Management, and that Marek could be read to at the Board, or he could come now.

So Marek Drasch went to the twenty-second level with his party and their instruments and their crate, and it was in that crate that the whole next eleven days came to happen.

* * *

The room they gave him was warm, and dry, and lit, and it had a bed in it, and a table, and a chair, and a window.

Marek’s first act in it was to go to the window. It was a wired pane in poured concrete, and it looked up a service shaft, and eleven levels above the shaft there was a lit street with a woman walking a dog. He put his thumb on the glass, and the pane was cold, and the street was the one immediately above the shaft he had come down.

The second act was to sit on the bed and understand that nobody was coming to hit him.

The guards were Colm Dree and Benda Sisk, and they came in twice with tea. Not a mug brought to be drunk quickly and taken away, but a pot, and milk in a lidded jug, and a cup. Benda asked how he took it, and Marek said, “However it is usually taken here,” and Benda said, “That is the one thing we do not have,” and set the jug down and waited.

They offered it both times, sincerely, and they asked him nothing. In two days he understood that they had no practice at this, that neither of them was cruel, and that neither had ever spoken to a person from the surface in their lives. They told him about the weather, which they took from the window.

“They are going to think I am a spy,” Marek said.

“They are going to think you are a spy,” Colm said. “I would think you were a spy.”

On the second afternoon Benda came in with a ledger, because there was nothing else to bring, and set it on the table. It was a level account in the chalk-and-ink hand of the undercroft: water drawn, copper recovered, shifts worked, complaints received from above and answers sent, and at the foot of every page the number of people living on the level.

“They cannot read it,” Benda said. “Nobody up there can read a word of it. We thought you might like to.”

* * *

On the third day the Board sent a drain-runner to take his inventory, because the inventory had to be taken, and there was a form.

The form was one page, printed underground, badly, from a plate that had been re-cut twice. It asked for name, trade, registration, unit, purpose, instruments, papers, and a final line which read: IS THIS PERSON A SPY. Wren Addo read it aloud, in order, in a level voice, and then waited with the pen poised while the man answered every question, correctly, without a flicker.

“You are not a spy,” Wren said.

“No,” Marek said.

“You are an engineer.”

“Yes.”

“Then you know what is in that crate, and it is sitting against a wall on the fourth as evidence.” Wren set the pen down. “I am going to tell you what I am going to do with you, and then I am going to stand in a corridor, and you are not going to be told why.”

They let him have the window. The days after that were the longest of Marek’s life and the least eventful: he read Benda’s ledger, asked for paper, worked the level’s water balance out to three figures, and found that the fourteenth level was running at four hours of head pressure instead of seven, which killed people slowly on the seventeenth.

Wren put his report on the slate at the change of shift. Tallow read it standing.

“Level Fourteen at four hours,” she said. “Since when.”

“Since before the war, by the look of the iron. Thirty years of never being repaired properly, because the money went up and came back as a number in a column.” Wren kept his voice down. “He is not a spy. He is the most dangerous thing anybody has sent down here in eleven years, and the only person in this war who knows the fourteenth level is being starved.”

“Then he is a nuisance,” Tallow said.

“He is a schedule,” Wren said. “You told me we were losing the schedule.”

* * *

The Fourth Works did not know it was at war. It had no war room, no intelligence, no historian, and no appetite for any of the three.

Ilse Crome was forty-one and deputy to the regional engineer, and she held the day’s sheet in a hand as small and square as a chess problem. She had inherited a backlog, not a conflict.

“We are not at war with anybody,” she said. “We are at nine hundred broken mains, four hundred deferred reclamations, and a schedule that has not advanced by one day in eleven years. Everything above the fourth level is on the sheet. Everything below it is not, and I cannot send a crew to fix a thing I am not permitted to measure.”

“Ma’am, they cut the Stile Street feed in the third year.”

“They did. That is an outage on the sheet, caused by a third party we have no standing to name.” She squared the sheet against the desk. “We keep winning because we can afford to stop. They have to be back down there in four hours or three hundred families have no water, and after four hours nobody writes anything down.”

The clerk at the end of the counter kept the Ground Levy account, because nobody senior would take it. In the twelfth year of the suspension the sealed box had been sent down once, empty, with a note reading RETURNED EMPTY — NOTHING TO DISBURSE, and a runner had carried it down and come back and said the box was full of brass, and that he had seen a man down there crying. Nobody believed him. Runners from the deep had been known to say worse things for a meal.

* * *

On the eighth day the main on the Ninth let go at twenty past two in the morning, and by four in the afternoon the water was at the ceiling of the board room on the fourteenth.

The work was ordinary work, and the Board did it the way the Board always did it. The new main could not be cut in under head pressure, and the head pressure came from the Stile Street feed. So the first item on the sheet, in Wren’s chalk, was: DROP THE STILE STREET BLEED. Below it: BLEED IS A TWO-HANDED VALVE. BELOW REQUIRES A HAND. ABOVE REQUIRES A HAND.

“It is bolted through the shaft wall,” Merrit Vahl said. “One hand at the bottom of the wheel, one at the top. The one above us is theirs.”

“The fourth level is ours,” Tallow said. “The eleventh is not. If we send a party up the Stile Street stair with a spanner we will be shot at by our own municipal guard inside four minutes.” She read the board again. “So we shut the feed, and we lose the upper city until the deep is strong enough to win something.”

“That is your answer.”

“That is my answer, and I will defend it at the assembly, and it will be the wrong one.” Tallow put the file down. “Go and get the collar.”

The cast collar had been in the deep’s foundry since the sixth year, sixty-one centimetres of grey iron, tapped and faced, the only one in the world, and Wren and two runners carried it up in a canvas sling on a pole, because nobody below the fourth level had ever been allowed to carry a part across the boundary.

“You are taking it to the surface,” Merrit said.

“No. I am taking it to the top of the Stile Street stair and standing there with it, because a collar is half a repair, they have the other half on a wall on the fourth, and there is a man in a room on the twenty-second who has read that crate’s parts list.”

“He is a prisoner.”

“He is a prisoner,” Tallow said, “and he is the only individual on either side of this shaft who can read both walls, and this is not a negotiation, Addo, it is a repair.” She paused. “Open his door. Not as a favour. Write it in the minutes as a work party under Board authority, with two witnesses, and I will sign it, so that nobody can ever say we did anything else.”

* * *

They met at the head of the Stile Street stair on the eleventh day, at twenty past nine in the morning, and neither of them could get past the other.

Wren had the collar on a pole, forty pounds of awkward iron. Marek had the upper half of the assembly in a canvas bag across his back: the gland, machined at the Fourth Works, eleven bolts in a tin, and the drawing roll. Below them the fourteenth was four hours of pressure over a drowned board room.

“All right,” Marek said. “Eleven minutes.”

“All right,” Wren said. “Ask.”

“I am not going to waste the eleven minutes on why there is a man in a room on the twenty-second instead of a room on the ninth.”

“You are on the ninth in your own account. I have read it. Level Fourteen, four hours of head, thirty years of iron.” Wren shifted the pole. “You filed a complaint about the twenty-first and they rescheduled you twice. That is on my board. I have been reading your writing for six years and could never read a word of it until last Tuesday.”

Marek almost laughed. “Then you are one of three people, and I am one of three, and the two of us have wasted a great deal of a city’s time being unreadable to each other.”

“Tell me the number. The head pressure we have to hold while the collar goes in.”

“Three hours and forty minutes. Below that the joint takes the load and the bolts shear. Do not let anybody tell you four.”

“Why three-forty.”

“Because the pipe on your fourth level has moved four centimetres in eleven years and is still moving, and if it moves while the joint is open you get a shear and not a repair, and a shear on that main takes the twelfth level off its water for six weeks.” He shifted the bag. “The only thing either of us has done is arrive at a wall where the other wrote the arithmetic in a hand we cannot read, and leave.”

Wren looked at the collar on the pole. “The valve is on the wall behind you.”

“Above that wheel is the eleventh level. Below it is yours. It is bolted through a wall both your government and mine claim, and in nineteen eighty-nine a man named Pettigrew wrote the maintenance number for it in the trade notation, because he was tired of people guessing.” Marek nodded at the wall. “That is Pettigrew’s hand. You can read it.”

Wren read it. He put his shoulder under the pole and went across to the wall.

They had four minutes to do the arithmetic and four minutes left, and the arithmetic was a spindle and a gland and a collar, the order of assembly, the order of the bolts, eleven minutes for eleven bolts, said aloud in the shorthand they had each taught themselves for nothing.

“When this is done,” Marek said, “it is repaired for a season. Perhaps two. And then on a Tuesday in your city, or mine, a man in an office will sign a form.”

“Yes.”

“And there is no treaty.”

“There has never been a treaty,” Wren said. “There was a habit, for four hundred years, and the habit was not written down, so neither side can produce the one document that would end this, and both of us are entirely right. That is why it has gone on for eleven years.”

“After the form is signed, what happens.”

“The wardens will meet.”

“The wardens will meet, and each will bring an engineer, and the engineers will agree about the pipe, and the wardens will go home, and the pipe will be fixed for another season.”

Wren looked at him. “That is the whole war. That is what it has been the whole time.”

“Nine minutes left,” Marek said. “And when the water goes down on the fourteenth, what do we tell the assembly?”

“The truth. That it was repaired by one man from each side, in a stairwell, in eleven minutes, because both of them had bothered to learn the other one’s handwriting.” Marek unrolled the drawing on the wall. “And then the other true thing, which is that we still have not agreed on the schedule, and the next time the Ninth lets go we will be back in this stair arguing about the date.”

“Then it is not over.”

“It was never over,” Marek said. “It was only, for a moment, legible.”

The bleed valve was four feet across and had last been painted in nineteen thirty-one, and it would not move at all unless the pressure on each side of the wall was within a few hours of the same, which was the entire design and the whole reason the city had welded two halves of a machine to two halves of a country.

“Ready,” Wren said.

“Ready,” Marek said.

They put their hands on it together, and the wheel did not go for two seconds, and then it went, half a turn, and a sound came up out of the shaft that was not loud, and on the fourteenth a man with a lamp watched the water come down off the ceiling and said a word that the Board did not enter in the minutes.

Below them the repair party set the collar. Above them, the box that had not been opened in eleven years was still in the Ground Levy Office, and would be opened one day by somebody who would not tell anybody what was in it. The water on the fourteenth was going down at three hours and thirty-nine minutes of head, which was a number, and a number was the only treaty either side had ever signed.
