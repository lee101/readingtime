# The Boy Who Inherited a Language Nobody Spoke

![Cover art: The Boy Who Inherited a Language Nobody Spoke](../art/advanced/75-the-boy-who-inherited-a-language-nobody-spoke.webp)

The Commission for the Public Tongue kept its files on the second floor of the Weighhouse, in a room that had been a scales office and still smelled faintly of the weights. On the wall hung a schedule of speakers, one column, forty-one names. Forty were struck through in the same blue ink, with dates running from 1741 to the present, and the last name had no line through it. OSVAUNT, ILMA. Aged 81. Household. Speaker.

The file said the language died when its last speaker died, and that a language was a public asset, and that the Commission had held a reserve against the event since 1889 and spent nothing from it. Councillor Iven, who had chaired it for six of those years, put the instrument on the table with the signature block facing the boy.

“Instrument Nine-C,” he said. “Custodianship of a Public Tongue. You are named sole custodian, which means nothing is taken from your grandmother, and that we may not begin the work without you, and that the work is a teaching programme with a budget and a date.”

Tev read the block. The signature line was already printed with a name in the form the Commission used, and he had to write his own underneath, and he wrote it crooked, and the clerk, Sarel, blotted it and did not comment. His grandmother was in the waiting room with her coat on, because nobody had told her there would be a waiting room.

“Three hundred and forty crowns,” said Councillor Iven. “Thirty-four months. A teaching manual, a conversational certification at level four, and an assessment file. Fourteen children were taught this way in the last hundred years. Twelve finished. Two of the twelve are remembered, which I consider a good rate for any public expenditure.”

Tev looked at the schedule on the wall, at his grandmother’s name, at the blue lines through the forty others, and then at the clerk, the only other person in the room who had not come prepared.

“A language is not a thing you can be custodian of,” he said. “It is a thing you live in.”

“That is a very good distinction,” said Councillor Iven, “and it has never once stopped us doing the work, and I would rather you said it here than in front of the Commission in nine months.”

* * *

The instructor arrived on the second Tuesday, in the wet, carrying a case of papers and wearing the same coat she had worn on the first Tuesday, which the clerk mentioned. Her name was Vessa Renn; she was twenty-six, she had finished three of these appointments, and two of them had been for languages with living speakers, which was the difficult kind.

She unpacked her case in the room the Commission had given her, with a stove and one window facing the yard: a household ledger, a bundle of land records, a cookbook with a grandmother’s writing in the margins, eleven sheets of parish notes, and one sheet of a ship’s manifest from a port that no longer existed.

“Forty-one documents,” she said. “Everything with a line of it in that language that anybody has ever put on paper. I read all of them in four years. I can give you a grammar, a word list of nine hundred and forty items, and a pronunciation key, and none of the three describes how anybody actually talks. I have known that since the second year and I have been paid since the first.”

Tev sat on the windowsill and gave her nothing for nine minutes. She let them run, because she had read a report on a boy in the Kalder valley who answered on the fourth, and she had been told not to trust reports about patience from adults.

Then she turned a page of the cookbook round to face him and asked him to say what was written at the top. He leaned in and read it, and she wrote in her log that he could not read a word of it and had not needed to, because it was a recipe for something his grandmother made in a particular month, and he had got there without moving his mouth.

“Item six,” she said. “Item six is a list of numbers. Say the numbers.”

He said them. They were in it, quickly, in a form she had not heard before and could not get a pen to record.

“Item twenty-one is a prayer. Say it.”

“That is a prayer,” he said, “and you have said it wrong, and it is not for the kitchen.”

She wrote that down too. At the end of the hour she read her own log back to him, in order, without comment, and the last line said: candidate produced fluent material in a domestic register not represented in any of the forty-one source documents, and the probable source is the household, and I do not have the household.

* * *

Joren was the only person in that school who thought any of it was unusual, and he thought it was unusual for four months, and then he stopped, and the stopping was the part Tev could not get over.

He said it was cool, in the way you say a thing is cool when it makes you the only person in a room who knows something. He asked Tev to say a word, any word, at lunch, and Tev said no, and Joren accepted that, and for a while it was the best kind of secret, a thing that was yours and did not have to be shown.

Then in the autumn Joren asked a real question, at the yard tap, without any preparation. “When your gran goes,” he said, “is it gone?”

“No,” said Tev.

He had not meant to say it. It came out of him the way the numbers had come out of him in Item six, before the part of him that was listening could get hold of it, and Joren looked at him, and Tev heard the sentence afterwards and understood that he had said the opposite of what the Commission said, in front of a witness, and that Joren would repeat it and not remember saying it.

Joren repeated it, to a girl called Nell, in the corridor, in November, and Tev heard her, and Joren saw him hear her. There was no scene. Over the following weeks Joren began knocking on the kitchen door before he came round, and stopped asking Tev to come to the pump, and in February he asked, at the tap, quite gently, whether it was all right if he said his own name first.

Tev said it was fine. Joren said that was a relief, because he had not been sure, and went to play, and it was the kindest thing anybody did for Tev that year and it was the end of the best thing anybody had ever had with him.

* * *

Vessa Renn went to the Foundling kitchen on a Sunday in the second year, because her log said the probable source was the household, and a professional visits a probable source.

Ilma Osaunt was eighty-one and small and had been a signalwoman at the harbour for fifty-four years, and she made tea without being asked and set the cup down in a way that Tev read instantly and explained to himself as politeness. The teacher asked her, gently, to speak the language. The grandmother said she would not, and gave a reason Vessa wrote down in full because it turned out later to be load-bearing: I have nothing to say in it that I have not already said.

Afterwards the teacher sat on the step with her notebook while the boy washed, and she made herself go back over the four years, because she had been troubled for some time and had not admitted the trouble, and now there was a reason to look at it.

She had been living in the two rooms above the Weighhouse since her first appointment four years ago, the same building the boy and his grandmother passed every Tuesday and Thursday on the way to the fish market, with a window that looked down on the yard. Four years, three mornings a week, an old woman and a boy going past under it, and two voices, and Vessa had filed them in her own margins the way a linguist files a thing she cannot place.

She had written them down as a family code. She had written them down as a pidgin. In the third year, in a note she never showed the Commission, she had written that she believed she was hearing her own language spoken badly by a family that had lost it, which was the one explanation available to a woman with four years of a grammar and no household.

And then the boy had said the numbers at her, in the schoolroom, in a register with no verbs in the place her grammar had put verbs, and the register was not an old form. It was Tuesday.

* * *

The Commission required an interim finding in the fourteenth month, it would be read aloud, and she wrote it in eleven drafts.

The examiner came from the provincial office, a courteous man of about fifty with a good eye, and sat at the schoolroom table and put the level four test in front of the boy and asked him to begin. The boy looked at the page, put it face down, said nothing at all for a long time, and then said, in it, “no,” which was not the word no.

She ruled against her own recommendation on the day. She certified the boy at level one, the lowest grade the Commission could record, on a form that took eleven minutes to complete and that the boy watched her complete, and the form said the candidate’s fluency could not be assessed, that the instructor declined to certify a level, and that a refusal of certification was a valid outcome under Article 12.

Then she filed the interim finding, seven pages, and the seventh said what she had been carrying since the Sunday in the kitchen: that the boy was a speaker of the language as it was used in his own household; that the forty-one documents were a domestic register catalogued by her as a literary one; that her manual described a form of the language not spoken in this city for two hundred years; that the Commission’s premise, that the boy might be the last person able to transmit a dying tongue, was unsupported; and that the likely position was that the language was not dying, and that she could be wrong, and that if she were right the programme had been designed to teach a boy something he had been given for free every day of his life.

The finding was not read aloud, because a finding that the language was not dying is not a finding, and a boy who is not a project is not a boy, and there is no drawer. It went into the file, and the clerk indexed it, and the clerk’s index card is the whole of what happened to it.

* * *

Councillor Iven did not treat her as disloyal. He read the seven pages in front of her and said the Commission had been wrong in a way it could not undo, and that the reserve had been held thirty-six years against a death and there had been no death, and that the honest course was to say so in the annual report in the terms the statute used, which were continued funding.

“You will recommend continuation,” he said. “That is the whole of what I have come to tell you. I have no power to stop it and I would not use one.”

“You have a power,” said Vessa. “You have the power not to recommend it, and there is a form for that, and it is the same form with one box changed, and I have watched clerks use that box.”

“Yes,” said the Councillor, “and when I use it, the boy is a boy again, the file closes, the schedule comes down, and in four years there is a young man of sixteen who has never been asked a question about any of it. I have seen that happen twice, and both times it was defended afterwards.”

He put her at the end of the table at his next meeting, a small thing she heard about for a year. The boy, when she told him, said: “You were on my side.”

“No,” said Vessa. “I was accurate. You will find the two are not the same, and you will find it a fairly late in life.”

* * *

The report was written in the twenty-sixth month, and it was thirty-one pages, and it recommended continued funding for three further years at a reduced figure, and on page nineteen there was a section headed SUBJECT, and the boy read it, because the clerk at the Commission was required to hand a subject the page containing his name, and the clerk did so, and the boy read standing up.

It said that the Commission had sought a custodian of a public tongue and found a family in which the tongue was resident. It said the subject showed no impairment. It said the subject’s cooperation was intermittent, and that the instructor attributed this not to reluctance but to a reasonable and articulable objection, on the subject’s part, to the conversion of a household into an exhibit, and considered the objection correct and had not attempted to overcome it. In the fourth sentence it said the subject appeared to regard the language as a means rather than an object, and that the Commission was unable to construct a form on which that view could be recorded.

He read the fourth sentence twice and did not tell his grandmother. He told Vessa, because she was the only person in the arrangement to whom he could say a sentence and have it not become a document. She said, “That is the most accurate sentence in the report, and I have no idea who wrote it.” He said, “You did.” She said, “I wrote the finding that the Commission ignored.” Neither of them said anything else, and the clock in the Weighhouse anteroom went past a minute that is in the record.

* * *

On the last afternoon of the twenty-sixth month, the Commission struck Item 9 from the schedule of dying tongues and moved it to a new schedule headed NOT ASSESSED, which is a different thing and is not an insult and which the boy found he could live inside. Councillor Iven came to the kitchen to say so, and was given tea, and told them the Commission had a reserve it had never spent and would keep on holding, and that his successor would have to be shown the line in the ledger, and that he had therefore written the figure in pencil as well, twice, and told two people, which was against every rule he had.

Then the room was the kitchen, and the boy did the thing he had been refusing to do for twenty-six months, which was to say it out loud to a person who had spent four years and a small fortune trying to teach it to him. The point was not to win and not to be a good project for one afternoon. He had been carrying it since the autumn of the yard tap and his hands were tired.

“You have been speaking it to me every day of my life,” he said, “and I have been speaking it back to you, and neither of us has ever called it a language, because it is not one, it is the way we argue about the price of milk, and I have been its last speaker since I was six, and I thought the only person in this house who did not know a word for a word was me.”

Vessa Renn did not write it down. She sat on the step with the notebook shut on her knee for some time, and the boy told her afterwards that she was crying, and she said she had been a long way from her own house and it had nothing to do with anything, which was not true and which she was entitled to say.

The milk was a half-penny dearer that week, and they went up the lane together to argue about it, in the way they had always argued about it, in the words that the Commission had spent three hundred and forty crowns to write down, and were still the words they used to argue with, and cost nothing, and were theirs.
