# The Funeral of the Sun

![Cover art: The Funeral of the Sun](../art/advanced/09-the-funeral-of-the-sun.webp)

At eleven twenty in the morning, the sun touched the western arch of Ophir.

Ansel Rue, thirty-seven, stood beneath that arch in the black wool coat of the House of Bell, one gloved hand resting on a cracked ceremonial mirror. Beside him stood Maera Kest, forty-one, the blue sash of the House of Wound cutting diagonally across her ocher robe. They had not spoken for six years, though each had received the other’s notices of death, sent by different roads and dressed in different colors.

The sun, one hour early, hung above the desert like a coin balanced on its edge.

“Closing time,” Ansel said.

Maera looked up. “Your humor has always been in the part of the sentence you omit.”

“My humor is theologically sound. Whatever we do next must be done now.”

The street bells began tolling. People emerged into the white glare, their faces confused above collars of desert blue. Every building in Ophir carried mirrors: small ones on doorframes, vast ones beneath awnings, civic ones at every crossing. They multiplied the sun into a thousand coins and now showed a thousand doors where the shadow had been.

Archivist Sava Vey descended from the Hall of Seven Reflections in a coat sewn with dull gold. He was fifty-two and walked with a ceremonial staff, though the pain in his left knee had made that staff practical rather than grand.

“The funeral begins at tomorrow’s eleventh,” he said. “You two will arrange it.”

Ansel removed his glove. His palm was broad, dry, marked by the cord burn that came from holding dying people through the last convulsion. Maera’s hands were smaller, steady, stained blue at the nails from the mineral salts used in the House of Wound.

“Who died?” she asked.

Vey stared eastward. The sun was still visible, though no longer bright enough to cast a second shadow.

“No one,” he said. “Yet.”

* * *

The mercy-houses existed because a person should not enter death alone, and because entering it need not be agony. A mercy-keeper eased pain, listened to refusal, opened the door when its owner freely chose it. The Houses disagreed over what survived them. Bell believed grief should be lightened by forgetting. Wound believed mercy without witness was merely tidiness.

Their rivalry had produced six attempts to murder each other, two constitutional crises, and an enduring official tolerance.

Ansel climbed the western tower with Maera and Vey. From its copper rim, they saw why the city was panicking. The sunlight did not vanish at the horizon. It descended into Ophir, pouring into the Crown Mirror above the central plaza in a column too narrow for a sun. The great mirror aimed its face at the burning sky, and the light made rainbows on every nearby wall.

“It is a siphon,” Maera said.

Vey said nothing.

Ansel crouched and studied the tower mechanism. Its bronze gears were moving backward. “Built to lower a god?”

“Built to hold a clock,” Vey said. “The founders did not call it a siphon.”

“They also did not build a city of a million possible endings.”

The priests descended to the Mercy Archive beneath the plaza. Its corridors were kept cold, its stone floor polished by generations of solemn soles. Vey unlocked the reservoir gate. Behind it lay pools of silver light and a dark machine whose teeth turned inside a lattice of mirrors.

Ansel remembered the rule: a living person who looked into an intact Ophir mirror and accepted a possible ending saw the death most likely to follow that acceptance. The mirror then took a genuine memory, always one that fear had made precious. It could not compel belief, bargain, or lie. It could only show.

The pools were not reflections. They were extracted memory: a wedding finger still circling an empty band, the bitter taste of a last apology, a child’s hand releasing its mother’s skirt. They had fed the Crown for decades. The Council traded each vision for another hour of daylight.

Vey’s staff struck the floor.

“The city cannot survive night,” he said. “Its granaries were built for solar preservation. Its cisterns will freeze. In one night, children and the old will die.”

Maera stood before the nearest pool. A man’s face hovered in it, then hers. “Then your arithmetic is sound.”

“It is not mine. The founders made it.”

“What have you done?”

“We ended death before it became a citywide appetite.” He indicated the pools. “Three hundred citizens gave a year. Six hundred gave less. Everyone woke. No one starved. The sun rose.”

Ansel saw the dates branded inside the machine. “By consent?”

“By civic necessity.”

Maera’s voice lowered. “That sentence is how our Houses become hangmen.”

“And your precious memory is how Ophir becomes a grave kept warm by defiance.”

The bells sounded again. This time they were not from the towers. Every mirror in the Archive was ringing, the glass vibrating at frequencies human ears should not hear.

Ansel placed his palm on the Mercy Mirror.

A vision took him.

He saw tomorrow’s funeral completed. The column descended cleanly into the Crown. Ophir endured a long night beneath clear stars. At dawn, pale light climbed from a trench in the plaza. He died there, struck down by Vey’s guards, while Maera held him and called him by a name he could no longer remember.

The vision released him. He staggered, suddenly unable to recall the night behind the archive cypress when Maera, thirty-five and freshly bereaved, had kissed him beneath her black veil. He still knew they had kissed. The fact remained. Its warmth did not.

Maera caught his elbow. “What did it take?”

“Something private.”

“Private is not a memory.”

“It is when people are most dishonest about what they need.”

She turned to the mirror. “Then I will be less generous than it.”

Her vision showed the same dawn and the same pale light. Vey alive in chains. Ansel at the Crown, cutting away the last siphon, and Maera carrying his body through streets that celebrated her as their flawless rememberer. It took from her the reason she had entered the House of Wound: her elder sister’s terrified face after Bell mercy-keepers closed her memory of a fatal stroke so death could come without pain. Maera had abandoned Ansel and every other keeper of Bell to build a house where pain was eased but nothing essential was stolen.

She came back pale.

“What did it take?” he asked.

“A fact I no longer own.”

“You said facts can be recovered.”

“No. I said they could be witnessed. That was before.”

Vey retreated toward the gate. Maera blocked him, and Ansel saw the cord burns on his own palm pale with anger.

“Let him go,” he said. “He is fifty-two and frightened.”

“He has scheduled a citywide viewing.”

“I heard.”

Maera glanced at him. “You always hear the rite and miss the reason for it.”

“And you always turn mercy into an argument you can win.”

For three years they had shared a table, a practice, and a silence. Love had not been their problem. Trust had been. Now neither could remember why that had mattered.

“You are right,” Maera said. “About the gate.”

She struck Vey’s staff from his hand and took his ring.

* * *

The funeral procession was meant to begin at the western arch and carry a black lantern to the plaza. At dawn, instead, Maera ordered the Houses to open their refuges and every guild to turn its hall into a shelter. Ansel led Bell’s bearers to the Crown, where a reservoir seal had already cracked.

The square was crowded and mirrors had turned the panic into separate worlds. In one reflection, Vey’s guards calmly accepted a sun that never rose again. In another, Ansel was alone at the foot of the chain, bleeding from the eyes. Every reflection that offered an accepted ending cost its viewer a memory. People who kept accepting what the glass showed began calling one another by the wrong names.

Ansel tore down the ceremonial shrouds meant for the Crown. “Cover the mirrors,” he said. “We are not buying hope with stolen years.”

The bearers obeyed. Black cloth swept across Ophir’s bright façades, leaving narrow windows of sky.

Vey’s guards closed the western arcade. From behind its gate, Vey spoke through a speaking horn.

“Look and the city survives. Refuse and darkness takes it. One year of memory from each household. One day from each orphan. I will not call that cruelty when your children begin to freeze.”

Maera mounted the steps beneath the Crown. She did not draw her priest’s knife.

“High Conductor,” she called, “your arithmetic has a hidden column.”

She pointed upward.

A filament of gold connected the Crown to the sun, thin as an eyelash, vibrating with stolen light. It was not feeding the city. It was hauling the sun down.

Ansel climbed after her, the seal-wrappers knotted between his teeth. The guards broke through the hall doors below. He heard Maera say, “Vey, answer this: do you believe the sun is dying, or have you been teaching it?”

The hidden line snapped.

The Crown tilted. Light struck the plaza like a falling mountain. Mirrors beneath the paving rang, cracked, and began to burst. The solar stream, no longer held in its channel, raced along the floor toward the river gate. If it entered the cisterns, they would flash-boil. If it entered the houses, the quarter would burn.

Maera chose the cisterns. She planted her knife upside down and spoke the death rite: not for a person, but for a journey believed to have no possible return.

The light struck her.

Ansel caught her before the column tore through the plaza. Heat stripped the blue from her sash and burned one side of her face. She weighed nothing in his arms. Her eyes stayed open.

“Do not make this a lesson,” she said.

“I was planning to eat alone for a year.”

“Ruinous vow.”

He tied Vey’s ring with cord and let the stream turn along the old river channel. A mercy-keeper could accompany death, but could not command it. He could only mark a direction and stay in the way.

At the channel gate, Vey stood alone. He had lost his guards, his authority, and the mirrored images of the future. Ansel released Maera against the balustrade and went to him.

Vey said, “It is too late to save the Crown.”

“It is never too late to turn it.”

“The reservoir will burst the plaza.”

“Not if you lower it.”

The Crown would turn only once. Vey had designed the engine to take the memories of the dead, but the dead had not consented. The final movement required his own memory and consent.

Ansel held out the knife. Maera had followed, pale but standing beside him. Vey looked at her ruined sash.

“You would let me,” he said.

“No,” Maera answered. “But I will not kill you for being afraid. That has always been the distinction.”

Vey took the knife. He turned the Crown toward the earth.

All the covered mirrors in Ophir boomed once.

The solar column bent downward. Heat vanished into the deep cisterns, where months of stored warmth would keep the pipes from freezing. Stone groaned. Glass sang. The Crown descended by inches until it fitted into a mosaic beneath the plaza, becoming part of the pavement rather than a mechanism above it.

The true sun, at last unburdened, touched the desert.

* * *

They held the funeral at midnight beneath the western arch, because the city no longer had a trustworthy clock but it still had bells.

Vey spoke first. He had forgotten most of his daughter’s face. Still, when asked her name, he said, “Neve.”

Then the citizens uncovered their mirrors one by one and turned them toward the wall. They did not ask the mirrors for permission. They spoke the names of those they had failed to mourn while calling themselves merciful: a brother lost to a fever, a rival dead in a riot, a mother whose last words had been sealed away. Grief entered the night without buying another hour.

The sun sank. Its edge slipped below the horizon with neither flame nor ruin. Far away, the desert answered in a flash of violet light.

For the first time in centuries, Ophir could see the stars.

Ophir did not wake saved. The poor crowded the Houses, disputing beds and soup. Vey was taken into the House of Wound, not for execution, but because he had asked to be watched. Guards carried the two rival House leaders through the blackened square.

At the steps of the western arch, Maera stopped.

“I know what I feel about you,” she said. “I do not know when it began.”

“So did I.”

“Do you want it returned?”

Ansel considered the aqueducts below, the squares of sky, the cold ahead. He had forgotten a kiss, but the hand that had remembered it still knew the pressure of hers.

“No,” he said. “I want you to choose again.”

Maera looked at him. Then she took his hand, and they walked into the first unaugured dawn.
