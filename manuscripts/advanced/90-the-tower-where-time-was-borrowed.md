# The Tower Where Time Was Borrowed

![Cover art: The Tower Where Time Was Borrowed](../art/advanced/90-the-tower-where-time-was-borrowed.webp)

The Comptroller of the National Account of Time signs every morning at seven, and on the morning Sabine Roth was forty-seven she signed one hundred and six withdrawals and set the pen down and could not recall a single one of them.

A withdrawal is made out on Form T-1. It asks for the amount in days, the office the days are for, the date they are spent, and the name of the person against whom they are drawn, and the date is always tomorrow's, because that is the whole instrument. The tower does not make time. It spends a day that has not come yet, and the day comes anyway, and the difference is the country's.

The form asks for a person's name in the fourth box, and the reason it asks is old and dull and unanswerable: the Account is a register of persons and not a treasury of hours, because repayment is a personal obligation, and a debt can only be personal if there is a person standing in it. Time has never been taken from a nation. It has been taken, four hundred and two years of it, from named individuals, and the office of Comptroller exists to sign the box.

The overdraft limit was fixed by statute in the year of the foundation and has been exceeded in ninety of the last hundred and twenty years. There is a memorandum on the subject for every year of the account, three paragraphs each, the last eleven of them in Sabine's own hand, and the Treasury has acknowledged every one of them, and nine have been printed. There has been no audit for a hundred and seventeen years, because the last one was refused, and a refusal is entered in the register and is not a suspension of the requirement.

The morning's list was on the desk when she came in, and it was not an unusual morning's list. The Health floor wanted four days for a fever ward in the south quarter, and had had them four times running, and named the same four people on each occasion in a way that made the page read like a rota of the dead. The Education floor wanted eleven days to complete a school year and named eleven children, and the children were eight years old. The War floor wanted six days and named a man of thirty-four in the Works office, and there was a note in the margin saying the man had consented, and the note was in the War floor's hand, and the man had died in 1911.

The country did not know that any of this was unusual, because there has never been a year in which the country was not doing it. A child born in the capital is taught the horizon before she is taught the alphabet: this is how far we can see. The horizon moves a little every year and the Comptroller's office publishes the new figure in the spring, and the schools copy it into the back of the geography book, and every household plans to the edge of it and not one step beyond, and there is a national cheerfulness about it that visitors take for irony and which is not irony at all.

* * *

Silas Cray had been in the tower fifty years and was seventy-one, and he looked sixty, and he was the only person in the building who had ever asked why.

He was the officer in custody of the ninth floor, which was the Residual Register, where the Account kept the difference between what it had taken and what it had given back, arranged by year, in a hand that had not changed since his first month. The floors below were Foundation, Custody, Works, War, Health, Debts, Education and Marriage, one for each century of the account's life, and the clerks who worked them were cheerful and overworked and forty years younger than he was.

Cray had come in at twenty-one, on the day after the second Comptroller died, and there had been nobody to teach him the forms, so he had taught himself from the Foundation Register, which begins in the year the tower was finished and carries the first Comptroller's name cut into the pediment above the door.

He stayed, and he had been asked why across fifty years, and his answer was always the same and never quite the whole of it: because the residual is kept in one hand, and one hand is faster, and he was good at it, and there was nobody else who would. Under the desk was a drawer of carbon tallies going back to his first month, and he had never reconciled them against the volumes, because reconciling them would be an audit, and a man may only begin an audit if he means to finish it.

"There is one further thing," he said, once, to a Treasury assessor who had asked to be shown the floor. "There is a fact about this country that nobody inside it is permitted to know, and I know it, and I have known it for fifty years, and I am seventy-one years old with the use of about sixty, and I have not got time for it to be both true and untrue. If I retire, it dies with me. If I stay, it is a book on a shelf in a building, and books are patient."

"The first Comptroller signed one withdrawal," Cray said, on the first morning of the week they agreed to do this in, "and it was for nine days, and the purpose was the foundation of a customs house, and the name in the box was his own. You can walk out of the lobby and read it. It took him a fortnight to pay that back out of his own mornings." He turned a page with a finger that did not have to search. "I have eleven years left in this account. I have known since about 1975. I have never needed a column to check it."

* * *

Veda Roth was fourteen and had been in the tower since she was six, because her mother was the Comptroller and there was nowhere else for her to be on a school holiday, and the second floor reading room had a window and eleven thousand books and a radiator that worked.

She read the Foundation Register first, on the second day, because it was the oldest and because she was that sort of fourteen. The names in the fourth box ran in no order she could see. She tried age first, and then the parish of residence, and then the date, and on the fourth afternoon she worked out that they were in order of how much time each person had left to spend, largest at the front, and that the person with eleven days in the box was the last one in the volume, and that his name was in a slightly different ink because he had been entered in a hurry by a clerk who was working from a floor below.

Four months later she asked her mother what the fourth box was for, and Sabine Roth told her, because she told her everything, and that was the first thing that had ever been a mistake.

She was not a nervous child and she was not a strange one, and she was the only person in the tower who slept four hours and did not look tired, because a pending entry is charged at a quarter rate, which the register states in a table and nobody has ever queried. She did the cross-index of the Marriage floor in the long holidays because the clerks were busy and because she liked the work, and she could rule a column to the millimetre, and the head clerk of that floor told the Comptroller in March that he would put her on the establishment when she was fifteen, on the strength of her handwriting.

On the Sunday in March she copied her own name out of the fourth box in pencil, onto the back of an envelope, and put it in a drawer in her bedroom, and did not tell anybody for four months.

* * *

The argument in the ninth-floor office ran for two evenings and neither of them won, and both of them were right about their own reasons, and Cray did not conceal how pleased he was to be losing and Sabine did not conceal how tired she was of winning.

The statute required an audit at least once in every hundred years, and the last had been refused, and the reason it had been refused was in the file, and the reason it had been refused then was the reason it would be refused now, which was that the country had been living out of tomorrow since the tower was built and an audit was a survey of the interior of a country that had no interior.

"I am not asking you to stop," Cray said. "I am asking you to count. I have the columns. I could bring the count up from the floor tomorrow and it would be a morning's work." He put his hand flat on the residual volume, which he had been keeping for fifty years and which was, in the opinion of two surveyors who had touched it once each, one of the best-kept books in the city. "There are four hundred and two years of borrowing in here and a memorandum for every one of them, and in four hundred and two years not one Treasury official has come up to this floor."

"You have a memorandum for every year of it," Sabine said, "and not one of them asks me a question I can answer."

"No," Cray agreed. "They ask whether I have anything further to report, and I have answered no every year since 1974, and every one of those replies is on the floor above me in a file with a Treasury seal on it, and a seal means the Treasury has read it." He turned a page. "I have never told a Treasury official what I think. I have never needed to. I have only ever needed a seal."

* * *

The girl worked it out on a Sunday, at the kitchen table, with the Foundation Register open in front of her and a pencil she was not supposed to have, and she said it in the flat voice she had learned to use in the building.

"You put me in the register before I was born."

Sabine Roth did not ask what she meant, because there was only one thing she could mean, and because she had been waiting eleven years for somebody to ask her about it.

"The day after," Veda said. "The day after I was born, in the fourth box, one year, and it is my name, and it is your hand, and you never countersigned it." She turned the page over and pushed it across the table. "Do you know what an unperfected instrument is? It is not a thing that happened. It is a thing that is proposed. As far as the tower is concerned I have not been born yet."

Sabine put the kettle on, which she did not need to do, and looked at the page, and the hand in the fourth box was her own from the second of a March eleven years ago, written at the end of a night shift with a pen that had lost its cap.

"You were born on the Thursday," she said. "The Yard trams had gone in and the fever ward went short and I had signed a hundred and eleven in a day, and I came in on the Friday to write it out properly, and I got as far as your name and then I sat there until the milk came up at five." She turned the page to face her. "The box does not want a reason. I want you to know that I had one, and that the form has no field for it, and that this is the whole of my defence, and I have not said it to anybody in that building because in that building it would have been a filing error."

"You said it properly," Veda said. "You did not countersign it."

"No," Sabine said. "I did not countersign it."

* * *

The rule was in the third century of the register, on the floor called Custody, and Cray had found it in his first decade and had not mentioned it to a living soul. Where an Account is drawn against in advance it is charged not interest but custody, and the charge falls on the officer in custody of the residual, and that is the whole mechanism, and it has never been amended, and the draftsman of the Custody century wrote a footnote under it saying that the arrangement was intended to prevent the office being dissolved and had not been thought about since.

"He has been paying since 1975," Sabine said. "He wrote every name. Every entry in the fourth box for fifty years came out of that hand."

"He did not choose to be in custody," Cray said, "and he was never asked, and he has never once said he was, and if you put that in a report I will resign before you finish the sentence." He squared the residual against the desk. "The residue was eleven years, and the residue was him, and there is no instrument in nine floors that can pay an officer back." He lifted it again. "He is not a creditor. He is overhead."

The closing instrument was Form Z, and Sabine had known it existed for thirty years and had never read the whole of it, and she read the whole of it on the Sunday night and the whole of it took four hours. A single account of four hundred years could be discharged in one act only, by a single drawing against a person not yet born, because the Account may only take time that nobody has laid a claim upon, and every living claimant in the register was spoken for. Her own time was claimed by an office. Cray's was claimed by fifty years of untransferable memory. Her daughter's was claimed by a school, a mother, and the account itself.

There was exactly one birth in the whole of the register that the tower was holding, and it was fourteen years old, and it was hers.

She wrote the count out on the back of a Form T-1 and did not sign it.

"He says you could have signed it tonight," Cray said, reading it upside down. "The drawer is not locked and you are the Comptroller."

"That is the difficulty," Sabine said. "Section nineteen of the closing instrument. The signature of the person against whom it is drawn, at the age of majority, witnessed by the Comptroller." She set the pencil down. "The only hand that can ever close this account is hers, and she will be fifteen in four years, and a person of fifteen cannot be made to sign, and there is no other hand in this building and there never will be, and so I have been keeping a four-hundred-year debt in reserve for a daughter who did not ask for it." She looked at the pages. "I did not put it on her to stop her knowing. I put it on her so that it would not be on me, and I did not countersign, and there is a difference between those two things which I have had eleven years to think about and it is not as large as I would like."

* * *

Cray had the statute on the desk by six and read all of section forty-four with his finger going down the page, and then he read it again, because it is the sort of section that rewards the second reading.

"Where an Account remains in arrear, and the account-holder is a person holding office, the Account may be discharged by repayment."

"The Comptroller's signature is the only control in this building," Sabine said.

"The Comptroller's signature is the only control, and the account-holder is not a control, the account-holder is a debtor." He put the book down with the care of a man putting down something breakable. "Repayment is made out of the unspent time of the holder, day by day, and the holder's horizon shortens as it is paid, and there is no other source in the statute, and the draftsman was not a fool, because a person who repays a debt out of their own life is the only kind of payment the Account recognises."

* * *

The refund instrument ran to four hundred pages, because section forty-four requires each of the four hundred years of the Foundation to be audited separately and discharged separately, and the work took eleven years, and the account was opened to the public for the first time in four hundred and two years on the first Monday of that eleven, with the whole of the Foundation Register, the whole of the residual, and the four hundred and two memoranda, bound in the reading room on the second floor, where a woman of thirty-five with a job in the Treasury read them for a living and took notes for a book that did not exist.

The cost was immediate, and it was total, and it was specified to the day, because a debt like this can be. Each repayment was a Form R-1, out of the holder's own unspent time, countersigned by the officer in custody of the residual, and the stack of Forms R-1 on the ninth floor came to be the longest physical object in the building. Sabine Roth paid for the country a day at a time, out of her own mornings, in a tower where the mornings were the last thing a clerk ever saw.

What it cost was visible on a form. Sabine Roth's horizon had been thirty-one years when she began and was twenty when she stopped, and the spring figures went down by the same measure, and the figure was printed in the back of every geography book in the country, and in the eleventh year a child in the Ninth Ward school copied it out and got it wrong and was corrected by her teacher. The tower lost two hundred and nine days of working time, which is to say it lost the second floor for most of a working morning, and the second floor is the reading room, so the country paid for its own audit in the room where the audit was being read.

* * *

The four hundred and first year was discharged on a Thursday in the eleventh year, and she signed the final repayment herself, at the desk on the ninth floor, at ten past six in the evening, in ink, with the counterweight of the old book against her wrist, and Silas Cray stood up, which he did not do, and read the entry, and put his finger on the line and said that the residue was clear.

Then he went to the window, because the office hours on the ninth floor were tomorrow's hours, and had been for fifty years, and a clerk on the ninth floor finished at dawn and walked home into a morning that had already finished without him.

Outside there was one still there, coming up over the yards and the chimney of the Ninth Ward school, orange on the slates, and he stood and watched it arrive, and it took eleven years off him while he watched, and when he turned round he was sixty, and his hair was what it had been in 1974.
