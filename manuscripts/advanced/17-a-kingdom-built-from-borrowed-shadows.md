# A Kingdom Built from Borrowed Shadows

![Cover art: A Kingdom Built from Borrowed Shadows](../art/advanced/17-a-kingdom-built-from-borrowed-shadows.webp)

On the morning Perrin Moss lost his shadow, he came to the Second Seat carrying a broken cartwheel and complaining about axle grease, as though the missing silhouette were a minor clerical error. “Which road took you?” the clerk asked, while morning light bleached the courthouse steps and exposed the soft grey blur beneath Perrin’s boots. “The Broad Way,” he answered, pointing behind him; “I know it by the cooper’s sign and the crack in the third paving stone.”

The clerk called the registrar, the registrar called the bailiff, and the bailiff finally called Mara Vell, magistrate of the Second Seat, who had learned in twenty years of public life that a person’s face was rarely as informative as the way they held their hands. Perrin’s fingers trembled around the cartwheel’s rim; he was broad and weathered, with a broken thumbnail and the habit of standing too straight before officials. His name appeared in the night ledger under the previous evening’s lending, the ordinary amount of one finger’s breadth, taken at first bell and returned at dawn.

Mara placed a brass sun-glass on the desk, angled it toward him, and watched the missing shadow appear as a thin, ragged line. “Where were you at first bell?” she asked. “Home,” Perrin said, and he named the Crown collector who had claimed the north tower had failed. “Did you consent?” Mara pressed. His jaw tightened. “I gave him the finger,” he said. “That is consent to one finger, not to the rest,” Mara replied.

When the bailiff refused to bring the ledger, Mara ordered the Night Office itself into the room. Queen Asteria’s seal covered the page, the broad black sunburst reserved for the sovereign’s central accounts, and beneath it someone had written *Emergency continuance, year eleven* in a cramped hand. Eleven years. Mara had signed her first petition against the old tithe at twenty-six. She had watched her locksmith father give one finger’s breadth of shadow each evening and receive it back each morning. She considered the system honest because it was visible, measured, and recorded, and because a child could learn the difference between one finger and a whole hand.

Perrin’s wife was buried six streets away, and he had not missed a dusk since. Mara signed a summons for the Night Office, then added: *Bring the Crown’s collector and the measuring key.* The bailiff read the Queen’s seal twice and objected that it superseded the order, but Mara made him read the charter aloud. “Emergency continuance is permitted where the outer beacons fail,” he admitted, naming the north, east, and river beacons. Mara supplied their keepers’ names and locations: the river beacon had been repaired in spring. The east beacon served as a private signal room for the Queen’s messengers, while the north beacon stood seven miles beyond the walls, where soldiers had spent the summer repairing a mountain road. “Who says they failed?” she asked. “The Night Office.” “Produce their reports.” The bailiff’s mouth closed.

Mara told Perrin he would not be charged for having no shadow, then saw him look at her as if she had offered a year of bread. When the hearing emptied into the street, word traveled faster than official seals, and by noon seven people had arrived with grievances: a baker whose hands shook after dusk, a guard who could no longer recognize the barracks from the river road, and a widow whose front door rejected her shadow. One man said the missing portion had been taken so often that his body now cast a shadow shaped like a coin.

Orrin met her below the palace, where the city’s foundations dipped into black stone; he was a thin man with a lamp-shaped nose, ink beneath both eyes, and a door that required three keys cut from different metals. When he said she should not be there, Mara replied that her office was conducting an audit, and when he objected that the reservoir belonged to the Crown, she answered that the citizens’ shadows did not. The lamps along the corridor leaned toward the wall, as if listening. Orrin warned that the reservoir was not supposed to be opened during a continuance, but Mara replied that it should have been opened before one began; he handed her the third key.

The chamber below was larger than the courthouse above. Glass tanks rose from the floor in rows, each filled with darkness that possessed weight and moved whenever the lamps moved; in the first tank a woman’s shadow hung like black cloth, fingers spread, and in the second a child’s shadow knelt beside grown hands although the child had never lent a hand, so Mara stopped. Orrin explained that every person gives a little, the reservoir returns what it can, and the Crown keeps the remainder when the outer road is unsafe. Mara objected that the remainder was supposed to return at dawn, but Orrin replied that it was not remainder anymore.

He led her to a narrow tank at the back, where years of pressure clouded the glass and overlapping shadows had become nearly solid. A brass plate identified it as the *Auxiliary reserve, Northern Beacon*, and when Mara pressed her palm to the glass, a small shadow turned toward her, its fingers making the gesture she remembered from a hundred petitions. It belonged to Sera Dain, a canal worker who had reported a missing shadow last winter. Mara recorded the date before discovering that Sera’s shadow had been drawn three times in one year, each withdrawal entered as a separate continuance. Mara called the practice theft, but Orrin called it provision; when she said provision required consent, he answered that consent was a word for peacetime, and Mara looked through the glass at the trapped faces. “Then peace has ended.”

Orrin unlocked the main ledger, bound in black boards and chained to a pedestal. Mara read its totals by lamplight: the first column recorded nightly loans, the second recorded returns, and for three years the columns matched before the return column began to fall short, eventually becoming a mountain of debt. The difference did not disappear; it traveled elsewhere through a narrow silver pipe that emerged from the reservoir wall and climbed through the stone. When Mara asked where it went, Orrin answered north, then to the palace first, and when she pressed him about its contents, he identified the Queen’s reserve and the Queen’s night. Mara closed the ledger. “Take me to her.”

Asteria received them in the Hall of Hours, a room with silvered windows that showed no sky and a ceiling painted with gold stars over a midnight field. A fire beneath the glass table heated nothing while Asteria sat beyond it in plain black wool, fifty-four years old, broad-shouldered, gray at the temples, and marked by a scar along her right thumb. Mara placed the ledger on the glass and reported that the north beacon had been repaired, the east beacon was a signal room, the river beacon was sound, and the emergency had no current cause. Asteria replied that the current cause was not a matter for a magistrate, and Mara answered that it was a matter for the people whose shadows were being taken, keeping both hands on the table as she observed that the charter’s daily promise had become a permanent condition.

“The charter was written when the kingdom had no war,” Asteria said. “The kingdom is at war,” Mara answered. The Queen replied that it stood beside one, explaining that the roads beyond the mountains were unlit, the old dark was pressing closer, and the soldiers at Grey Pass would be cut off before the thaw if the north beacon fell. Mara had seen the reports describing three hundred soldiers on that steep, cold road, where a traveler could pass the same stone three times without recognizing the journey. She knew the Crown’s lanterns were powered by shadows stored in the reservoir. “Then ask the people,” Mara said, “tell them what is needed, and let them choose.” When Asteria predicted refusal, Mara allowed that some might refuse, but the Queen answered that most would, which was why a sovereign existed. Mara opened the ledger to its final page and reminded her that a sovereign rules under the charter, not above it.

Asteria ordered Orrin to leave, but he remained because the reservoir was under audit and his presence was required. For the first time, Mara saw that the Queen feared not the reservoir itself but how quickly the truth could move through the palace without her permission. Asteria challenged her to return the shadows, open every tank, and let the city go blind. She asked whether ordinary people would thank her when the roads lost their names. Mara looked at the painted stars and accused her of forcing a choice between theft and danger, while Asteria said she was asking Mara to govern; Mara answered that the Queen was asking her to sign a confession and call it consent.

Asteria rose and announced that the continuance would be renewed at sunset. Mara could file her objection and lose her seat, and she could watch the north road fail while judges debated whether law was more comfortable than war. She walked away, and the door shut with the soft click of a well-made lock. Outside, Mara found citizens waiting in streets that had heard only that the reservoir was open; they did not know whether to be angry or grateful, and that uncertainty made them both dangerous and honest.

She convened a meeting in the old bathhouse beneath Cooper’s Bridge, standing among the benches rather than on a platform, with the ledger open under her arm. When a man asked what she would do, Mara explained that she could order the reservoir opened within the law. The excess shadows would return, and the north lanterns would go out, making some roads difficult and allowing people to get lost. A woman with flour on her sleeves asked whether Mara would watch from a warm room, and Mara promised instead to stand in the street with her. Another voice called that this was not a plan, and Mara replied that it was the beginning of one.

Nella Rook, a cartographer who had lost two fingers in a mill accident, unfolded a map on the bathhouse floor, marking ordinary roads in white and shadow roads, which held their shape after dark, in black. She said the black roads were not the only way between places, naming milestones, bells, wall seams, and the smell of ovens. Gide, a former watch captain with a stiff leg, asked who would walk them. When she answered that everyone who could would, he reminded her that everyone included the old, the sick, and those who could not see well. Mara concluded that they needed more than volunteers, and Nella counted the requirements on her fingers: oil, a hundred lamps, rope for the bridges, and food and water kept inside. The meeting broke into arguments about storming the palace, protecting the north road, and leaving before sunset, until Mara asked for the demand no law had previously made: withhold the nightly span, keep the lanterns burning, and give the city hands rather than another finger. Some people looked away, while others began calculating what their hands could carry.

* * *

At sunset, the first bell rang and windows across Veyr went dark one by one. The city did not become black; it became uncertain, as streets narrowed by half and a turning toward the market seemed to lead to the river. People stepped outside and found that familiar outlines beneath their feet had blurred. They began the lantern chain: Nella’s cartographers marked corners with chalk and twine, Gide’s watchmen carried rope between posts, and bakers brought out spare oil while children ran messages between wards under adult supervision. The city moved slowly, loudly, and with many complaints, and the cost arrived before midnight.

At the river gate, Eda tried to find the house she had inherited from her mother by its blue door, cracked step, and wind chime made of shells, but she saw five blue doors and no wind chime and sat down on the stones to cry. In the lower market, a frightened horse slipped on a road that no longer held its edges, overturning a cart of winter grain into the canal, where families watched the food they had expected to eat through the cold disappear. At the infirmary, the lamps burned low, and a physician sent word that two fevered patients needed to be moved to a room with a west window, though no one could find the stair. Mara carried one end of the stretcher with a watchman named Sol. The stair turned where it had not turned the day before, and they climbed by touch, following the scrape of stone and Sol’s steady breathing. Joren left the north gate for Grey Pass with a lantern and a sealed message, becoming the first person to cross into the unlit road and failing to return by dawn.

* * *

Mara did not sleep. At first light she went to the reservoir, where palace guards stood aside because the keeper had opened the doors and no one knew which law the Queen would obey first. Inside, the tanks trembled and the excess shadows pressed against the glass. Mara felt the missing parts of her city as a thousand brief absences, each carrying a person’s ordinary weight; when a small shadow curled into a ball in the nearest tank, she put her hand over the glass. A clerk from the Night Office warned that opening it would fail the north lanterns, but Mara said the garrison would learn that the city had chosen to open the reservoir and that a message would go with the next caravan. When the clerk said there might be no next caravan, Mara told him to let the road decide whether it carried it.

Mara invoked the Equal Dusk Act, an old law written not for a queen’s refusal but for a city that knew what a promise meant. She spoke the borrower’s name, the hour of lending, and the reason for the taking. The glass cracked, a second name followed, then a third, until the reservoir answered with a low note like a bell struck below water. The Queen came down the stair without her guards, because in their place stood citizens who had followed Mara through the palace doors, not to fight but to witness. Asteria asked whether Mara understood what she was doing, and Mara answered that she understood what Asteria had done, while the Queen warned that both the north and the city might suffer. When Asteria said the law could not carry the weight of every death that followed, Mara replied that no law could, which was why it had to be witnessed.

Mara placed both hands on the cracked glass. The Equal Dusk Act allowed a magistrate to stand surety for a common emergency, so she could open the reservoir and return what had been taken, but the night would enter the empty spaces until a lawful keeper was chosen, and for one hour every person in Veyr would need to hold the city together without a shadow. She could withdraw the extra shadows and let citizens maintain the roads by hand, or she could bind her own full shadow into the common reservoir, giving the night a center while everyone else reclaimed their missing pieces, though the law did not promise that the center would survive. Asteria asked whether she was asking the city to trust her, and Mara’s shadow loosened from her feet, rose as a long black shape, and spread across the floor while the chamber’s lamps leaned toward it. Cold entered her bones, but she kept her hands on the glass. “I am asking it to watch me.”

The tanks opened, and shadows poured into the room like arriving bodies, striking the stone with weight. Perrin’s shadow found him in the passage and Sera Dain’s rose from a tank and fled upstairs. Throughout Veyr, people felt missing pieces return, sometimes painfully and sometimes as sudden memories of rooms they had not visited in years. The city fell into deeper darkness; outside, the lanterns shook, a cry rose from the west, and the crowd at the palace doors panicked as if the night had won. Mara could not see them, so she held the reservoir’s edge while her shadow became the only shape large enough to keep the glass from breaking.

Then the city answered: a bell rang from the west ward, and another from the river. Someone struck a milk pan in the market, and someone else called a name that made a stranger open a door, tightening the lantern chain rope by rope until the streets became a web of human choices. Mara heard a child crying near the palace stairs, an older woman calling for help, and a man shouting that the north gate had gone blind, but the law gave her no order of importance. She called for the bathhouse bell to ring three times, signaling every ward to hold its nearest light and send one person to the nearest neighbor. The message traveled by hand, clumsy, slow, and incomplete, yet it worked: two bakers found the old woman, the child’s mother found her daughter in a doorway where the stones had shifted, and Gide organized the watchmen at the north gate into a human rope, able to feel one another’s sleeves even when they could not see the road.

At dawn, the reservoir emptied and Mara’s shadow remained while Asteria stood across the chamber with sleeplessness drawn into her face. The Queen said the private reserve was gone and the palace had no light of its own, but Mara answered that the north lanterns were out too, and when Asteria predicted the city would demand something in return, Mara replied that it already had. Asteria looked at Mara’s feet, where there was no second Mara beneath her, and reached toward the glass before asking what she wanted. Mara had imagined an emptied throne, a dismissed minister, and a rewritten law, but those questions felt too large for the room. Instead, she asked for the reservoir to open every dusk, each ward to count what it lent and what returned, and no continuance to proceed without a named cause, a named end, and witnesses from the affected streets. She added that the north road would still need light, but the city would decide together what light to give.

When Asteria said the people would fight over the new arrangement, Mara replied that they already fought and would at least see the cost. The Queen’s expression did not soften, but it became exact. Asteria declared that she would not surrender the Crown’s authority, while Mara clarified that no one had asked her to surrender it, only to share it. The Queen looked through the empty tanks at Veyr, where shutters were opening, lanterns were returning to doorsteps, and citizens were calling names across narrow streets to learn which ones still answered. Then she placed her hand on the cracked glass. “Then open it.”

The law was read in the courtyard before the first full morning bell, and the Queen signed beneath the witnesses’ names. Seven wardens carried the ledger into the street, where every household could inspect the count, dispute a mistake, or refuse to lend and accept the risk of the unlit road. The first civic night was hard, because three lamps failed and the river market lost another morning’s worth of fish, but the losses were counted in public, leaving no winter hidden in a private tank. Mara kept her seat for a while and refused the title of Night Warden, because the work required a magistrate who had never lost her shadow, and that was no longer her. She rented a room above a locksmith’s shop, where the floorboards gave beneath her feet and no one asked why she cast none.

On the seventh evening, a dispatch from Grey Pass arrived at last. Joren had reached the garrison, but the unlit road had cost the forward beacon and a cart of medicine; when he returned, two of his fingers were stiff with frostbite, and the message bore one line: supply the pass with visible lamps or withdraw the garrison. The city read the cost aloud before anyone could call the victory clean.

* * *

On the seventh evening, citizens came to the reservoir with measured shadows, bringing them not as offerings but as accounts. The east ward sent one span, the river ward another, the baker who had lost grain in the canal sent less, and a wealthy house sent twice its share and printed the amount on its gate. The north road received a line of lanterns tended by paid watchmen, every lamp supplied from a public chest. At dusk the sky darkened over Veyr, but the lamps did not fail. In the palace, a small fire burned in the Hall of Hours while the Queen worked beside a clerk beneath a lamp with no hidden reservoir, and at the Second Seat Perrin Moss repaired wheels by daylight before walking home beneath a lantern by night, casting a full shadow at his feet.

Mara met Perrin on the Broad Way, and he stopped when he saw her, looked down, and said, “You’re difficult to see.” She answered that he found her difficult only when the light was behind him. He laughed, then grew serious and handed her a small iron key, plain and unenchanted, meant for an ordinary door. “For the shop,” he said, “my wife’s old shop, which has a good lock now.” Mara asked why, and he answered that the house needed a better one. She looked up at the city, where seven public lamps marked the wards across the dark windows and people carried light from doorway to doorway behind them. Their shadows lay separate and visible on the stones, returning when morning came. Mara put the key in her pocket and walked with Perrin toward the river as the first star appeared. The night no longer belonged to one hidden hand; it belonged to everyone, and everyone could see the price.
