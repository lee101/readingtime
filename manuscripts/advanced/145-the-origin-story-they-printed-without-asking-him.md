# The Origin Story They Printed Without Asking Him

![Cover art: The Origin Story They Printed Without Asking Him](../art/advanced/145-the-origin-story-they-printed-without-asking-him.webp)

The Halversgate Advertiser ran a thousand words about Aurel Sarn on the seventh of March, on page three, with a photograph of him standing on the Cove Cut bridge, and he has not set foot in this town since the February before.

It has been read by every person in Halversgate and it is the only thing anybody has ever printed about him, because he does not give interviews and has not given one and there is a photograph on file of the back of his head at the harbour in 1998 which is the only photograph of him in the world.

He can walk into anything and come out of it. That is the whole of what is known and it is not known very widely. Each time he goes in, one year comes off the end of his life, and he has gone in nine times, and he is forty-six.

* * *

Aurel Sarn went into a pumping station at Corve in the January of 1998 that had flooded to the roof and had four men in it, and he was eighteen, and he came out with all four, and the water was to his chest when he went in and it was to his chest when he came out, which is not possible and is in the Advertiser.

He went into the Tavlans barn in the November of 2004, which is the hardest thing anybody in Halversgate can talk about. He went into the Cove Cut in the March of 2011. He went into a house on Weir Lane in the December of 2016.

Four of the nine are on the record. Four. There is a fifth that everybody in the town agrees on and that has never been in a newspaper, and that is the quarry at Tarnwick in the summer of 2007, and nobody has ever printed it because nobody except Aurel Sarn and the two men who were standing there know it happened at all.

* * *

The reporter is called Perrin Quist and she is twenty-six, and she came to Halversgate in the September from a college in the county town, and she had been at the Advertiser eleven months and had done four stories, three of which were about a road.

She wrote the piece in three weeks.

On the first week she went to the library and read the parish magazine and the two books about the harbour, and on the second week she had eleven names, and on the third week she rang the Advertiser's advertisers and asked, and a man who runs the boat hire came in and talked for an hour and forty minutes, and he was not lying either.

Every fact in the thousand words is a true fact. Every fact in the thousand words is a true fact about somebody, and the somebody is not always Aurel Sarn.

* * *

The bridge is the first of the three that is wrong and it is wrong by eleven years.

The piece says that in the summer of 1987, when he was seven, Aurel Sarn and his older brother Emrys built a rope bridge across the Cove Cut, four hundred feet of it, at the point where the cut comes out of the rock, and that the bridge was the reason the water authority put the fence up in 1996.

There is no rope bridge across the Cove Cut. There has never been one. The water authority put up the fence in 1996 because two boys went off the rock at the point in the June and one of them was in the water for eleven minutes before a man called Ned Teague, who is now seventy-four, went in after him.

Aurel Sarn was not in Halversgate in the summer of 1987. He was in a children's home in the county town and he has never discussed that and did not discuss it with Perrin Quist.

There was an older brother. That part is right.

* * *

The brother is the second of the three that is wrong and it is wrong by one year.

The piece says that Emrys Sarn drowned in the Cove Cut in the winter of 1987, aged eleven, and that Aurel Sarn went in after him, and that he was pulled out by his father, and that it was nine minutes.

Emrys did not drown. Emrys is alive. He is in a town forty miles away and he is fifty and he has never set foot in Halversgate since he was eleven, which is the same summer the piece is set in, and he did not go near the water.

The child who drowned at the Cove Cut in the winter of 1987 was a boy called Emrys Vaughan, who was eleven, and he was drowned in four feet of water at the beach end of the Cut with his skate in his hand, and his mother was forty miles away at the time, and her name in the Advertiser is written with a hyphen.

* * *

The rescue that went differently is the third.

The piece says that in the January of 1998 Aurel Sarn carried Saville Kerr across the ice on the reservoir at Cove Head, and that he stood on the ice for nine minutes, and that Saville Kerr was seventeen, and that the image of it has been on the cover of the Advertiser every year since.

It was not Aurel Sarn on the ice. It was a man called Ruben Teal, who was twenty-four, who was there that day, and who is alive, and who is now fifty-two, and who was not asked anything by Perrin Quist because he is not in any of the four sources.

Ruben Teal went out four hundred yards and came back with her and did not fall through. The nine minutes is the number in the parish account and it is correct.

Aurel Sarn was in Corve that day. He went into the pumping station at nine in the morning and came out at half past ten with four men in him, and nobody at the Advertiser has ever checked which happened first.

* * *

The thing that made it worse, and Perrin Quist has never denied this and has said in print that she thinks about it, is that all three errors had been believed in Halversgate before she printed them.

The Advertiser did not invent a false brother. It found one.

There is a grave in the churchyard at Halversgate with SARN cut into the headstone, under a yew tree, in the plot the family bought in 1990, and the name on it is EMELYSARN, and the date is the twenty-second of the winter of 1987.

The person buried under the yew tree is not Emrys Sarn. The name was miscarved in 1990 and the family did not know until March of this year, and the family that bought the plot is a family of two sisters who are now both dead, and their one nephew has come forward because of the Advertiser.

The nephew is fifty-nine. He has never met Aurel Sarn.

* * *

The first consequence was the bridge.

On the ninth of March, two days after the piece, the parish council put a card up at the Cove Cut point, because a walking group had asked for it, because the piece said there was a rope bridge at that point and eleven people had gone to look for it in the following forty-eight hours and had gone over the fence to look at the drop.

The drop is thirty-two feet to the water and the water is eleven feet deep at the state and the state is always, and there is no rail.

The parish council is an unpaid body of nine people in a village hall and it cannot fence anything, because fencing is the water authority's, and the water authority's engineers came out on the nineteenth and put a temporary barrier up in four hours and were very polite about it.

It is a foot of steel and boarding across a path that a hundred and forty people a week now walk because the Advertiser told them there was a bridge there.

* * *

The second consequence was the brother.

On the eleventh of March a man came into the Advertiser's office in Weaver Lane and asked whether Aurel Sarn had a family.

He is a schoolteacher in Corve and he is fifty and his name is Emrys Sarn, and he had read the piece on his phone on the Sunday and had driven the forty miles on the Sunday night to see whether somebody had written his name on a grave.

The Advertiser gave him a copy. He read the four lines about the drowning and put the copy down on the counter and said that his brother had been eleven and had been at his aunt's in 1987 and had not seen water.

Then he asked for the reporter, and Perrin Quist came out of the back, and he said that he was not angry about anything except the name, and that a man died at that point in the water in the winter of 1987 and it was not his brother, and that somebody's mother was entitled to know that.

* * *

On the fourteenth, in the Advertiser, in a box on page two, under CORRECTIONS, Perrin Quist published four hundred words.

She said she had taken the brother from a parish magazine for the year the family bought the plot, where a woman had written that the family had lost a child. She said she had taken the bridge from a man who ran the boat hire and who had told her that the two boys who went off the rock in 1987 had been cousins and that one of them had got across the Cut on a rope afterwards, which is a thing people did then, and which he believed, and which he has since been shown was in a newspaper in the county town in the August of 1987 with a photograph of two boys on a line across the Cut and a rope in their hands.

They were not on a bridge. They were on a line across the Cut with a man holding the other end, and the man was their uncle, and the piece had already been a lie in the county town when it was a lie in Halversgate.

* * *

The photograph of Aurel Sarn on the Cove Cut bridge is the fourth thing that is wrong and it is not the writer's fault at all.

The Advertiser had a picture of a man on a bridge and Aurel Sarn is the only man anybody in Halversgate has heard of, so the picture went in.

The bridge in the photograph is the railway bridge at Cove Head, four hundred yards upriver of the Cut, and it has been there since 1847, and it is a stone bridge with three arches, and the man in the photograph is standing on it.

It is not Aurel Sarn. Perrin Quist has never been able to establish who it is, and neither has the Advertiser, and the man who took it in 1998 sold it for a pound and does not work here any more.

* * *

The third consequence happened on the fifteenth and it was the worst one.

The Advertiser runs a schools column, and the schools column runs a competition in the spring, and Perrin Quist's editor set a hundred-word question for the two secondary schools in the borough: what would you do if you had a bridge of your own.

Forty-one children wrote in and eleven of them wrote about Aurel Sarn and Emrys and the rope, and the winner was a girl of fourteen from the school at Tarnwick who wrote about a boy called Emrys Vaughan who had drowned in the water by the end of the Cut and whose mother had been forty miles away, and who had made it up the paper's version of his death because her name was in the parish magazine.

The prize was a book token and a printed certificate and the girl's mother rang the Advertiser at half past four to ask whether it was safe for her to accept it.

* * *

What happened next took eleven weeks and it was not about any of the three.

Ruben Teal read the correction on the fourteenth and drove into Halversgate on the Saturday, and he came into the Advertiser's office on Weaver Lane and asked to speak to Perrin Quist.

She has written since that he was the most straightforward conversation of her working life, and that it lasted half an hour, and that he did not want a retraction, because there was nothing in the piece about him to retract.

He wanted the nine minutes to have his name on them. He said that he had been a rope-work man for twenty-six years and that he had not told anybody he had gone on the ice that day, because he was twenty-four and because nobody asked, and that he had been not telling people for twenty-eight years.

“There were four men in that pumping station,” he said. “Your man had the photograph and I had the ice, and there is nobody in this town who knows that both happened in the same week.”

* * *

And the fourth consequence was the one nobody expected, and it was the worst.

Aurel Sarn came back.

He came back on the seventeenth of March, on a Tuesday, and he came on the four forty bus from the county town, and he has not been on the four forty bus before and there is nobody alive in Halversgate who saw him arrive.

He came to the Advertiser's office at about eleven in the morning and he asked for Perrin Quist, and Perrin Quist has described the conversation in print and it is a thousand words, and the Advertiser put the whole thing on page three on the twenty-ninth of March.

He did not threaten anybody. He did not ask for a retraction. He said that the piece was mostly true and that the three errors were three errors and that they were hers and that she should say so, and that he had come about the fourth thing.

“The photograph is not a small one,” he said. “You put my face on a bridge that has nothing to do with me. That is not an error in a fact. That is an error in what a face is for.”

* * *

Then he did the arithmetic in the office, with Perrin Quist writing it down, and she printed it.

He is forty-six. He has gone in nine times, at one year each, which came off the end and not out of the middle, so he has thirty-seven years, which takes him to the twenty-second of March of the year his eighty-third birthday would have been in.

The Advertiser has checked that date three times with a different calculator.

He said that the pumping station in 1998 was the ninth, not the first. He said that the Advertiser had four of them on the record and that the quarry at Tarnwick in 2007 was the fifth, and that he had never told anybody and had not meant to.

He said that he had gone into the Tavlans barn in 2004 and had come out with a man called Iolo Pryce in it, and that the man had died in the spring of 2019, and that nobody had ever asked him who he had brought out.

* * *

The ninth time was the pumping station and it cost him a year.

The fourth thing that happened in March, after the picture, after the brother, after the school prize, after the rope work man, was that on the twenty-ninth, in the front room of the Advertiser's offices in Weaver Lane, Aurel Sarn said the number out loud for the first time, and Perrin Quist wrote it down and printed it in a thousand words of the doings of one week in her own town.

Nine in, nine years, thirty-seven left, and a photograph of his face on a bridge four hundred yards from where he was ever in the water, in a picture taken by a man who sold it for a pound.

* * *

What a town does with a printed origin is not a thing anybody voted on. Within three weeks the piece was being quoted at the bowls club, and by the end of April there were four accounts of how it began and all four had a man in them and only one of them had the man who had actually been there.

A bridge that was never built was quoted in the parish council minutes in March and struck out in the same document in April without explanation, and the reason is not in the minutes.

A brother who did not exist was written on a headstone in 1990 by a man who paid a mason, and the headstone is still there, and the nephew has asked the mason twice what it would cost to recut it and been told that a headstone of that age is not recut, it is ground and reset, and it is not worth doing.

And a rescue that went differently has been the correct version in Halversgate for forty-eight years before the seventh of March and has now been the correct version for eleven weeks, and both of them were in print, and the only difference between them is a photograph on page three of a paper nobody has ever stopped buying.
