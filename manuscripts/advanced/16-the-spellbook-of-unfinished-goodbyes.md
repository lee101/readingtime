# The Spellbook of Unfinished Goodbyes

![Cover art: The Spellbook of Unfinished Goodbyes](../art/advanced/16-the-spellbook-of-unfinished-goodbyes.webp)

On the morning the river broke its wall, Mara Vell was trying to forgive a dead man for choosing the wrong final word.

She stood in the damp vestry of Saint Orison, one hand on the little silver clasp that held her spellbook shut: the book was not large, but its blue cover had buckled once, and someone had traced a cracked constellation across it in fading gold; it weighed as much as a bucket of wet sand. In the pew behind her, Oris's sister argued with the priest about whether ashes should be buried or kept in glass; the argument had lasted half the ceremony, and Oris's body had not yet arrived. He was still somewhere beneath the broken east embankment, and his last unfinished words kept turning in Mara's mind.

Not a prayer, not forgiveness, just his narrow, hurried voice inside her head.

*Tell Lio I was afraid.*

Mara had been Oris's apprentice for three years; he had caught her stealing funeral notices from the dead-letter office because she wanted to practice binding on honest grief, and he had taught her that a finished letter and a finished person were not the same thing. People died mid-sentence, and entire relationships died quietly after the last train, years before the body followed, which was why the old binders called it a spellbook: it gathered words given to the air at the instant of death. It could not invent, explain, or improve; if a dying person fixed their last attention on someone living, the words appeared upon a page.

No courier could reach the dead; the book could.

Mara felt a tremor in the clasp, then another that did not come from Oris's unfinished sentence. Hundreds of small knocks rose against the cover, quick as hail on a winter roof.

Outside, a bell began to ring, and the priest stopped speaking while Oris's sister looked toward the high windows, where rainwater spilled from the broken eaves and struck the flagstones with a sound like applause. Mara opened the book.

Ink had flooded across the first forty pages, each line entered in a different hand, and the words did not drift or fade but pressed against one another as if their writers could hear the rising water and knew they had only one page left.

*Salt in my mouth. Tell Aya the net is under the west pier.*

*Not the blue cloak. He stole it. I knew and I kept quiet.*

*I am not afraid.*

*I wish I had asked her to stay.*

*Tell them the door was locked. I am sorry. I am not sure of what.*

*Mother, I did the thing you forbade.*

*Do not call it courage. It was only that I could not bear their faces.*

Mara slammed the cover, but the knocks did not stop; they were coming from inside her, not as words yet but as the pressure of unfinished speech, as if hundreds of people had gathered behind a thin door and were testing the handle.

“Sister,” the priest said. “What is it?”

“The river has breached,” she said. Her voice sounded calm because her hands had begun to shake. “The low wards are gone. The old post is flooding.”

People understood danger faster than magic: chairs scraped, coats were lifted from hooks, and Oris's sister caught Mara's sleeve before she could follow them outside.

“Will it find him?” she asked, and Mara looked at the book, whose silver clasp had turned black in her palm. “If he died with words for someone, yes.”

* * *

Bellwether woke to the smell of wet ash, because the river had broken through the eastern embankment at midnight, not with a roar but with a soft final crack, like a bone accepting a fracture. Water struck the slate quarter first, then the fishing steps and the Post Arcade; by dawn, three bridges were gone, and carts, fences, apple crates, and house doors floated in the current. The dead-letter office stood at the top of Carrier's Hill, its lower rooms dry while refugees crowded the stairway with blankets, toys, and salt-bright bundles of salvaged linen. Mara moved through them with the book strapped beneath her coat, and every few steps it warmed against her ribs.

In the receiving room, Acting Provost Senna Corr waited beside the Brass Index, a wheel of name-plates used to register the living dead: she was a narrow woman with ink stains on two fingers, a public commander's knife at her belt, and the Council's recovery clerks already sorting names behind her. “One hundred and eighty-seven confirmed,” a clerk called, “and fifty-three missing.”

Corr looked at Mara's coat. “The book.” “More than that,” Mara said. “It's taking the words.” Corr's expression did not soften; she simply said they had a use for Mara, offered no greeting or mention of Oris's funeral, and treated disaster as the practical language everyone in Bellwether now understood. Mara placed the book on the delivery slate, where its loosened binding sent a ribbon of black ink toward the receiving chute until Corr sealed the exit with wax, trapping the last line. Under her fingers lay the emergency counters, nine hundred blank squares marked for signatures.

“Order Seven,” Corr said, “permits a binding apprentice in a civil emergency to release authentic last words to every registered living addressee, because the Council understands that grief will not wait for forms.” “Registered addressees are the families named in obituaries,” Mara replied, “not the people those words were meant for.” The families could deliver them, Corr said—to a spouse, a child, a creditor, or someone who believed the dead no longer wished to speak—but Mara watched Corr's gaze hold steady as she called those distinctions a luxury in a drowning.

The book struck the slate hard enough to crack the wood, and behind Corr, a young courier named Ione Sar lifted her soaked cap. She had been Mara's friend since their first year at the post, when they raced sealed letters down the archive stairs, but Ione had lost her mother and brother to the flood and had also heard the words leaking from the book. “My mother is in there,” Ione said. “Isn't she?”

The room quieted; a woman near the stair turned, two old men looked down the delivery aisle, and Mara turned the book so no one else could see the page. There were no names of the dead in it, only the words themselves and, after each entry, a thin address-thread that would seek the intended recipient if the binding were opened. She could feel one thread pointing toward Ione, another across the river toward the prison hospital, and hundreds more reaching toward strangers, enemies, estranged children, and people not yet found. “I don't know,” Mara said. “I can't tell from a page.” Ione absorbed that without flinching and told her to find out.

Corr pushed the Order of Emergency Release across the slate and explained that the binding would become unstable at sunset, unopened pages would lose their address at moonrise, and nine hundred voices would vanish because the binders were too slow. Mara asked why that was true, and Corr said it was because the law said so. “That isn't a reason,” Mara told her, but Corr replied that it was the reason she had been taught.

Mara looked at the signature beneath the order, where Oris had witnessed the emergency statute years earlier, on the day the Guild revised its rules after a mine collapse erased forty-one people before the rescue crews arrived; his signature was small and upright, and he had never believed rules were the same as reasons. Corr pushed the brass seal toward her and offered two choices: one turn of the key would open every voice to every registered household, or Mara could hold the book while people came one by one, though three hundred were already waiting and there would be more by sunset than she could name. “What happens to the ones whose living person refuses?” Mara asked, touching the clasp. “They have lost a person,” Corr replied. “Are you protecting them from grief, or protecting yourself from hearing it?”

The first bell of warning sounded over the river, and Mara took the Order without signing it.

* * *

The binders lived below the receiving room because binding taught you not to trust clean air, and Mara descended into a narrow chamber of stone shelves and copper pipes while rain hammered the high ventilation grate. She lit the damp-line lamp, set the spellbook on the circular table, and found Oris's sentence waiting exactly as she remembered it.

*Tell Lio I was afraid.*

Below it, in letters that shook with cold, Oris had added a postscript: *The word is not a confession; it is a door, and you must not walk through it for me.* Mara pressed two fingers against the words, but they did not glow, because dead words never did. The spellbook had no gift for moving the dead, only the exact arrangement of a living person's attention when time ended, so doubt, mischief, and the last frightened joke all looked equally permanent on the page.

A knock came at the ladder. “You ducked the signing,” Ione said from above, but Mara told her she had missed the warning bell. “I heard it over my mother's name.” Mara heard it too, wrapped in the greenward tone: *Kesh, keep the south candle. The floor is rotten by the flour bins. You never listen. I—* Ione climbed down in dry clothes, though water still shone in her hair.

“The Corps lists a woman called Sava Iren as missing,” Ione said, explaining that the page had no name but its thread pointed here and tightened when she put her hand near it. Mara told her that threads recognized living addressees. “My mother's thread did, and so did my brother's,” Ione said. “Both were addressed to me.”

A sharp knock came from inside the cover and bent the lamp flame toward it, but Ione did not step back. She asked what would happen if Mara opened her page, and Mara said she would hear the dead exactly as binding permitted, while Ione wanted to know whether everyone would hear at once. “Everyone the Order names,” Mara replied, so Ione considered the answer, worried a loose thread at her cuff, and told her to find another way. Mara allowed that there might be one, as there usually was with Master Oris, and at the word *usually* the loose thread snapped. Ione looked at her hand and began to say she had meant no harm, but Mara told her that her name was enough.

They turned to the last forty pages, where address-threads crossed the margins and reached toward doors across the city, and Mara noticed thin blank leaves pasted beneath the dead words, one for every voice, each headed RETURN. Oris had shown her binding leaves a hundred times, but these were new; they could catch a sent spell when a target moved and send a question back to the caster, and no magic remained in them yet because they were only waiting for a reply. Ione asked why she had not seen them, and Mara explained that Oris had added them the previous month. At the bottom of the spine she found a note in his cramped writing, then read it twice and recognized the code beneath it: nine dots, a broken line, nine dots, the Ring Measure by which every receiving door in Bellwether could be opened. Oris had left her a way, but he had also left her the danger.

“If I sound the Ring Measure,” Mara said, “every post horn and bell in the city can announce a choice, so people can accept, refuse, or wait to understand.” Ione asked whether the book could carry that many answers, and Mara said it could if they bound them to reply leaves. “And if someone opens a letter without permission?” Ione asked; Mara warned that the person in danger was the one who read it, and the other dead voices might spill into the bell network. Ione looked toward the stairs as someone began pounding on the receiving-room door, then warned that sounding the measure too soon would panic the city. If they waited, Mara replied, Corr would open every voice at sunset, and Ione's jaw tightened as she declared that they needed a third option.

Below them, far down in the flooded cellars, a heavy lock released with a boom. “The Return Chain,” Mara said, explaining that its brass links had not carried a letter for ninety years and connected the Book of Names to the Address Stone holding the living city's consent seal; a courier could reach the old keeper's chamber, but the stair tunnel was filling with riverwater. Ione knew a way in: the roof. “The roof is under six feet of water,” Mara said. “Then you won't have to swim.”

* * *

They took the roof, where Mara buckled the spellbook into a waterproof courier frame while Ione tied a line around her waist and lowered herself one-handed from the eaves onto the crest of the drowned post horn. The river ran black and foaming between the buildings, broken chairs ticked against one another below, and a dog barked somewhere nearby before falling silent.

Mara followed, balancing on the slanted horn beam while the current lifted her boots, until a red chair leg struck the frame and the spellbook's clasp sprang open.

A voice tore through the rain: *Mara.* She nearly fell when the message continued, *Mara, I can see the book. Do not come to the east gate.* It was not Oris, but a real last message carried on a new thread from an unfinished page, and the bell network had awakened. *Don't trust—* The next word tore away, Mara's ears rang, and on every roof windows rattled as the unfinished sentence tried to complete itself.

“Hold the cover shut,” Ione called from the horn, and Mara slapped the blue boards together even though the clasp had fallen into the river and the binding would scatter by nightfall without it. Hundreds of pale lines pressed through the seam until, for a moment, a woman's voice seemed to rise from inside Mara's chest: *Run.* It was her mother's voice, then a stranger's, then Mara's own, older and breathless, warning someone not to listen, and she held the book until it passed.

The Address Stone stood at the tip of the bell tower, wrapped in blackened ivy, a waist-high circle of white granite veined with copper, with nine holes around its face and the emergency seal Corr had already set in the eighth. When Mara touched it, an official order pulsed through her fingers: *Release all authentic last words to registered addressees.* Below, the bell horns sounded one after another across Bellwether; although the Ring Measure was still inactive, the city was ringing with warnings, citizens climbed roofs and answered cries that were not calls, and someone fainted in the low quarter.

A man opened his dead wife's sealed message before the rescue workers had finished removing her from the river, and her final accusation rang from every public horn at once: *You knew I was afraid. You made me go anyway.* The crowd fell silent while Mara felt the accusation enter the spellbook and find its thread, becoming one of the nine hundred voices that was no longer private or consentless, yet no longer addressed only to one person. Corr had found a faster route to release, but Ione's wire knife could not turn the emergency seal, which locked from the city side where Corr kept the master key. When Corr reached the tower stair, Ione hooked the key from her belt and caught it in her palm; she passed it to Mara as the river shifted and a surge knocked loose a section of parapet. The stone tilted, Mara caught the frame as her boot slipped, and for one suspended instant she hung over black water holding Oris's unfinished words until Ione pulled the line taut.

“Address Stone,” Ione gasped. “Do it now.” With Corr's master key in her palm, Mara pressed both hands to the granite, where the binding apprentice's mark opened beneath the emergency seal not as a command but as a question: *Do you receive?* The bell network answered before Mara chose; across the city, hundreds of little brass reply leaves trembled in their housings, doors marked by living names opened on the Answer Stair, and the Return Chain pulled taut beneath the river. Mara felt every intended recipient offer the same empty response, not no to the words but no to the choice made for them, and drove Corr's key through the emergency seal until her order cracked and the bell tower rang the Ring Measure.

* * *

For one breath there was no sound, and then every post horn in Bellwether spoke in a human voice, repeating the words the old measure demanded: *Letters have arrived. The dead cannot open the door. Each living person must choose.* The message carried in nine variations, from the first ward bell to the poorhouse beyond the river, lifting corners of answer paper from windows and blank leaves from open doors; it was not a spell to compel a reply, but a spell to refuse compulsion.

The book fought her: pages struck the frame like moths trapped against glass, address-threads snapped taut and dragged warmth from her hands, and one pull nearly turned the spellbook toward the city while she thought of Oris waiting at the bottom of the river for a word she had not found. She wedged it against the stone and opened to the first reply leaf.

“I accept.” A woman's hand had written it in wet ink: roof carpenter Nessa Ro, receiving her brother's apology for leaving their mother's tools locked away. She was crying, but the words in the book remained unchanged, because the dead could not revise, appease, or explain and the living could only choose to witness them. Ione took the second leaf. “I accept. Give me my mother.” A third followed from the prison hospital: *I refuse. I love my father, but he does not own my ears.* The book did not burn, forgive, or destroy; it listened.

Below the tower, Mara heard Corr shout for the Wardens as boots struck the drowned stairs, and she understood what the Acting Provost had seen in the Ring Measure: not a release anyone could govern, but a loss of control by those who had governed too long. Mara bound each answer to its thread while papers folded in the fourth ward and opened in the low quarter, some people simply held their blank pages, and others tore them. One small message came from a child: *I don't want her last words, but I want to say goodbye. Is there another way?* Mara read it twice and her throat closed, but she understood the distinction the dead had not erased: their words existed without living permission, and the living could refuse to hear, answer, or wait where a blank page offered room for something wanted to be said.

A boot struck the stair door, and Corr's voice came through the rain: “Open the door, apprentice.” Mara kept binding while Corr warned that every page she held was a page Corr could have delivered. On the table lay Oris's letter, and Mara had not written a reply because opening it would mean hearing him afraid, a sentence she had long avoided because fear made his death seem less important than the knowledge he had hidden. Yet fear had not been waiting for her approval; Oris had left the words, and she could choose whether to meet them.

Corr's knife entered the gap between door and frame, and Mara wrote on his blank leaf: *I open this door.* The book warmed under her hands. *Tell Lio I was afraid,* Oris said, and his voice was smaller than she remembered, without thunder or final wisdom, only a frightened man on a stair below the east gate wondering whether an emergency order he had once witnessed would be called mercy again. Then he told her that Lio was his sister, that her real name was Elianora, and that he had asked her not to speak after Mara's first year; he was angry when she did, never told Mara, and now had no clean way to explain. Mara's eyes blurred because she had expected an apology, but instead the dead man gave her the shape of his silence and left her to decide what to do with it. *If she comes, I want to know,* he said. *I do not want to make her forgive me.* Then his voice faded.

Corr struck the door, and the lock tore loose as Mara placed Oris's answered leaf against his page, which the book marked RECEIVED, no more and no less. She lifted the addressed leaves one at a time and sang their paths into the Return Chain, which pulled the messages through the drowned office and the wards into the hands of their living names. The spellbook went slack in her arms.

Corr came through the doorway with rain in her gray hair and looked at the pages. “All of them?” she asked, and when Mara said no, Corr demanded to know how many. “Enough answered; the rest remain dead letters.” “Dead letters are failures,” Corr said, but Mara held up a blank reply leaf and reminded her that they were also letters without a living reader before placing the Address Stone in her custody. For a moment the Acting Provost looked old, then she took the leaf, accepting not forgiveness but custody. From that moment her name was attached to every unanswered voice, obliging her to keep them rather than spend them.

Outside, the river continued to rise; Bellwether still had missing sons, ruined bridges, and a hundred arguments about who deserved the truth, because nothing in the tower had made grief tidy or stopped the living from mistaking possession for love. At dawn the emergency horn sounded once, and couriers stepped into the streets with satchels of blank reply leaves, carrying them to every address connected to the book while the spellbook rested in a dry room upstairs, closed for the first time since the flood. No dead voice rang over Bellwether. Every carrier carried the same question instead: *You may open this. You may not. Both answers will be kept.*
