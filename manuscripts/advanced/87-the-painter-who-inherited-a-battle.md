# The Painter Who Inherited a Battle

![Cover art: The Painter Who Inherited a Battle](../art/advanced/87-the-painter-who-inherited-a-battle.webp)

Oskar Vane was thirty-one years old and had been asked to paint a battle, and the first thing he did, before he packed, was to ask what year it was fought in, and the second thing he did, when he was told that it had not been, was to ask what shape the ground was.

The estate of Harrow kept its records at Caer Vane, in a long room with a slate floor, and the room was the exact width of the conveyance it existed to document, which was nine feet, and nobody in the house could say why. At the far end, under glass, lay the Harrow Wager, a conveyance by contest, granting the vale of Harrow and its two hundred acres to whichever house should take it by force of arms, and the instrument had one term in it, ninety days, and a condition, that no battle shall be held to have begun until its Record of Contest is entered by the registrar.

“It has not begun,” said the registrar, a small dry man named Cobb who had been registrar of the Harrow estate for twenty-two years. “That is the position. It was won in 1402 and it has not begun, and the term does not run from the winning, it runs from the Record, and there has never been a Record, because the Record is the evidence, and you cannot have evidence of a thing that has not happened.”

“So a war is being fought,” Oskar said, “by not having been fought.”

“It is being administered,” Cobb said. “You will find the administering is the same work. Your plate is the second of three. The first is the ground. The second is the arms of the two sides. The third is the result, and Master Gault has the third, and Master Gault has had it for six years.”

* * *

The ground was two days’ walk east, and it was not what a battle looked like. It was a valley floor of thin grass and old stone, the Ard Slant, with a hedge on one side and a dry beck in the other, and the only sign that men had ever intended to kill each other on it was a row of chalked pegs at forty paces, and a stake with a tally board nailed to it, and a bench.

At the beck end there was a farm, and a man was mending a gate with a new upright, and he was seventy-four, or the roll said he was, and when Oskar asked him about 1402 the man said his grandfather had told him the Ridge side came over the hedge at the third hour and his grandfather had been in the hedge with a scythe, and that was the whole of his grandfather’s account and he had no reason to improve on it.

That was the first of the two hundred and eleven. Oskar counted them over four days, because the roll said two hundred and eleven and the roll was a roll, and a painter who paints a number has painted a claim, and a claim can be examined.

He found them in fields, in shops, in beds. He found a cooper, fifty-one, who took his afternoon off to be measured. He found nineteen men at a funeral in Harrow village, and the funeral was for a man who had died of a lung, and the estate paid the coffin, and the estate sent no flowers because flowers were not on the schedule. He found two men playing draughts in a barn, and one of them asked whether the painter was going to put them in the picture, and when Oskar said he did not know, the man said that he would rather be in it than not, because the not-in was a place a man could be in for a hundred and three years.

He found the nine-year-old, Wen Corry, on the fourth day, in a stubble field with a stick, driving nothing. The boy was the grandson of the man who had lost the Harrow Wager, which Oskar had not known and which Cobb had not mentioned, and the boy was not on the roll, because the roll was the victor’s roll, and the boy’s family had kept the other side of the valley for a hundred and three years by being the losing side on paper.

“Your grandfather lost,” Oskar said, because he was thirty-one and he said things.

“He says he won,” the boy said, “and he says the other one was his fault, and he says it every day, so it is a thing he says.” The boy looked at the pegs on the grass. “Are those the places?”

“They are the places.”

“Are you going to paint us fighting?”

“No,” Oskar said. “That is not what I am painting.”

* * *

The four painters worked in a long room above the barn with the north light, and the room was divided by a line of chalk on the floor that nobody was permitted to cross with a wet brush, and on one side of the line Master Gault had been painting the same third plate for six years.

Gault was seventy-three and painted horses and had done three of them, and the third was on the floor of the room face up, eleven feet long, and it was the finest thing Oskar had ever stood near. In it the Ridge came over the hedge at the third hour. There were forty men on the near side and thirty on the far, and a captain in the middle with his sword not drawn, because a drawn sword in a conveyance plate was a fault, since the conveyance transferred land and not blood, and blood was paid separately.

“It is wrong,” Oskar said, on the fourth evening, before he had any idea that saying so was a professional act.

“It is admissible,” Gault said. “That is a different property and it is not a lesser one. Look at the hedge.”

The hedge was the best passage in the painting. Gault had put a small hesitation in it, a man on the near side whose foot had turned in the mud, one degree of awkwardness in an otherwise forward-facing line, and it was so exactly right that Oskar stood over it for a quarter of an hour.

“You have made him hesitate.”

“I have made him pay for it,” Gault said. “He is the reason the ground is not in the picture the way the registrar would like it.”

Anse Kelp, who was thirty-nine and painted signs and letters, said from the other end of the room that the result plate had been commissioned in the reign of the last king and that a painter was paid by the plate and not by the war, and that a painter who did not understand the difference ended up painting churches for nothing. Rue Overy, who was twenty-two and painted doors and had a good hand for a wet edge, said nothing at all, because she was the only one of the four who had not asked what year the battle was fought in.

* * *

General Sir Alaric Noll was eighty-four years old and came down to the barn on the sixth day, and he came alone, and he sat on the bench at the beck end of the Ard Slant while Oskar worked, and he did not look at the field so much as at the pegs.

“That is a remarkable thing, you know,” he said, after a while. “They are in the correct positions.”

“They are.”

“I have wanted to see that since I was nineteen.”

Oskar put down his brush, because a man of eighty-four who has wanted something for sixty-five years is not a thing to talk over. The general had thin hands with the skin loose at the back, and he had brought a stick he did not need, and he was looking at the forty paces between the pegs as though they were an account he had been auditing his whole life.

“My grandfather was nineteen when he took this valley,” the general said. “He was a boy, and he had a bad horse, and he came over that hedge at the third hour, and there were thirty on the far side, and he won. And then a solicitor came out from Caer Vane the next morning, a man named Wardle, and told him the Wager was in progress, and would he be so good as to keep his men under arms and not to farm the Vale, because the estate could not pay the roll until the Record was entered, and the Record could not be entered until the battle was over.”

“He kept it like that for ninety days.”

“He kept it like that for ninety days, and then for nine years,” Noll said, “because when the ninety days ended the battle had not been recorded, and a battle that is not recorded has not been won, and a battle that is not won has not ended, and so the term did not run, and so the estate could not pay, and so I was brought up on the money this valley earns by not existing.” He put the stick across his knees. “He died at eighty-four. He died in the room upstairs with the ledgers on a table by the bed, and the last thing he said to Wardle was that it was still going on and that he was losing, and he said it that morning, and Wardle said yes, your grace, and it was not going on and he was not losing and he had not been losing for sixty-five years. The battle is a machine for turning a family into the administration of a wound, and my family is still in it, and I have one thing left to do about it, and it is due in eighty-one days.”

“What is it?”

“The record,” Noll said. “I have no authority. I have a chair, and a stick, and four painters, and a solicitor who is right about everything.”

* * *

Cobb was right about everything. He was right that the conveyance was sound, and that the term was ninety days from the entry of the Record, and that the entry required three plates, and that a plate not made was a defect, and that a defect in a conveyance operated as a stay, and that a stay was why the Wager was alive. He was right that the estate paid two hundred and eleven men a wage in the third week of every month, and that it had paid them in the third week of every month for a hundred and three years, and that they were not soldiers, and that he had never once called them soldiers in a document.

He was also right that Oskar’s plate could not be a battle.

“It is not admissible,” Cobb said, on the fourteenth day, reading Oskar’s first sketch from end to end without moving his face. “A result plate is a plate of the result. It is the finding, and the finding is a comparison. Yours is a landscape with people in it.”

“It is what is there.”

“It is what is there and it is not what the instrument asks for, and if you enter it the registrar will throw it out, and the Record will be incomplete, and the Wager will continue.” Cobb set the sketch down. “I am not asking you to lie. I am telling you that a frame does not have to lie in order to lie, and that you are a good enough painter to have noticed that before I did.”

“And if I paint a landscape with two hundred and eleven men in it and it is thrown out?”

“Then on the ninetieth day the Record is incomplete, the Wager has no result, the term expires, and the land reverts to nobody,” Cobb said. “Which is what your general wants. And which means that in four weeks I must open a new payroll, and there are two hundred and eleven men in this valley who have not been told that, because a registrar is not permitted to say a thing before it is entered.”

That was the fight, and it went on for three weeks, and it was not a fight with Cobb, who gave Oskar everything he asked for and put it in writing, and it was not a fight with Gault, who had six years of the finest painting Oskar had ever stood near and no reason on earth to spoil it. It was a fight with a document.

Oskar painted the men. He painted the cooper and the nineteen at the funeral and the two in the barn, and he painted the gate-mender at ninety-four, and he painted the stubble field with the pegs, and when he came to the fourth day he painted the boy with the stick driving nothing, and the boy stood still for eleven minutes, and then said that a picture of nothing was not a picture, and Oskar said that it was the only one that was admissible, and the boy said admissible was a stupid word to use about a field, and Oskar agreed with him, and they sat down in the stubble.

On the sixty-first day the general came out to the field at dusk with the stick and asked him, without preamble, how much of it was finished.

“Most of it,” Oskar said.

“How many are in it?”

“All of them.”

The general stood in the stubble for a while. “Then it will not be entered,” he said. “And on the ninetieth day the term expires and the vale reverts and Cobb closes the payroll, and the men will be paid off, and some of them will have been paid off in a hundred and three years of a war that was over in an afternoon when a boy with a bad horse came over a hedge.”

“Yes,” Oskar said.

“Then you have painted a thing that cannot be used, on purpose, having understood it, and I have to say to you that you are the first person in this house in a hundred and three years who has.”

He walked back up the track toward Caer Vane, and stopped once, and came back.

“If you had painted Gault’s hedge, I would have got nothing,” he said, “because a correct record of a war that was won is a receipt, and I have had a hundred and three years of receipts.” He put the stick into the stubble. “Mine is a thing nobody can file. That is all I have left to spend.”

* * *

The painter submitted on the eighty-ninth day, in the long room with the slate floor, in the presence of the registrar, the two stewards, Master Gault, and the general, who stood at the end of the table with his hands behind his back.

Cobb read the entry aloud. He read the first plate, the ground, correctly, and the second plate, the arms of the two sides, which Gault had painted in the fourth year of his life and which nobody had looked at since. Then he came to the third, and he read the heading, and he read the words under it, and then he read them a second time, because the registrar of the Harrow estate was a careful man and had a duty to be sure.

The third plate was nine feet long and it was the Ard Slant on an ordinary afternoon in high summer, with the hedge on one side and the beck on the other, and the chalked pegs at forty paces, and the tally board, and the bench. There were men in the field and men at the gate and men in the yard of the farm, and one man mending a gate with a new upright, and nineteen of them were at a funeral in Harrow village and were not in the picture, because they were not there, and a note at the foot of the plate said so.

“It records no contest,” Cobb said.

“It records the ground,” Oskar said.

“It is not a finding of the instrument,” Cobb said, “it is a statement of conditions, and the registrar has no power to enter a statement of conditions in place of a finding, and I am required to refuse it.”

“Then refuse it,” Oskar said. “Enter it anyway and let the refusal sit on top of it.”

They looked at the registrar. The registrar was seventy-one and had entered four hundred records and had never been asked to do a thing like that, and what he said was that the instrument provided for a finding of victory, a finding of defeat, and a finding of continuance, and that there was no box on the form for a finding of neither, and that a form could be amended by the officer who keeps it at the time of the entry, and that he kept it.

The finding of neither was written out at the foot of the plate. Then the entry was sealed, and the term was satisfied, and the Wager was recorded as drawn, which is a category, and which no one had ever used, and which had been in the form since 1402 in a box that was printed small.

The term expired at noon on the ninetieth day. The land reverted to nobody, because there was no victor, and the vale went back into the hands of the fief, and the two hundred and eleven names came off the roll, and the registrar entered it in the ledger in four words: Drawn. No victor. Reverted.

* * *

The last plate of the Harrow Wager is the field on an ordinary afternoon, and it is not signed by the artist, because the instrument did not admit his name, and it is kept in Caer Vane behind glass that is scratched in one corner, where a boy had once leaned on it.

In the middle distance, at the beck end, there is a chair on the stubble with a stick laid across it, and an old man sitting in it, and he is eighty-four, and he is watching a field, and in the near foreground a boy of nine is driving a stick at nothing, alive.
