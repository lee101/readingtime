# The Girl Who Ran Faster Than the Sirens

![Cover art: The Girl Who Ran Faster Than the Sirens](../art/accessible/135-the-girl-who-ran-faster-than-the-sirens.webp)

Priya was twelve years old, with long black hair in two plaits and a red woollen hat with a hole in it, and she could always beat the siren to a call.

The siren was not a siren to her. It was Coll, who was sixty-one and had been going up Beacon Hill since Priya's father was a boy, and she set the pace from the top of the tower.

The price was in Priya's legs. Her body had one gear. When she stopped she went on by herself for exactly as far as she had run since she set off, at the speed she had been going, so the faster she went the further past the door she got.

Halversgate had forty-one names on the running list, and Tam Ferrier kept the book at the depot. Beside each name there was a house and beside the house a telephone number, and if the number was blank the runner had to knock.

Priya was on the list because Bev had signed her card in the spring and written twelve on it in her small hand. The rules were inside the front cover. Rule four said a runner under fourteen should not be sent out alone, and rule nine said the runner who gets there first waits at the door and does not go in unless there is fire. She was fast and she waited at the door.

* * *

The horn went at twenty past four on a Thursday in May while Priya was sitting on the wall outside the library, and she was at the top of Shingle Row before the sound had finished. Half a mile in two minutes and forty seconds, a hundred times over.

Then she got to the maltings on Ropewalk Lane and the whole street was out in the road in its coats.

* * *

The smoke was coming out of the maltings chimney in a long flat grey line and there was a drill behind it. Bev was at her own gate in her nightgown. “I have rung the brigade and the paper,” she said.

They called it the third day of the barley drill. There was no fire anywhere in Halversgate that Thursday.

Priya got to the gate and then her body went on with her, because she had a mile and three-quarters in her legs by then. It took her up Ropewalk and past the allotments to the second bridge, a mile and a bit from the gate, and she stood at the rail until she could stop. It took nine minutes and she did not know where it had gone.

* * *

Tam Ferrier wrote the times in the book that night, and ruled a line under the last one and wrote RETURNED LATE against it in capitals.

The county paper calls that red mark a deficiency, and it goes on the runner's card for two years.

Priya read that page about eleven times. She worked out that the house she would have knocked last was on the far side of the Cut, where Mrs. Aldwin lives with her windows shut. She never told anybody what the mile out was worth.

* * *

On the ninth of July the horn went at ten past two and she was at the door of the house on Shingle Row before the last of the sound. That time it was fire. The men had a ladder up by the time she had done three doors. Then her body went on with her.

It took her a mile and a bit, over the old mine tip and in at the allotments, and forty minutes to come back in. By then the street was out in blankets and Mrs. Aldwin was on her own doorstep. “A child came and told me to come out,” she said, “and told me twice.”

Coll came down off the hill at four o'clock and let Tam Ferrier give her the times, and then said the runner had got there two minutes ten before the engine and a minute fifty before a man from the brigade.

* * *

Coll said the town needed two horns and no runner, and Tam Ferrier wrote that in the running book.

Coll said one horn was enough for a call and a call was for everybody and not for one fast person. And she said that if the town ever wanted a runner again it would be a runner with a card and a rule four and somebody writing down the time she came back.

Priya's card has a red mark on it and it is still there, and the two horns go up the hill at a quarter past ten and a quarter past two, and nobody on Shingle Row mistakes the second for a runner.
