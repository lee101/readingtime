# The Good Giant of Little Fen

![Cover art: The Good Giant of Little Fen](../art/accessible/87-the-good-giant-of-little-fen.webp)

Little Fen was a village with a peat bank at one end, a stand of birch at the other, and a bog in the middle that nobody would put a spade in. In summer it smelled of water and sweet grass. In winter it smelled of water and iron.

All through the autumn the prints came.

They were not the prints of boots, or of sheep, or of badger. Fenella measured the deepest one with her father’s tape and it was the length of her arm.

“They are big,” said Kester.

“It is a monster,” said Kester. He said it every day after that, to hear how the word sounded.

* * *

She went after them on the third of November, when the bog was hard enough to walk on and soft enough to keep a boot print. She took a stick, because it helped her tell her own steps from the other ones.

She counted the prints. She found where the thing had turned, and where it had stood still a long while, and where it had gone back the way it came. It never once pointed at a door in the village.

* * *

She found him face-down in the heather behind the old sheep dip, with his arms out flat on the ground.

He was the size of the sheep dip. His coat was green, and it had been mended a hundred times.

“Are you hurt?” said Fenella.

“I have fallen over,” he said, into the heather. “I cannot get up. If I put a knee down I will break the ground, and then the water will come up, and then everybody will come and look at the water.”

“Then lie still,” said Fenella. “I am not going to tell anybody.”

* * *

She sat down in the heather beside his hand, which was as long as her own leg, and she waited.

“You are not frightened,” he said.

“I am a bit,” said Fenella. “But not of you.”

“Thank you,” said the giant. “That is a very hard thing to be not.”

* * *

He told her in a low voice, and he told it in bits, because there is a great deal to tell a person who has never once asked.

He came to Little Fen from a long way off, where the ground was hard and grey and the houses stood shoulder to shoulder. Every house he passed had somebody in it who wanted something. A fence mended for nothing. A gate lifted off its hinge. Somebody’s boy, because his sister was cross with him.

“So you come here,” said Fenella.

“I come here because nothing out here wants anything,” said the giant. “The bog takes my boot prints and says nothing back. The birds move over for me and say nothing back. The fen does not care how big I am, and that is the whole of what I have ever wanted.”

* * *

“When can you stand up?”

“When the ground is hard under my feet,” he said. “I cannot know that from lying down.”

“It is hard by the ridge,” said Fenella. “I have walked on it.”

He got up in three goes, with his hands flat on the dip wall. When he was standing, the top of his head was above the bare branches of the birch wood.

He looked down at Fenella. She had to tip her head right back to see his face.

“Nobody has ever stood with me before,” he said, and then he put his hand over his own mouth.

* * *

They had not got far along the ridge when Kester came up the track with four boys behind him.

He wanted a monster. He had told them how big it was.

He got about four feet away, and he stopped, and his jug came down on the path.

The giant looked down at him, and then at the boys, and he took a step back.

“I am not doing anything,” he said. “I am not here. Please go back to the village.”

Kester said his name twice. He asked the giant to lift the cart at the smithy, and to carry his little sister over the ford, and to stand behind the chapel at dusk so the men could see him. The giant answered yes to every one of them, in order, and his voice shook so that the heather shook.

“Stop asking,” said Fenella. “Go home.”

They went, but they did not go away. Two of them began to follow him along the path, calling out to see if he was real.

“I have to go,” he said to Fenella. “If I stay they will follow me all winter, and I will do whatever they ask, and then there will be more of them.”

“Where will you go?”

“I do not know,” he said. “I never do.”

Before he went he pulled up a clump of wet heather and gave it to her, and he did not say one word about it.

* * *

She found Kester by the peat bank on the Monday. He had been telling the village there was a thing in the fen, nine feet high, and that it had killed two sheep. The sheep were not dead, and everybody knew it.

“What did he give you?” said Kester.

“He gave me a handful of heather with the peat still wet in it,” said Fenella. “It means everything and it is worth nothing, and you are not to tell one person that you saw him.”

* * *

Fenella told the village there was no monster, and she was very good at it, and she did it for forty years.

There was a giant in Little Fen all that while. He came in the autumn and he was gone before the frost, and Fenella kept the wet places and the turned stones and the holes in the peat exactly as they were, because a man who has been looked after does not turn his back on the one who looked after him.

She never once changed her answer.
