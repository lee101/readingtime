# The Baker's Clock That Went Back One Hour

![Cover art: The Baker's Clock That Went Back One Hour](../art/accessible/97-the-baker-s-clock-that-went-back-an-hour.webp)

Sol was six, and she knew the shop better than her own hands. She knew the flour came on Friday in a sack, and the broom, the tin, and the peel.

She did not know how to read the clock.

It hung high over the door in a round wooden case, with a yellow face and a smear on the glass. It never moved, and she could not reach it.

* * *

One morning her father took his coat off the hook at the moment the little hand came up to the fat face.

“You are late,” said Sol.

“No,” said her father.

“It is four,” said Sol.

“It is four on that clock,” her father said, and he did not stop, and Sol stood in the flour and thought about it.

* * *

She asked the woman next door, the man with the cart, and Mr Ozdemir, who folded his apron and was stopping at the month’s end.

“The clock is fast,” they said.

“What is fast?”

“An hour fast,” said Mr Ozdemir. “It has been for eleven years. That is the tradition.”

“What does the tradition do?”

“It makes us an hour late. We do not argue with the clock, and you will not argue with it either.”

* * *

She thought about it for three days. Then she got up in the dark and stood in the shop with a broom and a candle, waiting for the clock to say four.

When the little hand came up to the fat face she put the broom down and dragged the tall ladder out from behind the flour bin. She could not hold both, so she left the candle on the sill and went up in the dark.

Up top it was warm. The glass was cold under her knuckles. She took the little hand between two fingers and walked it back, the way you walk a clock that has got too far, and did the same to the long one.

Then she sat on the top step with her feet hanging and looked at the face, and it was the right time. She got down and went to bed.

* * *

The men came at what the clock said was four, which was what the sky said. Sol stood on a flour sack.

The dough had been in its baskets one hour less than it had been in for eleven years. It lay slack and pale and did not fill the cloth. The man put it in and it came out flat and grey underneath. He made it again and it came out flat again. By midmorning there were four trays of pale bread on the counter and no smell in the shop.

The woman from next door came in and looked at it.

“I have a shop,” she said. “I have customers. What am I to give them?”

“It is the clock,” said the man. “I am very sorry. It is the clock.”

“It has been wrong for eleven years, and today I am the one that pays for it,” she said.

“We will pay for it,” said the man.

The bread went down to the poorhouse, which had never happened before.

* * *

Mr Ozdemir found her in the flour-bin room with a candle stub in her hand and her hair full of flour. He did not shout.

“You thought I would,” he said.

“Show me your hands,” he said.

She held them out.

“You have turned back the clock.”

“Yes,” said Sol.

“At four in the morning, in the dark, on a ladder, by yourself.”

“Yes,” said Sol.

He looked at her hands, then at her face, then up at the clock, and sat down on the sack of flour.

“Listen,” he said. “The dough is not bread yet. It is a slow thing, and it wants an hour when nobody is watching it. The clock takes that hour. It has taken it every morning for eleven years, and it is the only thing in this building that knows what the bread needs.”

“Did you fix it? When it started?”

“No. I told them to wind it,” he said. “They wound it every Sunday for eleven years, and it gained an hour every morning since. Now the shop is late and the bread is right. You cannot have one without the other.”

“From this day on you will wind it, and you will keep the extra hour, and it will be your job and nobody else’s, for as long as there is a clock in this shop.”

* * *

The clock still says four when the sky is black. Sol winds it on Sunday with the key from the nail, and she does not argue with it.

The village eats good bread. Nobody has ever worked out why.
