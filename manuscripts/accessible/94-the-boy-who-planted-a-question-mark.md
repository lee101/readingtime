# The Boy Who Planted a Question Mark

![Cover art: The Boy Who Planted a Question Mark](../art/accessible/94-the-boy-who-planted-a-question-mark.webp)

Aunt Merle kept the garage with a green door and a padlock the size of Ivo’s hand.

“That strip of earth behind the garage is not for anything,” she said on the first morning, and by Friday Ivo knew the words by heart.

“What is it for?”

“Nothing.” She shut the green door. “Hold this while I get the pump.”

But questions do not go away because you shut a door on them. They wait behind it with their coats on.

* * *

Then, in the wet week in March, Ivo found the tin box where his aunt kept her seeds. Inside it were little hooks of green plastic with a bend in the middle.

“These are not seeds,” he said.

He pushed them into the wet earth, one for every question, and said each one out loud as he planted it. Why does the chain click. Why was the sky pink on Sunday. Why does Aunt Merle flinch when anybody says the word sea.

“That last one is mine,” he said. “You can lean at me, Aunt Merle.”

Then he went in and washed his hands and told nobody.

* * *

By Friday there was a row of them.

Each one was a green stem with a hook at the top, and each stem leaned at somebody. They leaned the way a sunflower leans, only they leaned at people.

“Stop that,” said Ivo.

It leaned at him harder.

“Stop following me about.”

It leaned at him harder still.

Aunt Merle came out with a broom and stopped in the doorway.

“You planted questions,” she said.

“I planted seeds.”

“You planted questions. I have seen those before, once.” She looked down the row. “Do not say the one you have no answer to.”

* * *

The question about the sea came up faster than any of them.

It was taller by teatime, and it leaned at his aunt. When she carried a box out to the van it bent after her, and when she sat on the step it bent after her too.

“Aunt Merle, why do you never talk about the sea?”

“Do not.”

“You always tell me to stop talking about things because I am seven.”

“Not this one.”

“Why not this one?”

His aunt went on with her sweeping. “Because they all lean at somebody. That is how you can tell which ones are real.”

* * *

On the Saturday Ivo counted them out loud.

“One for the chain,” he said, poking each one. “One for the pink sky. One for the gate. One for Aunt Merle.”

“That is the gate one leaning at the postman, then,” said Aunt Merle from the step.

“And the grey dog one is leaning at the boy with the dog.”

“Seven hooks,” she said. “How many people?”

“Six. Me, you, Mrs Lomas, the postman, the boy with the dog, and the girl from number nine.”

“Seven people,” said Ivo. “Eight hooks.”

“That one is not like the others,” he said. “It is taller, and it is pale, and it is standing straight up.”

“Why is it standing up?”

“Because it is not leaning at anybody.”

“Aunt Merle, whose is that one?”

“Do not go near it.”

“But whose is it?”

* * *

He did not go near it. He watched it from the doorway, and it was taller every time he looked. It was his chest by teatime, and his shoulder by the time the light went.

“Who are you for?” he asked it.

Nothing came back. It was not asking anybody anything, and that was the trouble with it.

Then he stood beside it, and it leaned towards him, enough that he could feel it on his arm like a cold sleeve.

“All right,” he said. “You can have some water like the others.”

So he filled the can, and he walked the whole length of the row, and he watered every single one.

* * *

Aunt Merle caught him at the tap with the can still running.

“I did not tell you to do that.”

“You told me not to say it,” said Ivo. “You did not tell me not to water it.”

She stood in the doorway with her hand on the frame, and something that had been holding her face up let go of it.

“Water it,” she said. “Water it every morning, and I will not stop you.”

* * *

His aunt went out to the fence that evening and stood by the pale one, and Ivo watched her from the kitchen window with his tea going cold.

“That one is my mother’s, and I have been not asking it for thirty years,” she said, to the plant, not to him.

Then she came in and shut the door, and she never said a word about it again.

* * *

The row is still there, and taller than the garage door now. The bicycle one leans at Ivo wherever he goes, and the sea one follows his aunt down the path.

And at the far side, where the earth was never for anything, her mother’s question stands pale and straight and leans at nobody at all.

His aunt waters it every morning before she opens the green door. She has never answered it once. She waters it anyway, and it stays.
