# The Compass That Pointed Toward Friends

![Cover art: The Compass That Pointed Toward Friends](../art/accessible/17-the-compass-that-pointed-toward-friends.webp)

Nella saw the compass first.

It was lying on the attic floor between an old sewing tin and a cracked teacup, though the house belonged to her grandmother, who did not own a compass. When Nella touched the brass cover, it sprang open beneath her hand.

A needle inside began to spin.

“Nella?” called Oren from the attic stairs. “Did something fall over?”

She held up the compass. The red tip slowed, trembled, and pointed toward the kitchen. Then it swung past her and stopped at Oren.

Oren, who was her oldest friend, looked equally surprised. The needle spun away from him toward the round window.

“Maybe it likes you,” Nella said.

“It didn’t point at me.”

“It pointed near you.”

“That is not the same thing.”

A knock shook the front door. Nella’s grandmother stood in the kitchen holding crescent buns. An old mapmaker named Aunt Vess had sent them with a note.

It points to whoever most needs a friend, Vess had written. The bearer must help. Help need not mean carrying a ladder.

“That sounds specific,” Oren said.

A crash came from the lane.

They raced outside. Paper lanterns flew everywhere. One burned, one struck a water pump, and a third rolled downhill after a small boy in an oversized hat.

Nella grabbed his collar. Oren grabbed his hat. The crate shot past, leaving the hat stretched around the boy’s ears.

“I said keep the lid closed!” shouted a woman from the cart.

The boy climbed down. “Sorry. It slipped.”

Nella looked at the compass. Its needle pointed straight at him.

“Are you all right?” Nella asked.

“I’m fine,” he said. “I’m Wilo.”

“Wilo, you could have asked for help.”

Wilo rubbed his ears. “I thought they would laugh.”

He was new in town and working alone in a crowd. Oren found a ribbon in a lantern crate and tied it around Wilo’s bent brim.

“Wear it to the lantern walk,” Nella said. “Nobody will laugh now.”

“That is not a promise people can make,” said Oren.

“Then it will be a dare.”

Wilo’s uncertain smile made the needle dip. Nella began to trust it.


* * *

The needle next pointed toward the shutter house at the edge of the market.

Loud bangs shook the shutter house. Old Mrs. Vale hurried from window to window, fighting the wind with storm boards. A ladder leaned against the roof.

“I am not climbing that,” Oren said.

The compass pointed at him.

“Maybe you need someone to stop you,” said Nella.

Mrs. Vale opened the door. “Could you reach the attic latch? Its spring has broken, and I cannot leave the shutters.”

“Aren’t you just asking us to help?” Nella asked.

“I am asking. Too many people hear orders and forget that asking is a gift.”

Nella held the ladder while Oren climbed. The attic latch had jammed, and the wind worried the ladder sideways. Oren pushed it free and came down with paint on his sleeves and a red ribbon in his hair.

Mrs. Vale poured hot apple drink. To Nella’s surprise, she filled a third cup.

“A neighbor hasn’t spoken to anyone since moving here,” Mrs. Vale said. “I’ll take this across the road.”

The compass pointed after her.

Their retired neighbor, Mr. Fen, sat beside a cold hearth. Mrs. Vale joined him instead of asking him to repair something or tell travel stories. Oren handed over the cup. Mr. Fen held it in both hands and said thank you.

Nella watched the needle settle. It was a compass for needs too small to shout.

Back in the lane, Nella repaired lamps and carried a water bucket. At the well, people began calling for the compass.

“Could you loosen this wheel?”

“Could you find my nephew?”

“Could you fetch a doctor from the next village?”

Nella enjoyed being needed. The rescued people waved as if she had crossed a great distance instead of a muddy yard. The needle pointed again.

“No,” she said. “We’re not chasing it.”

“It isn’t pointing away,” Oren said. “It’s pointing at you.”

The needle followed when Nella handed it to him.

* * *

Nella’s first thought was that the compass had been damaged. Her second was that magic liked a good joke.

Oren did not laugh. He sat on the broad step outside the shutter house. “Where does it say you are?”

“I don’t know.”

“Try.”

She looked toward the market. “A few of the cooks invited me to help at their lantern-walk stalls.”

“Because you helped them today?”

“That is how you get invited.”

“So you don’t need an invitation?”

Nella picked at a splinter. For weeks, she had carried water, mended fences, and carried babies uphill so people would save her a place. When the market guild admitted new members, they chose children who knew names, shared tools, and never wasted anyone’s time.

“I thought if I was useful enough, they would see that I belonged,” she said.

Oren touched the compass. “I saw you.”

“That isn’t what I mean.”

“I know.”

Nella turned away, but Oren stayed. After a while, he pointed out the green band gathering over the western hills.

“The river workers will need lanterns,” Nella said. “We should close the road.”

“All of us?”

“All of us.”

They ran from stall to stall. Nella asked people to fetch ropes and lamps, then to choose jobs they felt able to do. Some said no. They were not wrong. Those who agreed formed a bright line along the road.

At the river, they met Wilo. He was tying his repaired hat to a post so the wind could not take it.

“I thought the lantern walk was canceled,” he said.

“It isn’t,” Nella told him. “It starts where it starts. Stay with us until then.”

The compass pointed toward the roof of the old bathhouse.

“Tamsin is there,” Oren said.

Nella stared at him. “You know her?”

“Mrs. Vale’s daughter. She came to the market today.” Oren’s mouth tightened. “A group put a paper crown on her and called her the Lantern Queen. Then they tore it up and joined the parade without her.”

Nella felt anger arrive, hot and clean.

“She was playing,” she said.

“She was pretending not to mind.” The needle swung firmly toward the bathhouse. “I heard her ask for a ride home. The carriage driver said his load was too full for one more person.”

Nella understood that cruelty, though no one had called it cruelty to her face.

* * *

Rain ran over the bathhouse roof, and thunder shook its abandoned chimney. A rope from the lantern frame swayed above the door.

Nella pointed. “We can tie this and climb.”

“The frame is rotten,” Oren said.

“Then we find another way.”

Inside, Oren made a loop with two carrying straps while Nella dragged a ladder outside. They tied the straps to the chimney, tested the knots, and climbed. Halfway up, the rotten lantern frame split, and their rope fell with a crack.

Below them, a voice called, “If you came to make me come down, you can go away.”

Tamsin sat beneath the highest window, her knees drawn up. Mrs. Vale’s key hung from one finger.

“We came to invite you to supper,” Nella called.

“What supper?”

“Any supper. We’ll choose it when we get there.”

“I don’t need food.”

“Nobody pointed to you because you needed food.”

Tamsin looked at Oren. “You are very bad at comforting people.”

“I know,” Oren said. “Nella fixed three lamps today.”

“Four,” Nella said.

A distant cheer rolled across the valley. The lantern walk had begun without them. Another followed as lights curved over the wet fields.

Tamsin wiped her face. “I was supposed to be useful. I would march first, and they would give me a cup. Then they were going to leave me alone on the platform.”

“No,” Nella said. “You are not a cup.”

“You want me to march anyway. Then everyone will laugh again.”

“We are not asking because you are useful,” Nella said. “We are asking because we want you with us.”

Tamsin stared down. “Really?”

“Really.” Nella held up the compass. “It points to whoever most needs a friend. All day, I thought helping was the same as caring. It pointed at me, and I was too scared to say what I needed. It does not mean you have to be brave, interesting, or useful.”

“What do I have to be?”

Nella thought of Mrs. Vale’s extra cup and Wilo’s ridiculous hat.

“Yourself,” she said. “We will wait for the rest of you.”

Tamsin’s key flashed. She opened the highest window. Below, the last procession lanterns waited at the turn.

“That is not a safe way down,” Oren said.

“You chose a rotten ladder,” Tamsin replied. “I am improving the plan.”

* * *

They reached the square when the sky opened.

Rain bowed the paper lanterns inward, though their flames still burned. People carried lights on broom handles, fishing poles, and children’s coats. Wilo walked beside Mrs. Vale, his hat decorated with the rescued ribbon. Mr. Fen had put the extra cup in a small cage to keep it dry. Other friends of the afternoon laughed and called directions.

Tamsin stepped into the lantern light. No crown appeared. No carriage came for her. Oren held out his carrying strap, and she took it.

Nella opened the compass. The needle did not settle on the hidden bathhouse, empty market, or silent river. It swung from one familiar face to another, resting on Wilo, Mrs. Vale, Mr. Fen, then Oren. When Nella closed the lid, it clicked like a gate opening.

At the center table, they left room for one more chair.

That night Nella understood. To be seen is not to be measured. A person can need company without carrying supplies, solving mysteries, or earning a place. A friend’s worth is not a job waiting to be done.

Nella put the compass in the middle of the table. Above it, lamps moved among the crowd, each small light joining another until no one had to shine alone.
