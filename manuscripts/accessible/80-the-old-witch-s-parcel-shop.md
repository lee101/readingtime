# The Old Witch's Parcel Shop

![Cover art: The Old Witch's Parcel Shop](../art/accessible/80-the-old-witch-s-parcel-shop.webp)

Granny Vetch kept a parcel shop in the front room of her house. The sign said PARCELS SENT ANYWHERE, and under it, in small letters, ASK.

You could send anything anywhere, so long as the box was the right size for what was in it. Get that wrong and the parcel came back, and Granny Vetch kept the postage.

Marnie went in on a Monday with a cake box tied round with string. She was eight, and she was careful, and she packed it herself.

“Eight for that one?” said Granny Vetch.

“Six,” said Marnie.

“Six,” said Granny Vetch, and turned the box over and looked at the bottom. “Then this box is a lie. It is a box for six.”

* * *

The cake came back on the Wednesday with one side crushed in and the ribbon off.

Marnie took it up to the front room. Granny Vetch put the net back on and counted the spaces with her nail.

“Say the numbers out loud.”

“One, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight.”

“The candles,” said Granny Vetch.

“Eight,” said Marnie. “I lit them once and the wax went in one piece.”

Granny Vetch took a bigger box from under the counter.

Her mother came in that day with her hat still on.

“It was packed for six, so I have done it again, in a bigger box,” said Marnie. “The postage is the same.”

“Twice,” said her mother.

“The first one was a cake for six,” said Marnie. “You could put a big cake in a small box and be happy. Then it is not a cake. It is a cake-shaped way of not saying it.”

“I know Nerys is eight,” said her mother, and went out without taking the cake.

The bigger box went on the Thursday, and Nerys had the cake on the Sunday.

* * *

Mrs. Pryse came in on the Monday with the biggest box in the shop. It was the size of a chest of drawers, with rope round it twice.

“I want to send this to my daughter.”

“Walk round it,” said Granny Vetch. “Then open it.”

Mrs. Pryse undid the knot and the tape and lifted the lid. In the middle of all that straw there was one small thing. A key on a ring, and on the ring a tin horse with the paint rubbed off.

“That is the front door key,” said Mrs. Pryse. “The house is sold on the Thursday and I am going to my daughter’s on the Friday. I cannot take it with me. You said you would send anything anywhere.”

* * *

“I said I would send anything anywhere in a box that fits it,” said Granny Vetch. “That box is not for a key. I cannot take it.”

“It is a perfectly good box.”

“It is a box for a table,” said Granny Vetch. “And the thing in it is not a table. If it went like that she would open a great brown box on the worst day of her year, and at the bottom of it one key. She would have to feel that all the way down the stairs.”

Mrs. Pryse stood quite still. Then she put the lid back on, and it would not go down level, because there was only that much in it.

“How small a box?” she said.

Granny Vetch took down a box the size of a tea tin. The key went to her daughter in it, and the postage was the same as on a letter.

* * *

Marnie had been listening at the counter.

“A parcel has to be honest about itself,” she said. “A big box for a small sad thing is a lie, and a small box for a big happy thing is cheating. That is all the size rule is. It is not about inches.”

Granny Vetch turned the tin horse on the counter with one finger.

“You can say that again at the counter,” she said, “when somebody comes in with a hat in a trunk.”

* * *

Marnie’s grandmother was a hundred and one and lived nine miles off, in a row of houses where nobody is allowed to shout. She could not hear the kettle any more, and she was right.

So Marnie packed a parcel for her, and it took her most of a Tuesday.

It went in a tin the size of a thumb, with a bit of cloth in it. And then six dried peas, a marble, three stones off the beach at Ty Mawr, and the two halves of a set of brass keys, because they made a noise you could hear through a floor.

She put the note under the lid and shook the tin to be sure. Granny Vetch weighed it, and said nothing at all, which from Granny Vetch was a great deal.

* * *

It came to the row of houses on the Thursday morning. Her grandmother was at the door with two fingers on her ear.

She shook it, and shook it again, and the peas and the marble and the stones and the keys went up and down inside the tin like something alive. It was very loud in a quiet house.

“That is the sea,” said her grandmother. “That is the stones at Ty Mawr. I can hear the keys.”

“It is not the sea,” said Marnie. “It is only noise. Loud noise in a small box, so it is a fair size for what is in it.”

Her grandmother put it in her apron pocket, and she could hear it against her hip every time she moved.
