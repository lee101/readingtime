# The Summer the Spires Learned to Dance

![Cover art: The Summer the Spires Learned to Dance](../art/accessible/19-the-summer-the-spires-learned-to-dance.webp)

At noon, the tallest spire of Bellweather put its foot through a bakery roof.

It did not mean harm. Spires did not have feet, at least not on ordinary days. This one swung sideways on its copper foundation, kicked off a weather vane shaped like a dancing goat, and landed with a crash that sent two hundred flour sacks sailing over Juniper Street.

The spire was singing.

Every stone in Bellweather sang with it.

“RUN!” bellowed Broad-Master Tolen, striding down the middle of the street in a coat buttoned to his chin. “Clear the Danceway! All apprentices to the Drum House!”

Iri Vale seized her tool roll and ran.

So did everyone else.

* * *

The Drum House stood where seven streets met. Its doorway said:

THE CITY LISTENS IN TURN.

A century ago, the first Drum Master declared that Bellweather’s bells must never all ring at once. So they played in a fixed order. The final piece, heard only at summer solstice, was the Dance of Stones. It told the spires to shift their weight before summer heat cracked them. No one could change its melody without a unanimous Council vote.

Iri Vale, fourteen and an apprentice tower tuner, knew every pipe in the municipal music machine. Its heart was a great brass drum. Hidden cables ran from it through streets and walls to the bells, canal gates, and balancing weights inside the spires.

Now the cables sang too hard, the drum turned backward, and every spire tried to dance at once.

Iri slid through the Drum House door and nearly collided with Oren Pike, the apprentice lamplighter. His sleeves were singed from hauling lantern hooks out of a bonfire.

“Someone cut the governor rope,” he gasped. “The music just keeps going.”

“Who?”

“Everybody wants to know that.”

The floor crowded with the people who kept Bellweather running. Broad-Master Tolen, the master tuner, stood at the central console. Mara Sen, the Council Archivist and fountain keeper, argued beside bridge mason Dabo and bell-ringer Grandmother Hessa. Pip, the council chamber’s sweeper, hovered nearby with his ring of keys.

One blow from Tolen made a tower lean across the river.

“Shut the canal weights,” he ordered. “Clear the eastern bridge. Silence every bell.”

“The Dance Tune is in those bells,” Hessa said.

“One already hurt the bridge.”

The Dyers’ Spire stretched tall enough to pluck a cloud. Iri studied the cut chain around the console.

“I can jam the governor wheel for ten minutes,” she said. “Mara can shut the water weights.”

“Do it,” said Tolen.

“I’m going.”

Iri swung over the side of the console.

* * *

The governor chamber lay inside the drum’s curved wall. Iri crawled along a narrow iron beam above the turning wheels. At the back, she found the governor wheel. Its rim had been warped into a shallow bowl.

“Tell me when to cut the weights!” Oren called.

“I haven’t found them yet!”

The safety pins should have locked the wheel one tooth at a time. Instead, their holes were packed with wax mixed with silver dust, the kind used to polish the Council Chamber floor.

Someone had done this on purpose.

Pip appeared on the beam, clutching his broom. “The wax trail leads to the old Music Vault. The stair is behind the west bell.”

“A bell can’t be locked.”

“It can now. Also, the Council has blamed dragons, foreign musicians, and irresponsible apprentices.”

The wheel jerked and threw Iri sideways. She caught the beam as the Moon Market fountain rose like a silver needle.

“Cut every blue weight,” she called. “Leave the green ones holding the bridge.”

“The blue ones are under the courthouse!”

“Then the courthouse gets wet!”

Pip headed for the west bell. Iri hooked the governor with her wrench. It slowed, revealing a strip of black paper marked in red: STONE SINGER.

“What’s a stone singer?” she asked.

Pip’s face went pale. “The old name for a spire.”

* * *

Pip went down the hidden stair first, carrying a lamp and the Council keys.

“So the spires aren’t broken?” he asked.

“They’re healthy,” Iri said. “Heat makes them expand. Without the Dance Tune, each one chooses its own step. The drum is forcing them to choose the same one.”

They found the Music Vault’s round door chained shut and caged by wax-sealed pipes. Behind it stood a woman in a white Council robe.

“Archivist Sen,” Pip whispered.

The fountain keeper looked as surprised as he did. “Apprentice Vale. I wondered who would follow the wax.”

The dancing spires shuddered. Dust fell.

“You locked the vault,” Iri said.

“I kept the Dance safe.”

“The Dance is breaking the city. The old tune moves the spires inward. The one on the drum moves them outward.”

“I gave it a second voice,” Mara said. “A western response.”

“It wasn’t chosen.”

“Was there a vote?”

“There was your decision.”

The floor thrummed. Iri saw that the old Dance was running through the stone, rising from Bellweather’s oldest bell.

“The old tune was made for a cooler city,” Mara said. “Summers are hotter now. The spires need to dance farther, not less.”

“Then the city should choose a new song.”

“They would refuse.”

Pip lifted his keys. “They vote.”

“Only after the roofs fall in.”

The west bell chimed: one low note, then six quick notes. A call to the Dance Hall.

“We don’t have time for a careful vote,” Iri said.

“Then we don’t choose for them,” Mara replied. The vault door bucked. “Oren has found us.”

* * *

They climbed into Bellweather’s bright afternoon, and the city was moving.

Spires rocked over rooftops. Balconies rose and fell like the decks of ships. The Dance Hall had opened its wide doors in the square, but no one could enter. The oldest bell stood outside it, a bronze tower without a mouth, and its song made the paving stones hop.

The crowd’s anger had cooled into fear. Hessa stood on the Dance Hall steps, gripping her staff. Dabo had one shoulder under a temporary brace. Tolen worked the fountain weights by hand, swearing between clenched teeth.

Iri climbed onto the fountain beside Mara and shouted for attention.

The city quieted, though several spires kept humming.

“The old tune is still playing under the new one,” Iri told them. “The spires need both patterns. The one pulls them inward. The other lets them dance outward. The governor wheel is broken, so the machine is giving every spire half of each tune.”

Mara nodded. Iri wished she had rehearsed, but the city was out of time.

“The Council can restore the old song,” Tolen called. “It will be safe, because it is proven.”

“For a hundred years,” said Mara.

“And every solstice for a hundred years, the Council has changed the schedule, the repairs, and the flood gates,” Hessa called back. “Some things change because time changes them.”

A baker shouted, “Tell us what to do!”

“Join the assembly!” Hessa answered. “Anyone may speak. The bell carries every voice.”

Iri looked at the oldest bell. Hessa was right, but voices could cancel one another. The spires did not need a louder argument. They needed a pattern all of them could hear.

“Then we ask the stones,” Iri said.

Mara’s eyes widened.

“The Dance of Stones is a call and response,” Iri continued. “Each spire chooses a step. The song tells it how much weight to move. We can open every bell and let each district play one answer. Not a council vote. A dance.”

“A dance needs a tune,” said Tolen.

Iri looked at the broken machine.

“Not from the machine.”

* * *

Iri, Oren, Hessa, and Pip raced across the square while Mara sent messages along the old bell pipes. Districts answered in voices, bells, drums, shutters, and boots.

From the orchards came a hard triple knock.

From the docks came a rolling wave.

The spires of Bellweather leaned and listened.

Iri reached the central drum. Without the governor chain, it still turned, slow and backward. She struck its emergency heel. A single clear note rang beneath the stone song.

Everyone stopped moving.

“Now!” she called.

Oren hit the western pipes. The new response rose in bright, climbing notes.

At the same moment, every district gave its answer.

The orchard knock. The docks’ wave. The bright chime of children’s spoons in Juniper Street. The deep slap of a baker’s tray. Dabo’s wooden mallets. Tolen’s bass-drum boom. Hessa’s clear singing. Even Pip struck the council chamber’s little practice bell with a broom handle.

Nothing fitted neatly. The notes collided, stumbled, and reached toward one another.

The spires groaned.

Iri felt it in her teeth. She pushed both hands into the spinning governor wheel. Its warped rim turned hot beneath her palms. Somewhere inside, old teeth began to click into new spaces.

Oren added the western reply again.

This time, no one stopped him.

The notes found a way through themselves. The inward steps came first: bells and beams, walls and roofs drawing close. Then came the outward answer, bright and spreading. The city’s many sounds leaned together, climbed, and opened.

Bellweather danced.

The Dance Hall roof lifted. The fountain spun into a silver crown. Bridges rose from their hinges, high enough for boats to pass, then settled. Across the river, the Dyers’ Spire caught the sun instead of a cloud.

The oldest bell sounded last.

Its deep note passed through every street. Iri felt it rise up the Drum House wall, ring through the pipes, and reach the stones themselves.

The spires answered, one by one.

* * *

Morning found a new joint in the eastern bridge, shaped like clasped hands. The Dance Hall roof had settled into a bowl, and clear spiral steps climbed its outside. The oldest bell had been rolled to a low pavilion where anyone could reach it.

Dabo called it a temporary danger. Hessa called it a municipal earache. The children called it the best dance ever.

Oren’s western pipes stood beside the old ones in a case anyone in Bellweather could open. Hessa had written every district answer on paper. Pip offered to guard the box, though Council rules said the city clerk owned it. The clerk handed it to him anyway.

Mara admitted her unsafe choices before the Council and asked for the locks to be changed. She put the old bell in public and called for assemblies before each solstice, so citizens would have time to understand what they decided.

“Who will approve the new music?” a member asked.

Mara looked toward the children gathered around the drum. “Ask them.”

At sunset, Iri climbed the new spiral stairs with a hammer in her belt. Above the square, every spire stood easy in the violet sky. The oldest bell rang below, not alone.

From the rooftops came the orchard knock. From the river came the rolling wave. From her own tower came a bright western chime.

Stone answered. The city answered. And Bellweather danced with all three.
