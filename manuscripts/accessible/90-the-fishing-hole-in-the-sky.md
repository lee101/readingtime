# The Fishing Hole in the Sky

![Cover art: The Fishing Hole in the Sky](../art/accessible/90-the-fishing-hole-in-the-sky.webp)

Grandad said the sky had a crack in it, while peeling a potato.

“There is a crack in the sky,” he said.

“That is the aeroplanes,” said Nella.

“That is the other side of it,” said Grandad. “Behind the crack there is a pond, and in it there are the stars, and stars are the best eating there is.”

Nella went on with her sums.

“Fine,” she said.

She was ten. She decided on the back step that she would not bring it up again for a year at least, and she kept to that for about six years.

* * *

The evening came.

Nella was in the garden with the last of the beans when the light went the long way it goes, gold and slow, with an edge on everything. The ladder was leaning against the apple tree and the tin of nails was on the step.

“Grandad,” she called up the side of the house. “Are you coming out.”

“I am going to bed,” his voice said. “I have had a busy day of nothing.”

Nella did not tell him where she was going. He would have come out in his dressing gown and held the ladder, and she did not want to be careful. She wanted the crack. She got a clean jam jar out of the shed, and the string off the ball of wool, and a lump of butter for the hook, which slid about until she chased it round with the knife.

Then she put the tin of nails on the windowsill to weigh the foot of the ladder, and she climbed to the top of the apple tree.

* * *

From up there the sky was the whole way round her. The world went flat and gold, and then it went blue at the edges, the way water goes in a bath.

She looked for the crack. She looked a long time.

And there it was.

It was a long thin dark line in the blue over the allotments. It was not a scratch and it was not an aeroplane. It was about the length of her arm from elbow to fingertip, and it was exactly where Grandad had said it would be, which frightened her more than the sky did. A sky could be wrong. Her grandad had not been wrong about anything since the war.

Nella put the butter jar on the string and let it down.

* * *

The jar went through the crack, and it did not splash, and it did not go fast. It went down the way a thing goes down in a well.

Through the crack, the sky was a pond about the size of Grandad’s bath. It was warm, and lit from under the water so that you could see the whole bottom of it.

The fish were in it. They were slow and bright and there was a lot of them, and they were not frightened of the jar.

She set the string over the branch and pulled it tight and waited.

A star came up to the butter.

* * *

Nella caught the first one on the first go and felt it go hard and small on the string. She lifted the jar out of the crack, and in it was one pale fish turning in clear water.

The second was quicker. The third was clever and went round the jar. The fourth came up to the butter of its own accord, like it had been waiting for her.

Then the string went slack.

Nella looked up. The crack was closing. Not fast. It came together the way your two hands come together when you are very tired, and then there was a blue sky with nothing wrong with it.

She was sitting on the apple tree in her garden, in the dark, with the jar in her lap.

Four fish in the jar. All four of them still turning.

Nella put her hand into the jar. The water was warm, and it was not wet the way water is wet. Her hand came out shining, and there was star all over her fingers, and she could not wipe it off and did not want to.

* * *

Grandad was at the kitchen table with the lamp on. He had been waiting up.

Nella came round the door holding the jar like a lantern.

“Grandad.”

“I would not,” said Grandad, “if I were you. I am telling you as a man of seventy-one years.”

“It is four fish.”

“So I heard.”

“They are stars. I knew you were not making it up, but I had to see if it was where you said.”

Grandad looked at the jar a long time. He did not touch it.

“The tin of nails was to weigh the ladder,” he said. “I had thought of that. I had not thought of what to say afterwards.”

Nella put the jar on the table and sat down. The four fish went round in it in a light of their own.

“You knew where it was.”

“When I was nine,” said Grandad. “And I had nobody to hold the jar and be sure it was real. That is the whole of it.”

“You should have said.”

“I should have said,” Grandad agreed. “And now there are four stars on my table.”

* * *

They ate the stars on toast on New Year’s Eve.

Grandad toasted the bread, and Nella cut the four fish into pieces, which was not easy and she was not sorry, and they ate it with the rain on the window.

“It tastes of nothing at all,” said Nella.

“That is the point,” said Grandad. “You would not eat a sunset. There is not one sunset in the world that would taste of anything. Nor a rainbow. That is what they are for.”

“Grandad —”

“Go to bed,” said Grandad. “It is nearly a year, and I am tired.”
