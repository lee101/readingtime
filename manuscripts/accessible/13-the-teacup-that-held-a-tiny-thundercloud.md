# The Teacup That Held a Tiny Thundercloud

![Cover art: The Teacup That Held a Tiny Thundercloud](../art/accessible/13-the-teacup-that-held-a-tiny-thundercloud.webp)

Nia found the teacup on the highest kitchen shelf.

It was pink, with gold flowers around its wide cup. A little tag tied to the handle said, “For Nia, when you are ready to listen.”

Nia was six. She was ready now.

She climbed onto a chair and took down the cup. The saucer beside it had a note. Grandma’s round handwriting filled both sides.

“Rain answers a true need. Orders only make bigger trouble. Open your ears.”

Nia lifted the lid.

A tiny thundercloud rose out with it.

The cloud was gray and soft as a kitten. Lightning flashed inside. Thunder shook the sugar bowl.

Clink!

Nia held the cup closer. The cloud sat in her palm, blinking with little sparks.

“Are you hungry?” she asked.

The cloud made a soft rumble.

Nia fetched a saucer of water. She placed the cup inside it. The cloud drank, and three drops fell onto the pink roses.

Nia had always wanted a garden. Her family’s yard was mostly hard dirt. One lonely strawberry plant grew beside the fence.

“What does my plant need?” Nia asked.

She listened.

She heard a dry tap inside the teacup.

“A drink,” Nia said.

One small raindrop landed on the strawberry plant. Another drop landed nearby. The third landed on Nia’s nose.

Nia giggled. The cloud twinkled.

The next day was Garden Day at school. Nia wanted the biggest, reddest strawberry anyone had ever grown. Her plant had only three leaves. The soil around it felt like dry sand.

Nia ran home with the cup under her coat.

When no one was looking, she opened the lid.

“I need lots of rain,” she said. “Please make my plant grow.”

Rain sprinkled the garden. One strawberry unfurled.

Nia jumped for joy.

Now she waited for another.

The cloud licked its tiny lightning. No rain came.

Nia tapped the cup twice.

“I need more rain now.”

A gray curtain fell over the yard. Water filled the wheelbarrow. It ran past Nia’s boots and under the back door.

The strawberry plant grew tall, but its leaves curled.

“Stop!” Nia called.

The rain did not stop.

Nia lifted the lid higher. The cloud puffed itself wide. Thunder rolled over the rooftops. A stack of paper lanterns toppled into the wet grass.

Then the cloud raced away.

Nia ran after it. Her shoes splashed through puddles. She could not see the little cloud. The sky was dark and busy.

Thunder shook the old bell tower.

Where had the cloud gone?

Nia stopped beside the school garden. Rainwater streamed down its path. Teachers lifted pots. Children carried buckets. Everyone worked quickly.

The garden wall stood beside a deep ditch. The gray cloud hovered above it, growing darker with every flash.

Nia’s first wish was to call the cloud down.

She opened her mouth. Then she remembered Grandma’s note.

*Listen.*

Nia closed her eyes. Wind pushed her wet hair across her face. She heard rushing water. She heard buckets knocking together.

She heard someone crying.

A small girl held an empty flowerpot. Mud covered one sleeve.

“I can’t find my worm,” the girl said. “He helped my beans grow.”

Another child held up a soggy picture.

“My frog drawing is ruined.”

Farther away, Nia saw a worried teacher. A large barrel was floating toward the broken bridge.

Nia’s ears filled with all these little troubles. She had only listened for what she wanted before.

The cloud rumbled above the ditch.

“Your storm is scaring everyone,” Nia called. “You need more room.”

Lightning answered with a sharp flash.

“No!” Nia said. “I mean you need to tell me.”

She put both hands behind her back. The waiting felt much longer than the rain.

At last, she heard a soft, tired puff.

Pffff.

The rain softened. The dark cloud grew smaller. It lowered itself beside Nia.

“There you are,” she whispered.

Nia opened the lid. The cloud slipped inside. Thunder sounded far away, like a cart over cobblestones.

The teacher tied the floating barrel to a post. Children carried dry wood for the bridge. The little girl found a shiny button in the mud. Her worm was safe beneath the leaf where she had left it.

Nia carried her teacup to the garden bed.

The strawberry plant was drooping. Its tall stem leaned into the ditch. The roots near the ground had swollen, while the leaves above were dry.

Mara, the garden teacher, knelt beside it.

“Too much water below,” she said. “Those roots cannot breathe.”

“What does it need?” Nia asked.

Mara pointed to the dry soil around the stem.

“Shade, mulch, and a little water. Not a flood.”

Nia looked into the cup. The cloud waited quietly.

Not a command. Not a wish shouted into the sky.

A true need.

“The plant needs cool mulch,” Nia said. “Will you help?”

The cloud made one bright flash.

Nia and Mara spread dry mulch around the stem. A soft rain moistened it without making any puddles.

The storm cloud moved on to the thirsty creek beyond the school. It left rain where it was needed.

When the cloud passed, sunshine returned. A rainbow stood over the garden. Water dripped from every leaf.

On Garden Day, Nia’s strawberry was the reddest one.

Mara gave it to her. Nia carried it home beside the teacup.

* * *

That evening, Nia set the cup by the window. A silver drop sparkled on the pink rim. It did not need thunder to say it was there.

Outside, her strawberry leaves stood wide and green.
