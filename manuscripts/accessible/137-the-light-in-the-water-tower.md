# The Light in the Water Tower

![Cover art: The Light in the Water Tower](../art/accessible/137-the-light-in-the-water-tower.webp)

Halversgate's signal light is a lamp in the top of the water tower, lit by hand, and nobody can say what it burns. It gives a white light that carries further than any lamp has a right to carry, and it drinks the tower while it burns: a lamp burning to the far edge of town puts a foot off the level for every hour it burns.

The water in that tower is Halversgate's water. It goes to Shingle Row and to the market fountain and to the maltings, and the licence says nine feet at six in the morning, and the gauge is painted on the lamp room wall.

Mungo was thirteen and had put his name down for a watch because a watch pays two pounds and a plate of supper. He did not find out until he was twenty-two steps up the ladder that the job was the level.

* * *

Noll was eighteen by then and had done two hundred and eleven watches since he was fifteen, and he showed Mungo the four things: the key on a chain that shuts the outside door behind you, the door of the lamp room, which sticks, the trimmer wheel on the wick, and the book.

The book was a hardback with a canvas spine, open on the shelf by the window with a brick on it. Every watch wrote the same four things: the hour it was lit, the hour it was put out, the level at each hour off the gauge, and the weather. Then there was a fifth column, and it said CALLED.

* * *

The list was on the inside of the lamp room door and Mungo read it before he lit up. He had been told it would be a list of things to do if the lamp went out. Lights going out here are rare, and the town had been told a drum on the tower step would do it.

The list was not lights. There were eight names on it and an address under each one, and beside each address a line in Noll's writing.

Bev Sowter, seventy-one, Cotter's Row. Deaf in her left ear, so knock on the right. She sets her clock by the light and it is wrong by eleven minutes.

Joe Pyke, Cotter's Row, top floor. On his own till Tuesday, and nobody on that stair till Friday.

* * *

Mungo lit up at a quarter past eight and wrote 8.15 in the book, and went down the tower and round all eight of them.

Bev Sowter had her clock by the window. “It is eleven minutes wrong,” she said, “and I do not know which way.” Mungo wrote it down.

He was thirty-five minutes on the round and back in the tower at nine, and the level was nine feet one inch. By ten it was eight feet eight.

The frost had a strangle hold on the pipes and half the houses on Shingle Row were drawing at the same time, and the tower was the only water in the parish, and the light drank from the same tank as everybody's cold tap. Noll had left a note on the gauge saying to read the level out loud.

* * *

At eleven he went round again, because there was a woman on the list at Number Four Mill Row he had not been near, and her light was off.

She had the door open in a dressing gown. “My sister is in the fever,” she said. “Can I be left till eight.” He wrote fever, sick, leave till eight in the fifth column, and in the margin he wrote the number of his own house.

At half past eleven he was back up the twenty-two steps and the level was eight feet three.

* * *

The frost went at four in the morning and by six the pipes were quiet and the water came up on its own, and Mungo put the lamp out at ten past six and shut the door.

Eight feet one inch. The licence said nine.

The water committee met on the Tuesday and Joel was on it with the book, and there was no argument at all, because the book said what it said and anybody could come and read it. The fountain was off for a fortnight and the paper printed four lines.

Since then the light in the tower is lit to the coast road and the harbour and no further, and the far end of the Cut is dark from six in the evening all winter, and the men from the water committee say that is how far this town can see, and they say it the way you say a thing you have decided is true.
