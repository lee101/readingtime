# The Lantern at the End of the Ridge

![Cover art: The Lantern at the End of the Ridge](../art/accessible/07-the-lantern-at-the-end-of-the-ridge.webp)

Every dusk, Sella walked the Ridge Line with her grandmother.

She was seven years old, and she lived in the room above the boat shed, where the floorboards always smelled of rope and salt. Together they lit nine little brass lanterns, one at each iron post, and sang the walking song all the way to the end.

* * *

The ninth post stands out on the black point, furthest from the town, where the wind has room to be itself. It is the smallest lantern on the whole coast, and the coldest, and the one every child is given to light last.

* * *

That evening, the first lantern in the harbour would not catch.

Sella tapped the glass twice with her knuckle and gave it a patient little breath, exactly the way she had been taught. It only coughed a tiny grey puff and went out again.

* * *

“Try the eiderdown,” said her grandmother, and pressed a small stoppered jar into Sella's hands.

It was the good oil, the kind that smelled faintly of vanilla, and they had been saving it all winter for a night when a lamp needed help.

* * *

Sella poured one careful finger of oil onto the wick and lit it.

The flame caught at once and went up tall and gold, and the whole bay below turned soft and green, like the inside of a bottle.

* * *

But when Sella stepped back to look, she counted the shine and not the light.

Out on the black point the ninth lantern was a smudge, barely there, dimmer than any of the other eight.

* * *

“That one is the farthest,” Sella said. “And the farthest is always the coldest.”

She ran back down the wet stone steps for her grandmother's little roll of trimmed wicks.

* * *

The old wick was thin and grey and smelly.

Sella pinched it off with her teeth and fitted the fat new one in, and the ninth lantern lifted a bright little flame into the sea wind, like a fish rising.

* * *

Sella went back up the Ridge Line, counting out loud: one, two, three, and nine.

Behind her, one by one, all the boats came safely in, and nobody in Brinehold held their breath any more.

* * *

“You gave the far one the better wick,” said her grandmother at the top of the steps.

“Yes,” said Sella. “The far one is the coldest.”

Her grandmother smiled, and the nine small lights stood all around them like a ring of little suns.
