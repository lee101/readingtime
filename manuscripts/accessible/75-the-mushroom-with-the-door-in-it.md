# The Mushroom with the Door in It

![Cover art: The Mushroom with the Door in It](../art/accessible/75-the-mushroom-with-the-door-in-it.webp)

In the wood behind the house there was a fairy ring.

Otto counted it on his fingers twice and it came out the same both times. Thirty-one white mushrooms with their caps shut up, standing in a circle in the wet grass, and one in the middle was taller than the rest.

In the cap of that one there was a door.

It was green, with a small round knob, and not much bigger than the door of a doll’s house. Otto put his thumb on the knob and pushed.

It did not open.

“It is locked,” said Otto.

* * *

He ran and got his grandmother. She came out with her stick and her basket and stood at the edge of the ring, looking at it for a long time.

“There is a list on this ring,” she said. “It is the only list there is.”

“What does it say?”

“If I say it, it is not a list any more.”

“Show me.”

“You cannot read a ring, Otto. There is no first stone.”

“Then I will start anywhere.”

She looked at him then, and she went home and put the kettle on.

* * *

Otto tried everything that looked as though it ought to work.

He tried his own tooth, which had come out at breakfast with no pain at all. He set it on the step of the green door, and it stayed on the step.

He tried a robin’s egg. He took it out of the hedge in his hanky and set it down, and it did not open the door, so he put the egg back in the nest himself.

He tried a wish, out loud, with his eyes shut, and then again, louder, in case the first one had gone soft on the way.

He tried a hazelnut, a marble, his own button, and a spoonful of milk.

The door did not do anything at all, which is a great deal of nothing, and Otto sat down on the moss to think.

* * *

“What did your mother try?” he said.

His grandmother put a cup down on the table.

“A great many things,” she said. “None of them worked. Nobody has ever opened that door by giving it something.”

“Then how?”

His grandmother did not answer with words. She hummed.

It was five notes long. She hummed it twice, and then she stopped.

Otto tried to sing it back. He got the first note out, and then the third note where the second one should have been, and then he made a noise like a chair being moved.

“You cannot carry a tune,” she said.

“No,” said Otto. “But I can do that part.”

* * *

He did that part with a hazel stick from the path. He stood on a stone and tapped the notes on the mushroom cap one at a time, in the order his grandmother had hummed them.

Nothing, for a whole week.

Then on Thursday, on the fourth tap or the fifth, the door knocked back.

Otto sat down in the grass.

He had not thought the door could knock. It was only a door, the size of his hand. But it had knocked, once, under his stick.

* * *

He tapped again on Saturday, and the door knocked again, and Otto wrote the date in his notebook, and was pleased with himself.

Then he tapped a note that was not in the list, and the door knocked back exactly as it had for all the others.

“It is only the knocking it wants,” said Otto. “Not the tune. The sound.”

His grandmother said nothing at all, and Otto noticed that she had said nothing at all.

* * *

He came on Monday for the first right note, and on Friday for the second. He slept in the hayloft on the Sunday to get the third one out of his head, and he had it on the Monday at four in the rain.

He tapped it.

The door pushed out a hair’s width, and green light came out, and Otto put both hands on it and pushed it open the rest of the way.

* * *

Inside there was a room about the size of a doll’s parlour.

An old woman sat on a stool with grey hair like duckweed, a cup and saucer on her knee, and a sock on two knitting needles that had been going a very long time.

She put the needles down.

“I am nine hundred years old, I live here on my own, and nobody has knocked on that door since the good bread,” she said.

* * *

Otto went back the next Sunday, and the Sunday after. The old woman had a cup ready.

He tapped the three notes on the cap, then went in, and after a while he stopped tapping.

In September the door was open when he came, and the ring had gone wrong. Otto had pushed through it so many times that the circle had come open into a horseshoe, with a gap in it where the mushrooms were trodden down and never came up.

His grandmother walked out to the wood with her stick.

“You cannot read that,” she said. “There is no first stone in it now.”

She sat down on the wet grass, and she did not hum.
