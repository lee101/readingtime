# The Moon-Slice Bakery

![Cover art: The Moon-Slice Bakery](../art/accessible/27-the-moon-slice-bakery.webp)

At seven every evening, the blue door of the bakery opened before the streetlights. Warm flour, cinnamon, and roasted almond drifted over the square. Niko, age nine, tied his floury apron twice. His sister Sana, age seven, set out one small moon-shaped pan and counted the blue bowls with her thumb.

One pan. One moon pastry. One rule.

The silver crescent over the oven was an old heat gauge. Its hollow gathered the room's scent, and its tips darkened as the oven rose. At the first street bell, it released that scent beside the moon pastry, carrying the shared evening back into memory.

Niko pointed toward the hook above the oven. "Morning light makes the silver dull. Fill the water pan. I will start the moon dough."

But the hook was bare.

Their mother, Inez, crossed the kitchen. She touched the empty metal. "It was there last night. I tightened the hook before we closed."

Sana leaned close. The oven stones smelled cool and dusty. Niko touched one, then pulled his fingers away.

"They are barely warm," he said.

“But Dad started the fire at six,” Tamir answered.

The family looked at one another. Then Inez checked the clock. It was seven fifteen. The small clock above the flour bin still said six. The lunch bell had come and gone.

Something had happened between them.

Sana placed her nose near the warm oven door. "The cinnamon jar smells too strong."

Niko leaned beside her. "The almond tin has cinnamon on it."

"It did not last night." Sana pointed across the room. "The clean cloth is on the shelf. The floor cloth is by the blue bowls. My moon pan is empty. The crescent is gone. And nobody knows why."

The morning orders were stacked beside the sink, and the first customers were waiting outside.

Niko examined the oven mouth. Soot lined the inner arch. The old rule was cut into the tile below it: THE SILVER MOON KEEPS WHAT THE WARM OVEN GATHERS. LEAVE IT EMPTY, AND THE HEAT TAKES THE HOUR AWAY.

“The crescent was removed while the oven was hot,” Niko said. “The heat had nowhere to rest. It trapped the scent of our evening, and the smell faded from everyone's memory. That is why Dad remembers starting the fire at six, and we remember opening the bakery at seven, but nothing between.”

"That does not tell us who took it," Inez said.

Sana opened her small black notebook. She drew what remained: her empty pan, the folded cloth, and three clean half-moons on the flour. She always recorded smells, sounds, and positions because memories tricked her.

"My pan smells like clean soap," she said. "But look at the oven."

Niko touched the three half-moons. "The hook points to the copper arc. Last night, the crescent covered the line beside the full moon. The first-quarter line is cold, the next line is warm, and the last is only dust."

"It disappeared after the first-quarter mark," Niko said.

Sana pointed to the back door. "I opened this at the second knock and smelled Mrs. Vale's rosemary bread. Someone could have come in."

Outside, every evening customer had the same blank stretch. The tailor opened his lunch basket, and warm rosemary bread filled the air.

"Mrs. Vale brought that after closing," Sana said.

Milo Vale, her ten-year-old son, went still. Niko noticed his coat pocket move.

"Did you take the silver crescent?" Niko asked.

Milo pulled it from his satchel, wrapped in a drawing of eight moon phases. "It came loose on the hook. I wanted to sketch it. I meant to return it after school. Then I felt dizzy and everyone inside kept saying the same thing again."

"You took our family's memory," Tamir said.

"I did not know it remembered anything," Milo answered.

Niko touched the crescent. It was as warm as bread just out of the oven. "It belongs over the oven before the first street bell. Sana can guide its hollow from below while I lift with the peel. Milo, use your drawing as a target. Both tips must enter the blue outline."

They ran back. Inez had folded the morning dough into a plain roll. Tamir scraped it from the tray.

"Our evening does not need to end with nothing done," he said.

Sana climbed onto the flour stool. Niko held the long peel. Milo slid the silver over the copper arc.

"Too high. Turn the narrow tip toward the skylight," Sana said.

Niko eased the handle. Heat shimmered inside the crescent. At the second mark, the silver flashed.

"Now," Sana said.

Niko pulled. The crescent rose into the blue outline and clicked onto the hook. The oven stones stopped trembling. Cinnamon and almond strengthened. The blank hour filled with small pieces: Niko kneading while Sana counted, Inez shaping pastry, Tamir letting in warm air, and Milo peering through the doorway.

No one remembered every motion, but they remembered the evening together.

The rescued dough began to rise. "Sana, count the oven knocks. Dad, warm the milk. Mom, fill the water pan. Milo, set out the cups."

Sana counted. Niko rolled the dough. Inez brushed almond cream into its folds. Together they baked one crescent-shaped moon pastry.

Its first scent was cinnamon. Next came almond. Last came warm flour, clean cloth, open air, and hard work shared. The waiting neighbors remembered the missing hour without being told a single thing.

The next night, Niko checked that the crescent sat securely on its hook. Sana drew a small moon beside every count in her notebook. At the first street bell, its hollow returned the scent of laughter, hurried steps, and the blue door opening for the neighborhood.

No one forgot again.
