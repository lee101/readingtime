# The Boy Who Kept the Cape

![Cover art: The Boy Who Kept the Cape](../art/accessible/136-the-boy-who-kept-the-cape.webp)

Tamsin was twelve, with black hair cropped short and a thumbnail gone for good from a broken biscuit tin, and he delivered bread for his father's shop on Bridge Street.

On the twelfth of March a red cape came up on the shingle below the point. It was four feet long and heavy as a wet coat and heavier than that, with a stiff collar and no label inside.

Worn, it gave you back the strength you already had, only more of it, and it went on giving for as long as you were wearing it and not one minute after. That was the price and it was all of the price. It made nothing up. It took the extra out of the person wearing it, and the person before Tamsin had been his grandmother, and she had worn it fifty years without once saying why.

He had always been the strongest boy on Bridge Street, and that was not the same as being strong. On the first morning he put it on he carried the coal hod up the outside stairs with one hand.

He told nobody. Noll had a stack of the Halversgate Adventurer, printed on Bridge Street thirty years ago, and the hero in it was a man called the Gatewarden with a red cape the same colour who could shift a barrow up a hill. In all four of the books nobody ever saw him take it off.

* * *

On the ninth of April a boy called Ruben Shale got hold of Tamsin behind the fish stalls and put him in a headlock he could not get out of.

The cape was in his bag. It had been raining at lunchtime.

Tamsin got his arm up with it on and had Ruben face down on the wet cobbles before anybody in the market had finished swallowing. He did not hit him. He set him sitting on the kerb and stepped back, and Ruben said thank you in a voice nobody had used before.

* * *

Afterwards he could not take it off. He got his head through and his right arm through and then his arms stopped working. He could hold the cape up at the shoulder, but he could not take the weight of four feet of wet wool over his head.

So he put his head back through the collar and stood under the awning a while with his hands shaking. The price was not a mystery any more. It was his own arms, a better pair than he had ever had, and they had come off with the cloak.

* * *

His grandmother was seventy-eight and her name was Esme Cardew.

He asked her in the kitchen on the Sunday while the tap ran, and she did not stop what she was doing. She said it had belonged to his grandfather and had come out of the moor in nineteen forty-something, and that she had put it on to carry coal and never taken it off.

She said it gave her what she had got and took it out of her, and that for fifty years the only thing it had done was make her the strongest woman on Bridge Street, and that she had liked that, and had carried other people's shopping up the steps without mentioning it.

Then she shut the tap off. “I cannot lift my own shopping now,” she said. “I measured it. I can carry a full basket as far as the second step and not any further, and I have never once needed to ask how that happened.”

Tamsin said he would give it back the next day. “Put it on the shelf,” said Esme Cardew. “It will sit there.”

And she said that she was not going to say anything to make him keep it or to make him hand it in, because a thing like that is not worth a person being told about twice.

* * *

He carried it the long way by the river and did not put it on.

The shop room at the back of his father's shop has a chair by the window with a cushion on it that has been going yellow for eleven years, and Tamsin shook the cape out, folded it along the old folds and laid it flat on the cushion with the collar square to the edge of the seat, and then put the kettle on.

Noll came in at five with the four books under his arm and asked whether he wanted to see the one with the flood in it. Tamsin said he would in a minute, and when he came back the boy was still at the window, looking at the bridge over the Cut with the name on it.
