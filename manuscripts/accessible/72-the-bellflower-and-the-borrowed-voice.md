# The Bellflower and the Borrowed Voice

![Cover art: The Bellflower and the Borrowed Voice](../art/accessible/72-the-bellflower-and-the-borrowed-voice.webp)

Sena’s voice was not her own.

When she was frightened it came out as a robin, small and quick, saying her words four times too fast and twice as loud. When she was cross it came out as a cart. Tomas said everything like a man reading a notice, in a scrape, and the family learned to translate it inside three days.

“Put the kettle on,” said her grandmother, and the room said it like a cart.

Sena had been trying to mend it since she was six.

* * *

On the Monday of play week she took her own voice out to the orchard, where nobody would come and ask her what was wrong.

She stood between the two apple trees with her hands at her sides. She said, “Good morning,” in the voice she used inside her own head, the flat quiet one that sounded like her.

It came out as a gull.

A gull, standing in an orchard, going on at the apple branches.

Sena tried again. “This is Sena. I am ten. I am here.”

The gull went on. It wanted the sky. It went up over the hedge and it did not come back.

* * *

So she tried to hold a sparrow instead, because a sparrow was small, and the small ones had not been cross at her yet.

Sena sat down under the tree and made her throat as small as she could and said, “I have lost my voice. Have you seen it?”

It came out as a sparrow, and it was a good sparrow, better than her robin. It went along the grass and up into the hedge, and it sang for a long time about a leaf.

Sena said, “Is it at your nest?”

The sparrow sang about the leaf again.

She tried three more times. The sparrow never waited for a reply, and neither had the gull. Nothing in the orchard came back and asked her a thing.

* * *

Then she tried not talking, and that was worse.

She came into the kitchen and stood by the table and said nothing for a whole hour. At twenty past she turned her cup round on its saucer, quietly. At half past she turned it again.

At twenty to six her grandmother said, “What is wrong with you.”

At the same moment Tomas said, “Do you want a slice?”

And out in the yard, where Sena could not see her, the play teacher called in through the window, “Did somebody die?”

Three questions, all at once, and every one of them broke into somebody else’s voice. A robin, a cart, and a flat scrape.

Sena put her fingers over her ears and ran up the stairs, and sat on the top step, and the house went on talking under her.

* * *

The play was on the Friday. The hall had the flags out and the chairs in rows and forty children waiting behind a curtain of brown paper.

Tomas was in the second scene as a woodcutter, which suited him.

Behind the curtain Sena said, “I cannot do it.”

“You can,” said Tomas. “You are the one who has to say the line about the door.”

“They will all laugh.”

“They will not.” Tomas put his hands in his pockets. “Listen. Forty people out there are waiting for the line about the door, not for a voice. When you say it, they will hear a door.”

Sena said, “What if it comes out a bird?”

“Then forty people will be looking at you instead of the door, and Miss Peel will be cross, and you will be quicker than a bird.”

* * *

Tomas put his hand on the top of her head, the way he did when she was small and would not let go of a thing.

“You are not borrowing a voice,” he said. “You are borrowing permission.”

He took his hand away and went off to find his axe.

Sena stood behind the brown paper for a long minute. Then she stopped thinking about the voice, because that was how she had been getting the wrong one. She thought about the door instead, and about the man who would have to knock on it, and wait.

* * *

She stepped up. The hall was dark after the lamps, and the paper was brown, and forty faces turned round at once.

“Sena,” said the king. “Go and find out who is knocking at the door.”

Sena crossed to the middle of the stage and said her line in the flat quiet voice that sounded like her own.

“There is a light under the door and it is moving, so somebody inside does not want to be found.”

It came out as Sena.

Nobody in the hall made any sound at all, because nobody had been listening for a voice. The king said, “Then knock.” And Sena knocked.

Out in the road, on the wall by the school gate, there was a blue bellflower with three bells on it that had never once made a sound. It made one now. It rang once, clear, and that was all.

* * *

Sena’s voice still changes. It is a robin when she is cross about a dish. It is a cart when she wants a door opened for her.

She keeps the robin for the jokes now. Tomas laughs at the jokes so hard that he drops the washing, and her grandmother asks her to say the one about the cat again, and says it is worth the washing-up.
