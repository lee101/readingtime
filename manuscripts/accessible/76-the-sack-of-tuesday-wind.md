# The Sack of Tuesday Wind

![Cover art: The Sack of Tuesday Wind](../art/accessible/76-the-sack-of-tuesday-wind.webp)

Mr Fenwick sold wind out of a shed behind the ironmonger’s, and he sold it by the bag.

“I sell Monday and Wednesday,” he said. “I do not sell the grey one. Tuesday is the good kind. It stirs things. It pushes a smell out through an open door.”

“I have never hurried a child counting coins,” said Mr Fenwick, and he waited while Tess counted hers twice.

“A bag of Tuesday,” he said. “That is your week’s money and a penny over.”

“Does it want out?” said Tess.

“It wants to work,” said Mr Fenwick. “Tuesday wind does not want to sit in a bag. You will have a hard walk home.”

The sack was grey cloth with a drawstring, and it would not sit still on the counter. It rolled a little left, then a little right, its corners lifting and dropping like a dog asleep on a step.

“That is Tuesday wind in a bag,” said Mr Fenwick. “Hold it, or it will work without you.”

* * *

The walk home was hard.

It began where the lane narrowed. Tess felt the sack lean, and the top of her satchel went off her shoulder and sailed beside her. She caught the strap and felt her hair go up round her face.

“Sit down,” she said to the sack. “Please.”

A hat came off a washing line three gardens away. A bucket tipped over and spread itself flat on the cobbles.

Tess got it to the school gate by holding it down with both arms. In the porch it turned itself once, like a dog turning on a rug, and settled.

* * *

On Monday she kept it under her desk. On Tuesday she kept it in her locker. On Wednesday the hall door stood open and the smell came out to meet her.

“It is the old cabbages from the cook’s store,” said a girl at the door. “It has been coming out of there all week.”

“It has been there since Friday,” said the headmistress. “The windows have been up the whole time.”

Tess walked to the front of the hall with the sack in both hands. Twenty-six children sat on the floor, and twelve paper notices were pinned to the board.

She put the neck of the sack on the floor and slipped the drawstring off.

The smell went out of the room. It went up through the high windows and away over the roofs.

“Oh,” said somebody at the back of the hall.

Then everything moved a little.

All twelve notices came off the board at once and went round the room, past the clock and past the globe lamps and past the headmistress, who stood with her hands behind her back. Tess’s page came off her desk and joined them.

* * *

Mr Fenwick came at four o’clock with a receipt.

He stood in the doorway with his hat in his hands, looking at the notices going round, and at the headmistress, who said nothing about the windows.

“The smell is off,” said Tess.

“It is,” said Mr Fenwick. “That is Tuesday wind doing what it was bought for.” He watched a notice go past his ear. “Those are not what it was bought for.”

“They came off the board,” said the headmistress. “All of them. There was no draught.”

“I am not saying there was,” said Mr Fenwick. “I am saying there are twelve pieces of paper going round your hall, and somebody is going to want them back.”

Nobody said anything.

The headmistress watched the notices go. “The parents are in the corridor,” she said. “In eleven minutes they will be in here. There is nothing wrong in this hall.”

* * *

Then Tess put her hand in her pocket.

“I have got two,” said Tess. “I had two pennies for Monday. The other ten are on a card at home.”

“You could ask me for those,” said Mr Fenwick.

“I would rather buy them,” said Tess. “One coin each, out loud, in front of everybody, so it knows who they belong to.”

She said it out loud in the middle of the hall, with the notices going round her. A father said, “Are you quite sure, Miss?”

Then Tess put a penny on her own desk for the first notice and said, “That one is mine now.” The notice came down out of the air and lay flat on the desk, and it stayed.

She did it twelve times. A penny for the lost glove. A penny for the hockey match. A penny for the door that sticks. Mr Fenwick did not move from the doorway, and the headmistress got a chair and sat down.

* * *

By Friday the hall smelled of nothing at all, not even floor polish.

Mr Fenwick came at four o’clock with a different sack.

“Sit down,” he said, and sat down with the sack between his boots. “This is how you sell it. Take hold of the bottom, not the neck. Shake it out in a wide arc, away from your face, and say the price while it is going.”

Tess took hold of the bottom and shook it out in a wide arc.

The headmistress’s hair went straight up. Every paper on her desk went to the ceiling. One notice came down on her head, the one about the door that sticks.

“That,” said Mr Fenwick, “is how it is done.”

“That is not how it is done,” said Tess.

She sells wind on Tuesdays now, from the corner of the shed by the gate. She measures the money wrong. She forgets to say the price until it is over. And she does not mind at all, because the hall smells of nothing at all, and it is hers.
