# The Bridge of Three Wishes

![Cover art: The Bridge of Three Wishes](../art/accessible/79-the-bridge-of-three-wishes.webp)

The bridge over the River Alyn had three arches. The first was wide, the second was wider, and the third was low and grey and nobody liked it.

Every child in the village knew the rule. One wish under each arch. You stood on the flat stone beside the water, you said what you wanted out loud, and then you went home.

Nobody had ever wished for anything large. Rhiannon the baker had wished for a second oven and used it every day.

“You did not want a horse?” said Emrys.

“A horse,” said Rhiannon, “is a great deal of hay and a great deal of hoof.”

* * *

Emrys was nine. He had used the first arch at six for a red kite, and got one for most of a winter. The second he used at eight, for his father to come back from the sea.

The third arch was the last one, and Emrys had been keeping it. He went to Cadoc Fenner, who was famous for it.

“What did you wish for under the third arch?”

“A bike,” said Cadoc.

“Guto’s bike.”

Cadoc stopped mending the gate.

“A bike,” said Cadoc. “Guto’s bike is blue, I know that. But a bike is what I said. Mine is green and it has a bell on it. Guto’s has no bell. A bell is for somebody who needs to be found, and Guto can be found anywhere.”

* * *

So Emrys went round the village asking, and he kept the answers on a slate.

“A bootlace,” said old Prys. “The good leather one. I had a boot with a good bootlace and a boot with a bad one.”

“My brother to stop whistling,” said Nesta. “And he did stop, and then I wished I could have it back, and you cannot have it back.”

He had a hundred and six on the slate by the last day of the fortnight, and not one was big.

He sat on the wall and tried to write down something that was not a bootlace. “A palace,” he said out loud, and crossed it out. “To be taller.” He crossed that out too. Then he looked at the slate, and it was a small grey shape.

* * *

His grandmother was in the yard with her back to him.

“You have been asking people,” she said. “I heard you at the gate with old Prys.”

“Did you wish, Grandma?”

“I never wished.”

He put the slate down.

“Never once?”

“Never once in seventy years,” said his grandmother. “People think I am still waiting. I am not waiting. Every morning somebody brings me two or three things to pick from. A pot, or a hook, or a bit of string. And I pick one. And that is the whole of the day.”

“That is not a wish.”

“No,” she said. “It is a day.”

She leaned on the fence. She looked very tired.

“You keep trying to make it big,” she said. “It is not a size. You will not find a big one.”

* * *

On the last day of the fortnight he went down to the third arch, and his grandmother sat on the bank.

He stood on the flat stone and thought about a hundred and six small things, and about a red kite.

Then he said, “A dog.”

He said it out loud, the way you are meant to.

“There is no dog,” he said, coming back up the bank.

“No,” she said.

She was right the next morning. “There is no dog in the shed,” said Emrys. “There is no dog under the bed. There is no dog down the lane.”

“You are disappointed for one day,” said his grandmother.

He looked at her.

“Yesterday and today,” she said. “That is the whole of it. Come and have your tea.”

* * *

On the third morning the dog was on the step.

She was not a big dog and she was not a small one. She was brown, with one ear bent forward and one bent back, and a white sock on one front leg. She wagged her tail and sat in the dust.

He put his hand on her head, and her tail went along the ground.

“Good dog,” he said, and nothing else.

She was the most ordinary dog alive. She slept in the sun. She chased hens.

He named her on the Saturday, for the arch she came out of.

“Third Arch,” he said. “That is your name.”

On the Sunday Cadoc Fenner came to look at her, and stood a long time.

“She is not blue,” he said at last.

“No,” said Emrys. “She has a bent ear.”

“She is a good dog,” said Cadoc, and went home up the lane.

* * *

There was a meeting at the bridge, because there was one every month. People stood under the three arches, and nobody hurried.

They got to the last item, which was whose wish had been the best use of an arch that month.

Gwilym the carter said his had been for a good trade. There was an argument about the bridge.

Then somebody said, “Third Arch, then. That one is Emrys’s.”

“It was only a dog,” said Emrys.

The woman from the mill stood up on the low wall so they could see her.

“He has got a dog,” she said, “and he has got the last arch, and the dog has a bent ear and a white sock, and she sits where he tells her to. Every one of us knows what we got. He is the only one who asked a hundred people first.”

They wrote his name on the slate by the gate, under the date, and put a line under it, which is what they do.

Emrys walked home with Third Arch at her heel, and the dog was the most ordinary dog in the village, and his name was on the slate.
