# The Bean That Asked for Water
![Cover art: The Bean That Asked for Water](../art/accessible/19-the-bean-that-asked-for-water.webp)

Jack’s bean sat in his palm like a small green egg.

Jack had found it beside the village gate. The bean was warm, smooth, and larger than his thumb. When he held it near his ear, it made a soft squeak.

“Please,” said Jack.

The bean squeaked again.

“Please grow,” Jack said. “Mother’s garden has dried. If you grow tall, I can climb to the giant’s house and ask for water.”

The bean cracked open.

A tiny mouth appeared.

“I can grow,” said the bean. “But not for climbing into a locked house.”

Jack frowned. “Everyone says there is gold inside.”

“Everyone says the path is easy when they do not walk it.”

The bean hopped onto the soil. “I will not become a ladder for taking what another person has not offered.”

Jack felt heat rise in his cheeks. He had wanted the giant’s water, not the gold. Still, the old story had made a golden shortcut sound easier than asking.

The bean shook its green leaves. “If you need water, let us solve that need.”

* * *

The village garden had one cracked rain barrel. Its lid leaned loose. Rain slipped down the sides and vanished into the ground. The soil smelled dusty. Beans, beans, and carrots drooped beneath a hot sun.

Jack studied the barrel. The bean hopped beside him.

“You do not need height,” it said. “You need direction.”

They found a broken flowerpot beneath the eaves. Jack mended its crack with clay. The bean pulled a tiny vine around the barrel. The vine grew along the gutter, leaf by leaf. It did not climb toward the giant’s house. It climbed toward the roof.

When the next rain came, the vine carried the water along the barrel. Water slid into the flowerpot instead of the dirt. The soil drank deeply. Tiny green shoots lifted their heads.

Mother came with a wooden ladle. She looked at the barrel, then at Jack.

“You found a way to ask the rain,” she said.

Jack smiled. “The bean helped.”

The bean swelled in the soil. Its leaves made a little patch of shade.

* * *

A loud voice shook the garden wall.

“Who is taking my sunlight?”

Jack’s knees knocked together. A giant stood beyond the wall. He had a red nose, a blue coat, and a garden rake taller than Jack. The giant’s boots stamped the ground.

The bean climbed halfway up Jack’s sleeve. “Do not hide behind me,” it whispered. “Speak.”

“I am Jack,” Jack said. “I am not taking your sunlight. I am asking about your wall.”

The giant lowered his rake. “My wall keeps the garden safe.”

“Your shadow makes my beans sleepy,” Jack said. “I can move my bed. I can also plant near the edge where the sun is better.”

The giant frowned. “People always ask for a favor. They never bring one.”

The bean hopped into Jack’s hand. It pointed toward the giant’s gate.

Behind it stood a garden full of dry furrows. A row of barrels leaked beside a stone wall. The giant was keeping water out of the low land, but the water was escaping before it reached his roots.

“You have a gutter problem,” the bean said.

The giant stared. “A bean is giving me advice.”

“A bean is asking for water,” it replied. “Advice is part of the answer.”

* * *

Jack and the giant met at the gate.

The giant had a deep well. His neighbors used its water, but the bucket was too heavy for them. The villagers wanted the low stream, but the giant’s wall blocked the flow. Both sides blamed the other for the brown grass.

Jack spread a clean board across the mud. He drew a line with a stick.

“Here is a plan,” he said. “The rain goes into your barrel. It runs along this new channel to the bean bed. The extra water goes down to the lower garden. Your wall stays. Our gate stays open for one hour each morning.”

The giant frowned at the drawing. “And what do I receive?”

“Seed,” Jack said. “My mother saves bean seeds. We can plant a row by your wall. You may have half the first harvest.”

The giant sniffed. “I like a fair plan.”

He lifted the leaking barrel. Jack opened the old channel. The bean vine grew along the new stones. By noon, the water sparkled in the sun.

The giant took one ordinary seed bean from Jack’s mother’s saved seeds and put it in his pocket.

“Next spring,” he said, “you may plant beside my wall.”

The speaking bean rested in the damp soil beside the new channel. It was a small green plant that had asked for water and helped a neighbor make a promise.
