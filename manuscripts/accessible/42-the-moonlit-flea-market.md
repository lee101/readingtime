# The Moonlit Flea Market

![Cover art: The Moonlit Flea Market](../art/accessible/42-the-moonlit-flea-market.webp)

Mara slipped through the hedge in her blue coat and yellow boots just as the moon climbed behind the pear tree. Pip followed between her boots, his silver tail tucked into a moss-green scarf.

“Do you smell cinnamon?” Mara whispered.

Pip pointed his pink nose toward a lane where lanterns floated like warm lanternships. The lane opened at once, revealing the moonlit flea market.

Stalls crowded beneath strings of silver bells. Spoons chimed softly whenever the breeze passed. Baskets held thimbles, spools, cracked teacups, and jars of buttons sorted by color. The smells of toasted oats, clove smoke, and cold apples mingled with the damp scent of mushrooms.

A small brass bell hung over the main gate. Mara had seen it last spring, when the market had borrowed the old spring gate as its entrance.

Tonight the space beneath it was empty.

A woman in a patchwork cloak examined a wooden horse. “The bell is gone,” she said. “Without it, the borrowed doorways will close at midnight.”

Across the market, a tiny door appeared beneath a stall table. It was only Mara’s hand high, with a purple glass button for a doorknob. A brass knob inside turned once, plucky and impatient.

A button had become a door.

Mara crouched beside it. “Maybe the lost buttons make the doorways.”

Pip sniffed the threshold. A thread of golden air curled around his whiskers and led beneath the table.

Together they crawled through.

The little world beyond was no bigger than a tea chest. Shelves curved overhead, crowded with objects too large for them: a thimble like a bell tower, a spool like a striped well, and dozens of lost buttons. Every button had become a round door. Through the gaps, Mara glimpsed familiar places: the warm kitchen at home, a boat shed smelling of rain, and the hollow below the pear tree.

The golden thread led to a tall wooden box. A hand-lettered sign hung inside—but the market’s magic had folded its message into dancing pencil lines.

Pip jumped onto the box.

The lid banged shut.

“Let us out!” Mara called.

The button doors trembled. Outside, the midnight bell began its first warning chime.

Mara seized a loose thread and pulled. It did not break. Instead, a needle popped from a pincushion, pricking her glove. Pip used his teeth to tug free a pearl-headed pin. Together they pried the lid open.

A beam of moonlight shone through. They slid home into the lamplight.

The missing bell lay beneath a stall of empty jars. Its clapper had become a tiny door. The bell itself had rolled beneath a stack of tin plates and stopped when the clapper swung shut, trapping the market’s ringing inside.

Pip pawed at the bell. It would not budge.

Mara could lift one plate, but the others would tumble down. She tucked Pip inside the bell and crawled beneath the stall. Tin pressed her blue coat against the ground. She lifted the first plate, then the second, grunting as the stack shifted.

“We need something to hold them,” she said.

A nearby stallholder tied three empty seed packets onto a twig. They puffed into soft, round cushions.

A baker dusted the cushions with flour so Mara’s gloves would grip. A tailor looped a measuring ribbon around the plates. A woodcarver wedged a block beneath the bell. Pip raced from one helper to another, fetching the button that matched each tiny door.

The stack shifted.

The cushions squeaked. The wood block tipped. The little clapper-door flew shut.

Mara caught the bell against her stomach. Pip leapt and caught the clapper’s door before it clicked. For a moment they held it between them, Mara’s hands on the bell and Pip’s paws on the tiny knob.

The midnight chime rolled through the market.

Without the clapper, the bell made no sound. The button doors all began to close.

Mara remembered the golden thread.

“Keep it open, Pip.”

She unwound the thread from the button door where they had escaped. Then she looped it around the bell’s clapper and tied its free end to the wooden horse above the stall. The horse creaked forward. The thread pulled tight. Pip held the clapper-door steady.

Now they rang the bell together.

Clang!

The sound was small at first. It traveled through the jars, cups, spoons, and hanging lanterns. Button doors swung open. The wooden horse lurched once more, and the clapper rang again.

Clang!

Every stall joined the rescue. A bootmaker struck the bell with his wooden lasts. The spoon seller chimed with three silver teaspoons. A gardener rang her small brass hoe against a flowerpot. Each clean note opened another lost-button doorway.

Clang, clang, clang!

The tiny doors stood wide. People retrieved forgotten things from distant rooms and gardens. A blue mitten came through first. Then a crooked cup, a ship’s key, and a paper star.

At last, the clapper-door popped free in Pip’s paws.

He dropped it inside the bell.

The market’s great brass note rang across the moonlit stalls. The crowded little lanes of the market unfolded again, and the pear-tree gate appeared, shining with dew.

The stallholders cheered. The tailor unwound her measuring ribbon. The baker brushed flour from Mara’s sleeves. Pip bowed, though his scarf had already fallen over one ear.

Mara held the missing bell carefully. Now its clapper was an ordinary round clapper again.

Behind them, the woman in the patchwork cloak lifted it onto its silver hook.

“A borrowed doorway needs a voice,” she said, “or it forgets where it belongs.”

Mara looked back at the button doors. In each tiny opening, someone waved.

“Good thing ours were very loud,” she said.

She and Pip slipped through the pear-tree gate carrying the sweet scent of cinnamon. Behind them, the market bell rang once more, soft and bright, and every lost button found its door.
