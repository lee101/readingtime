# The Slipper That Fitted Nobody
![Cover art: The Slipper That Fitted Nobody](../art/accessible/16-the-slipper-that-fitted-nobody.webp)

The glass slipper rested on a velvet cushion in the palace hall. It flashed in the candlelight, clear as a cupped spring. A blue cord was still caught in one heel.

Mara saw it while carrying her master’s tools. Mara was the cobbler’s apprentice. She was small, but she knew shoes by the stories their leather told.

This slipper had been hurried.

Its left edge was rough. Tiny bubbles ran through the clear glass. The blue cord had been pushed into a crack, not sewn through it. The palace cobbler had patched the heel in less than a minute. He had forgotten to pull out the old thread.

That made Mara curious. The slipper was famous because it fit the lost girl at the ball. Yet the fit looked narrow in the toe room and tight over the arch. Could it have fitted anyone?

The next morning, Mara followed the sparkle of the slipper into the long palace corridor. Near the kitchen, a woman hurried past with a jug on one hip. Water spilled over her sleeve. One of her shoes was missing.

Her bare heel was red and swollen. Blue flour stuck around her ankle. The woman hurried on, trying not to limp.

Mara ran after her. “Please, may I see your shoe?”

The woman looked around. “I haven’t time. The prince has ordered every shoe in the kingdom searched.”

“I am a cobbler’s apprentice. I may find the error before your foot becomes worse.”

The woman’s name was Ada. She had attended the ball as a kitchen helper. In the crowded hall, her partner had dropped a stack of saucers. Ada had kicked off her shoe as she bent to gather them. Then the crowd closed in. A palace guard saw the fallen glass shoe. The prince heard the story before anyone checked the other foot.

In the ballroom, Ada had worn only a thin sock. She had not been a mystery. She had been overlooked.

* * *

Later, Mara showed her master the glass slipper. He sniffed. “Royal work is not for us to question.”

“The heel is cracked,” Mara said. “And the thread was cut, not broken. Someone hurried.”

A bell rang in the palace yard. The prince had arranged another fitting for the shoe.

Mara wrapped Ada’s heel in a clean cloth and cooled it with well water. “When your foot is ready,” she said, “we will test the shoes on a last. No more steps.”

The prince was waiting. “The search will not wait.”

“Then it can wait five minutes.”

Mara measured Ada’s foot and drew its shape on paper. They tested each shoe on the last. A baker’s wide clog was too broad. A soft boot was too short. A small silver shoe looked right at first, but its lining slipped loose when Mara bent the last.

The palace grew quiet.
The prince leaned forward. “Why do you care about the cook?”

“Because no shoe should choose a person for her,” Mara said. “And because this one needs mending.”

Ada stood straight. “My name is Ada Numm. I made the blue cord for this slipper. I used waxed linen because it would not snap in the rain. I put three blue knots under the heel. Look.”

The three knots were still there. The prince looked from Ada to the slipper. At last, he asked her to try it.

This time, Mara loosened the hidden seam first. The slipper slid over Ada’s swollen foot. It fitted from heel to toe.

* * *

The palace wanted to call the moment a happy mistake. Mara knew the truth had more edges.

The prince had seen a shoe and decided that its owner must be beautiful. He had ordered feet to march without asking what they could bear. His guards had looked for a lost princess. They had not looked for a woman with a hurt heel and a busy kitchen.

“I am sorry,” the prince told Ada. “I nearly chose the wrong person, and you suffered for it.”

Ada lowered her eyes, then lifted them. “Thank you for saying it plainly.”

Mara passed the glass slipper to the palace cobbler. “Cut the heel open. I will show you how to mend it without another scar.”

That evening, the palace workers made a wider fitting room. They put a low chair beside a basin of warm water. They gave each person time to sit. They wrote down the names of everyone who had been made to stand in line.

Ada’s name was first. The prince added his own beneath it, with the words *I chose without asking.*

Mara’s master leaned over the new list. “What is that for?”

“For finding every heel we forgot,” Mara said.

* * *

The repaired slipper never went back on the cushion. Ada kept it for market days, when her feet were rested and her blue-corded shoes were mended.

The prince sent a pair of soft slippers to the kitchen. Ada accepted them for the cold stone floors, but she did not thank him with a curtsy. She told him which rooms had leaks. He sent a mason. The floors were dry by the next rain.

Mara was given a commission of her own. She made narrow shoes with firm heels and a little brass bell near each ankle. The bells gave a clear ring, so a hurried court could hear when someone limped.

When the prince tried her newest pair, he stepped carefully. He asked whether the toe felt soft. He asked before putting on another.

Mara listened to the bell. It rang across the quiet hall, bright and steady.

She smiled. This time, a shoe had helped someone speak clearly.
