# The Lighthouse Made of Worn Buttons

![Cover art: The Lighthouse Made of Worn Buttons](../art/accessible/73-the-lighthouse-made-of-worn-buttons.webp)

Pim’s grandad kept a blue tin on the shelf in the hall.

For fifty years, every button that came off one of his coats went into it. Brass, bone, and small wooden ones with a hole worn through.

“What do you keep in the tin, Grandad?”

“Coats,” said Grandad Kai. “That is all. Fifty years of coats.”

This year his hands were too slow to sew a button back on. He said that once, and never said it again.

* * *

The coastguard came on a Thursday with his book. His name was Tomas Harrow, and his new coat had no buttons.

“I am to clear the old kit out of the station,” he said. “There is a tin on your shelf with a lid and a handle.”

Grandad Kai took the tin down and held it in both hands.

“It is the same one,” he said. “Your grandfather sold it to me. My name is not on it anywhere.”

He turned the lid over. Scratched inside it with a nail was a name.

“W. Harrow,” said Pim.

“It is the only name I have got. Take the tin.”

* * *

That is how the lighthouse came to be.

Pim emptied the tin onto the table, over three days, in the order they had gone in. Grandad Kai told her which coat each one came off. His hands could not do the work. His head could do that.

They carried them down to the shingle in a sack.

“Coastguard,” said Pim, “we are building a lighthouse.”

Tomas Harrow looked out at the flat grey sea.

“With buttons,” he said.

“With fifty years of buttons,” said Pim. “A boat only has to see it. That is all a light has to do.”

He wrote it down, and his face went red.

* * *

They built it round and low, taller than Pim. At the top they set a tin lamp on a post, and inside it a candle and a wad of string dipped in fat, because a light needs something to burn.

The stones at the foot were grey and wet. Every button went in with two fingers. Pim put the oldest at the bottom.

Sixpence the dog watched every button go in.

At the top she ran out of good ones, so she tipped the leftovers from the jar into the gap and pressed them in any way at all.

“There,” she said. “It is round.”

Grandad Kai said it was round.

* * *

The real storm came on a Thursday in October. It blew from the north-west, and the spray went over the sea wall. Grandad Kai sat by the hut stove with a blanket over his knees. He had walked from the car, and that was all he was doing tonight.

“The Marigold is late,” said Tomas. “She is out past the point with four men on her, and my light will not work.”

The candle was wet. The string was wet. The lid had held the rain in, and the lamp was a dark pool inside.

* * *

Pim put her hand on the tower at knee height. The button under her palm was warm.

Not stove-warm. Not pocket-warm. Warm the way a coat is warm when it has hung all day on a chair in the sun.

“It is going,” she said.

It was going up. One button, then the one above, then that, a slow amber line climbing the wall in the wind. Then it stopped.

It stopped eight buttons from the top, at the crooked band where Pim had tipped in the leftovers.

“Grandad,” said Pim, “those are the wrong ones. I put the wrong ones in.”

“Yes,” said Grandad Kai, from the chair.

“I can get them out.”

* * *

Tomas held the lamp and did not go up the ladder. Pim went up in the rain, and the amber light from below was enough to see by. Sixpence the dog shouted at the sky at the foot of the ladder.

The eight took a long time. Her fingers were cold for an hour.

Then she counted along the wall with her thumb, oldest first, and put them in where they belonged.

The light ran up the last eight. Out past the point, the Marigold turned and came home.

* * *

“I shall have to put it on the chart,” said Tomas. “A light has to have a name. I could call it Harrow Light.”

“No,” said Pim.

“It is the station’s tin. It is the least I can do.”

Grandad Kai leaned forward. His voice went when it started.

“It was never the buttons, Tomas. I kept them fifty years for the one name in that lid, and now it is on a chart, and I did not have to ask.”

* * *

They did not move the lighthouse. That was settled on the chart, and it stayed settled.

It is still on the shingle above the wall, the size of a sheep pen. Every chart printed since has a small ring on it where the tower is, under the name Kai Light.

Pim was nine before she could read a chart. Tomas set her on the stool by the office window, and she put her finger on the ring.

“Small,” she said. “For a light.”

“That is where the boat was,” said Tomas. “That is the only thing a chart has to be right about.”
