# The Sock Drawer of the Wind

![Cover art: The Sock Drawer of the Wind](../art/accessible/82-the-sock-drawer-of-the-wind.webp)

Mum had no socks. That was the start of it.

She stood at the sock drawer with it pulled right out, and she moved the socks from one pile to another pile, and the piles did not get any better.

“Two of everything,” she said. “One of nothing.”

Dee, who was six, came to watch. She counted them out on the carpet. Nine socks. Mum needed eighteen. The machine in the kitchen was old and round on top, and it ticked while it cooled.

“Is it broken?” said Dee.

“It is old,” said Mum. “Old things do one job well. This one loses a sock.” She said it the way people say the price of bread.

* * *

The wind was in the back of it.

Dee put her hand in after the door was shut, because you have to feel a gap to know it is a gap. The gap was the width of her finger, and the gap was cold. Not cupboard cold. Outside cold, with rain and grass in it.

Something small and soft went past her hand. Whoosh.

“Stop,” said Dee.

She put her head in and looked.

“Stop it,” she said to the machine.

The machine ticked and said nothing at all, which was its one good habit.

* * *

The sock was not in the grass. It was on the roof.

Dee saw it from the kitchen window, a small grey thing up on the ridge, where nobody goes. She got the ladder out of the shed. Mum said not to go on the ladder. Then she came out and took hold of the bottom of it with both hands, which is the other half of not going on the ladder.

The wind up there was blowing steady from the west. It did not take the sock. It went round it.

There was one sock on the ridge. Then there was the row behind it.

Eleven more, and not a tangle, and not a heap. They stood up on the tiles in two lines with their toes turned out, the way a shop sets out bread.

Dee sat back on the ridge with her boots over the gutter.

“Who did that?” she said.

The wind in the chimney drew one long breath. Every sock lifted a little and settled down again.

Dee stayed up there until her legs went numb. The wind stayed with her the whole time and did not take one.

* * *

The washing line was a door. That is what it turned out to be. You could speak into it and it carried on, the way a wire carries between two houses.

Dee held a sock up in the air and talked down the line to the wind.

“You are keeping them for the washing day,” she said. “This is what you are doing.”

The line hummed up. It was glad to be found out.

“Mum says you are stealing them.”

The line hummed down.

“If I give you one of mine, on my own, will you let the rest go?”

A long hum, and the washing on the line turned all its pockets to the left at once.

“Then take that one,” said the wind. “I will bring them back on Mondays, and I will know the girl who asked.”

Dee pegged the grey sock on the line in the middle, where it would be seen. Then she went down the ladder, and Mum let go of it, and neither of them said a word about any of it.

* * *

On Monday there was a noise on the roof like somebody shaking out a blanket. It stopped.

Mum did the washing, same as always, and she did not count the socks first. That is how she knew.

The socks came down in two lines on the grass. They were matched, and folded over once, the way a shop folds a towel, and the grey one from the line was on the top with the rest of them.

“That is all of them,” said Mum.

Dee carried them in and put them in the drawer on the top shelf, in rows. Eighteen that evening. Eighteen the Monday after that, and the Monday after that, folded and paired, in and out, in and out, in and out.
