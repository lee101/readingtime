# The Compass Rose in Her Pocket

Brill’s brass pocket compass did not point north.

She had wanted it to point north all week. Her family had left the house on Weaver Street in June and moved two streets over to a flat with a back yard and no stairs. She wanted the needle to walk her back so she could look in the box room.

“Point north,” said Brill, and turned all the way round on the grass. The red point went left, then right, then stopped.

“North is that way,” she said, and pointed at the cold chimney. “The chimney knows where north is.”

The needle swung round and pointed at the fence.

* * *

She went to see Old Puck at the pawn shop. He had been taking in things in since before Brill was born, and never asked why.

He was polishing a kettle.

“Your compass is rude,” he said.

“It is broken.”

“Nothing is broken. There is a mark on the glass, where a man who wanted to go somewhere put his thumb.”

“I want it to point at my old house,” she said.

Old Puck gave the compass back and sat down on his stool.

“A thing that helps you find what is lost will not tell you where you have been,” he said.

He picked up the kettle again. The needle was pointing at the shop door.

* * *

The railings at the end of the lane had blue posts and iron spikes. A mitten was jammed on the top spike, one of a pair, gone hard as a shell, with the thumb torn off.

“That is not mine,” said Brill. “Where did you come from? I have never seen you before.”

The needle went past the mitten and kept on down the street. Then it swung back, then went past again, quicker.

“Both,” said Brill.

“Fine. We will go that way then.”

She got up on the low wall and hooked the mitten off the spike with her sleeve. It came away with a crackle. There was paper sewn inside the cuff with a name on it in pencil. She got it out with her fingernails, and the needle went west.

* * *

The shop it pointed at was the laundry. Outside it, on a bench, there was a boy with a bag between his feet.

He was about her own age, with his hands up his sleeves.

“Are you lost?” said Brill.

“No,” he said, very quietly.

“You are sitting on a bench.”

“My mum is inside. She is putting the washing in.”

“Then you are not lost, and I am not asking where you live. I am asking if you lost a mitten.”

The boy did not put his hands out.

“Yes,” he said.

* * *

Then he shut his eyes, and Brill saw his mouth move twice, and nothing came out at all.

“You do not have to say it out loud,” said Brill. “I can read it.”

She held her thumb under the number.

“Fourteen Birtley Row,” she said.

The boy opened his eyes.

“That is our row,” he said. “We were at the top of it, the house with the iron gate. We came away on Tuesday. It went out of my head.”

He pulled a letter out of his coat.

“I have a letter for my gran and I cannot write.”

Brill got a pencil off the counter and wrote the number on the back of the paper. He said it after her, to be sure.

The needle in Brill’s hand swung wide, right around, and then settled. It did not go north. It went down to the paper and stayed there.

* * *

Brill shut the lid and looked at her two hands.

Then she took the blue mitten out of her coat and put it on. It was stiff, a size too big, and had no thumb.

The needle spun round and stopped, pointing straight at her hand.

“It is mine,” Brill said. “It was lost, and now I have got it.”

She pulled the mitten over her fingers. “I will keep you,” said Brill.

She thought about the shelf in the box room she had not looked at. Then she thought about the compass. It would never take her back to a house. It would only take her on to the next lost thing.

* * *

She took the compass to Old Puck on the Monday. He turned it over, looked at the mark on the glass, and said nothing for a long time. That was how she knew it was worth something.

“I want a windmill,” she said. “The paper ones, with the stick.”

He took one off the top shelf and put it in her hand, and put the compass in his drawer, and that was the trade.

Brill came back to the laundry. Tamm was on the bench with the letter in his hand.

“The postman is here,” said Brill.

“He comes down the row and calls out the numbers,” said Tamm.

The postman called out the numbers. When he called fourteen, the boy stood up and held the letter out.

“Fourteen Birtley Row,” he said, out loud. “For my gran.”

The postman put the letter in his bag, and Tamm watched him write the number on his hand.

“Are you pleased now?” said Brill.

“Yes,” said Tamm. “I said it out loud to a man I did not know.”

“It has your name in it,” said Brill. “I am going to keep it.”

Brill waved the blue mitten at him, and spun her windmill on its stick all the way home, in and out of the gutters.
