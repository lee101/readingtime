# The Acorn With a Name on Its Shell

![Cover art: The Acorn With a Name on Its Shell](../art/accessible/23-the-acorn-with-a-name-on-its-shell.webp)

Tova’s red coat was too long by two fingers. It swished around her boots whenever she hurried along the forest post-office path. A green cap covered her ears, and a yellow scarf bounced behind her like a small flag. At six, she was the youngest junior runner allowed to carry acorn mail.

On the morning when the first frost was predicted, Postmistress Pell placed an acorn on Tova’s desk.

“Do not shake it,” Pell said. “A letter is inside.”

The acorn had a brown cap and a smooth golden shell. Across one side, dark letters had grown into the shell.

To The Tree With the Warmest Window

Tova read the name twice. The only tree she knew with a window was Old Brann. He stood behind the post office, and a round amber window ringed with red wool had been built into his hollow trunk. When Pell banked the fire, that window glowed like honey.

“Is Brann sick?” Tova asked.

Pell shook her head. “He is homesick. This letter must reach him before winter.”

Brann’s low branches drooped beside the wall. They had lifted happily when sparrows called from his roof. Now every nest was empty. Cold wind slipped through his bare branches and made the post-office shutters knock.

Tova placed the acorn in her leather satchel and set out.

* * *

At the first forked path, three pale seams appeared on the acorn. One pointed toward a bakery. One pointed toward a green hill. The last trembled between them.

Pell’s words came back to Tova. When a letter had only a description instead of a name, it followed the strongest matching scent. One runner could be tricked by a warm smell. Three runners, each holding a seam, braided their scents together. Then the acorn would keep the same true destination, even when the paths moved at sunset.

Tova watched two seams pull at once. The hill seam pulled north. The baker’s chimney sent out the smell of cinnamon toast, and the last seam swung toward it.

She tied the acorn securely in her satchel. Even if the seams shifted, the letter was safe.

At the berry hedge she met Bram, another junior runner. He wore a blue cap and had snow dust on his shoulders.

“Is the acorn for the bakery?” he asked.

“For Brann.”

Bram frowned. “The toast smells very certain.”

“We need the true scent first.”

At the post office, Pell opened the delivery book. Old Brann had signed every page for forty years. His fingers had left pale marks beside his name.

Tova and Bram rubbed clean note paper across those marks. Brann’s scent came away: rain on warm bark, crushed juniper, and the dusty smell of an old wool coat.

They folded the scented paper around the acorn.

* * *

The sun touched the treetops.

Tova took the acorn from her satchel. She held one seam. Bram held another. Pell, dressed in her long green coat, held the third. They agreed to step on the same count.

“One. Two. Three.”

They moved together.

Behind them, the path began to turn. A stone wall slid between two trees, and the well they had passed popped up ahead. This was the forest’s sunset trick. It moved every path from its old place.

Tova’s seam tugged toward the well. Bram’s tugged north. The acorn trembled hard.

“Don’t follow one,” Pell said. “Keep the notes close.”

Tova unfolded the first note. Juniper berries grew beside a bent birch.

They turned that way. On the second page, wool fibers clung to the corner, exactly like the red wool wrapped around Brann’s hollow window. They followed it to a low wooden bridge.

Pell’s corner smelled faintly of rain. Ahead, a young oak had a hollow just like Brann’s.

“Rain on bark,” Bram said.

They crossed together, one pace at a time. The acorn shone between their hands. Behind them, the well vanished. Ahead, the river changed direction, but their three joined seams pulled the letter through the center of the true path.

The sky turned pink, then purple.

One last page showed an acorn-cap mark beside a note: Warmest window. Just beyond the last fern, they saw Old Brann. The round window in his trunk shone against the dusk.

Bram counted. “One. Two. Three.”

Tova held out the acorn.

Brann’s branch lifted it gently from her hand. The shell opened along its seams. Inside lay a thin oak leaf and a note from a sapling in the northern forest.

The leaf smelled of snow, pine, and the place Brann had grown before the post office was built.

I keep your old branch beside my window, the note said. I remember your roots. When spring comes, I will send my first leaf.

For the first time in weeks, Brann lifted his crown. The round window shone brighter, and the post-office shutters stopped knocking. His branches reached toward the northern sky.

Pell tied the note to the warm window. Tova returned the empty acorn shell to the mail basket. The next morning, frost silvered the path, but Brann stood tall, remembering the forest that had not forgotten him.
