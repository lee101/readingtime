# The Bread That Ran Away

The bread came out of the oven at half past six.

Nella was six, and small for it, and she sat peeling shells off plums. Odile was at the bench with four baskets of dough waiting.

The loaf on the top of the rack was brown and pale at the ends. It was the best of the morning, and it was finished.

It looked at the window.

Then it rolled off the rack, over the bench, and out through the open door.

“Grandmother,” said Nella. “The bread has gone out.”

Odile did not look up. “Which bread, Nella.”

“The brown one. The best one. It went out of the door and it is going down the lane.”

Odile put the last loaf in a basket. “Then shut the door,” she said, “or the wind will get my fire.”

* * *

Nella went out after it, and did not shut the door.

Pudding the dog was sitting by the pump. He watched the loaf go past, then went after it too, and Nella ran to catch him.

“You leave that bread alone,” she told him. “You are not hungry. You had a bone.”

He got within one paw of it on the bridge, and the loaf stopped, and the dog stopped. Then he went and drank from the rain pipe.

“I have to fetch a dog,” said Nella. “That is all I am doing.”

The loaf waited until Nella had caught up. Then it went on.

* * *

“I am not chasing you,” said Nella, running. “You are going too quick. Slow down.”

It did not go any slower.

They went up the hill into the wind, and the loaf rolled on with its crust gone pale and hard.

A farm dog came out of the top field. He ran at the loaf and missed it, then ran at it again, and Nella shouted at him.

“What am I to do with you?” said Nella. “Go on home. Go home, you daft thing.”

The loaf went on for twenty steps, and stopped, and waited, and let Nella come up beside it, the same as before.

“He has not got us,” she said. “You do not have to be quick.”

The wind took her hat into the grass. She did not go back for it.

* * *

At the top of the hill was a woodcutter’s house, with wood stacked under a tarpaulin and the handle on the wrong side.

The loaf rolled up to the door and stopped. It did not go on.

“It has stopped being bread,” said Nella.

She put her hand on it. It was a bundle of split dry wood tied in the middle with string, the kind that burns for an hour.

The door opened. A woodcutter stood there in a shirt with no sleeves. Behind him the house was as cold as the hill, with no fire in the grate and no cloth on the table.

“I have brought you the end of a loaf of bread,” said Nella, and then she stopped, because that was not true any more.

“Is it bread?” said the woodcutter.

“No,” said Nella. “It is what it wanted to be.”

“Come in,” said the woodcutter. “Do not stand in the cold holding the door.”

* * *

He cut the string off, carried the wood in to the hearth in three trips, lit it, and stood looking at it.

“That will last me till Thursday,” he said.

Then he cut the bundle in half with his axe and gave her one half.

“Take that home for your oven,” he said. “You are small enough to lift it and I am not.”

“Do you want to keep all of it?”

“I have got a hearth,” said the woodcutter. “Go on.”

* * *

Odile was still at the bench, with four loaves under the cloth and the rye man’s money in her hand.

“You were gone a long time,” she said.

“Grandmother. Why did the bread run away?”

Odile took the cloth off the last loaf and looked at it.

“It was finished,” she said. “Finished things look for their use.”

“Did you see where it went?”

“Not far,” said Odile. “Put the wood down by the oven and bank the fire.”

* * *

The next morning the oven made four loaves, and Nella carried one up the hill herself, held in both arms, and it was warm at the top.

She left it on the step. The woodcutter came out and stood in the doorway holding it.

“You did not have to,” he said.

“I know,” said Nella. “It was finished.”

He gave her the loose ends off his chopping block. There was enough for two pockets, and they were dry and light and smelled of pine.

“What am I to do with kindling?” said Nella.

“Bake,” said the woodcutter.

She came down the hill with them. Her hat was still in the grass by the bridge.
