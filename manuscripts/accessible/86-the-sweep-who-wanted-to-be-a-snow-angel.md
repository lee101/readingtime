# The Sweep Who Wanted to Be a Snow Angel

![Cover art: The Sweep Who Wanted to Be a Snow Angel](../art/accessible/86-the-sweep-who-wanted-to-be-a-snow-angel.webp)

The snow came in the night, and in the morning Tam went up the ladder to the chimney at number four. He was seven and a half, and he was the sweep of that street, with one brush and a hat with no brim.

Somebody had made an angel in the snow at the bottom of the lane. Tam came down the ladder and looked at it. Round head, two arms, a long trail behind it. A boy about four was patting the snow back onto one arm.

“Was that hard?” said Tam.

The boy looked up. “Which bit?”

“Making it.” Tam sat down in the snow to be nearer it. “Is it difficult?”

“You have to do the arms last,” said the boy.

* * *

Tam wanted to make one, and he wanted somebody to stand and watch him do it.

He tried the corner by the coal shed, where the snow was thin and full of gravel. He tried the yard behind the church, and the cook came out of the door and told him to move along, because the dogs were after her peelings.

That left the square. The town had cleared the noticeboard that morning, so there was a wide patch of clean snow under it with nobody’s boots in it.

* * *

He worked on the angel all morning. He rolled two balls for the head. He drew the arms out long, and put a curl of snow on the tip of each one, like a hand. He turned the head to face up the lane, so the angel was looking where the sun comes from.

He stood back and looked at it.

“That is a good one,” said Tam. He was talking to the square, and the square did not answer.

Then he had time over, so he built a snowman beside it, with a tin lid for a hat.

The baker’s boy came round the corner with a tray of hot pies. His boot caught the edge of the snow, and the tray went up into the air.

The pies came down all around him like rain.

He came down on the angel’s face, and the tin lid went into the snowman, and the snowman’s head rolled to the kerb.

* * *

Tam looked at what was left. It was a long smear, with the head at one end of it, like a boot print.

“You have stood on it,” he said.

The baker’s boy had flour on his knees and a pie still in his hand. The colour went out of his face.

“I am sorry,” he said. And then he stopped, because there was nothing else he knew how to say.

“You are sorry,” said Tam. “You have taken the face off with your boot.”

* * *

Nan came across the square with her basket. She looked down at the smear, and then at the boy, and then at Tam.

“It is snow,” said Nan. “Go home. Your hands are wet.”

* * *

The boy did not go home. He put his tray on the wall, got down on his knees in the wet snow beside the smear, and began to fix it.

He rolled a new head and pushed it into the place where the old one had been. He joined it to the arm with a sweep of his hand, so there was no join at all. He made the arms shorter than Tam’s, and the hands fatter.

It took him the rest of the afternoon. His knees were soaked through, and he did not stop.

“You cannot bake,” said Nan. “And you cannot say sorry when you have ruined something. But you can make the best angel in this square, and you make it better than he does.”

The baker’s boy said nothing to that. He put his hand flat on the angel’s chest, and then he took it away again, because he was not sure whether that was allowed.

* * *

Tam’s angel stood on the left of the board and the baker’s boy’s stood on the right. They stayed all winter under the edge of the roof.

The baker’s boy pointed at them at anybody who stood still long enough. He told the whole story over and over, with the tray going up. Nan said he would not stop, and he did not, and Tam let him.

There are two angels in the town square that winter, side by side, and the baker’s boy is insufferable about it for months, and Tam lets him be.
