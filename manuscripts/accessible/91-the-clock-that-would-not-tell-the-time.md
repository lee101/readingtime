# The Clock That Would Not Tell the Time

![Cover art: The Clock That Would Not Tell the Time](../art/accessible/91-the-clock-that-would-not-tell-the-time.webp)

Hester had been at the school for nine days when she looked up at the clock.

It was above the door in the hall, round and wooden, with a white face, black numbers, and a small bell on top. Children ran past it all day and never looked at it. Hester was new, and she looked at things.

The hands stood at ten past ten.

“You have not wound it,” she said.

Mr Bray, the caretaker, was on a chair with his sleeves rolled up. He got down and looked up.

“Wound every Friday since the summer,” he said. “Since the summer before that.”

“Does it tick?”

“It does not.”

* * *

Hester asked to help. Mr Bray said it was not her clock, and then said that it was, because it hung on her wall as much as his.

So she tried everything a person can try. She wound the key until her wrist hurt. She tapped the glass with her knuckle. She put a pencil on the minute hand and walked it round to four o’clock. The hands stayed there with great obedience, like two nails.

* * *

The hall was loud all day. Two hundred children in and out, wet coats, boots, a drum. Every time she looked up, the clock was doing nothing in the middle of it, like a stone in a river.

“Why does a clock make a sound, Mr Bray?”

“To tell you the time, lass.”

“No. Why does it go tick, tick, tick? Nobody needs the time that much.”

He stood up off the chair and folded his cloth.

“It has a back on it,” he said. “If you are going to look at it, look properly. Get the ladder out. Mind the third rung. It goes.”

* * *

The back came off with a bit of turning. Behind it was a flat brass plate, green at the edges. Hester read it standing on the second rung with the ladder wobbling.

It said: This clock tells days.

“That is the whole of what it says.”

“Then that is the whole of what it does.”

Hester got down and stood in front of the face. A day is much bigger than a minute, and this clock had no number for a minute, so she asked it the only question she had left.

“Show me a day,” she said.

The hands went round fast, faster than hands have any business going, and the little second hand chased after them, and Hester shut her eyes. It went on about as long as it takes to count to twenty.

Then it stopped. Both hands pointing straight down at six o’clock, the bottom of the face, where nothing interesting is.

The bell on top did not ring. Not a peep.

* * *

Hester’s face went hot. She had made a speech to a clock, and all she had got for it was six o’clock and a bell that would not even try.

“That was a very slow way of saying nothing,” she said.

“A day has no sound,” said Mr Bray.

She turned round. He was not looking at the clock. He was looking at the door.

“There is no bell at the end of a day. You have been waiting for a noise, and there is not going to be one, and there never was.”

“Then how do you know when it is gone?”

“You watch it,” said Mr Bray. “Watch the whole thing go by, and do not say a word, and see where it stops.”

* * *

Hester waited for the noise to end, and it did not end on its own. Mr Bray walked out and shut the door, and the children and the boots and the drum went on without them, heard through the wood as a sort of weather. Then he came back and stood at the foot of the ladder and said nothing, which for him was a great deal.

The second hand went first. It moved one notch and stopped. It moved another notch. The hour hand came along with it, a fat brown finger, creeping round so slowly that Hester could watch one chip of a tooth go past. The hall went quiet except for that. The hand went on.

* * *

When it stopped, the short hand was not at the bottom. It was up near the top, sitting on the third of September, which was the day Hester had walked in through that door with a bag she had packed herself.

She looked at it a long while.

“Mr Bray,” she said. “It gave me the day I came, and it is going to point at the first day of term for the whole of the year, and that is a promise about the year.”

Mr Bray did not say that it was not a promise. He picked up his cloth and wiped his hands, looking at the clock the whole time he did it.

“Right,” he said. “You had better write that down.”

* * *

Hester asked it for a day every Friday after that, and every Friday it gave her one, and she wrote each of them on a card and kept the cards in a tin under her bed. Her birthday. The day the snow came. The day she lost a tooth in the hall.

And Mr Bray asked it for a day as well, on a Friday in March, when the hall was empty and the only one who saw was Hester. He wrote it on the back of an old wood delivery note, and folded it in four, and put it in his coat pocket, and it was still there in the summer.
