# The Cat Who Counted the Clouds

![Cover art: The Cat Who Counted the Clouds](../art/accessible/74-the-cat-who-counted-the-clouds.webp)

On the farm, the counting was Bracken’s job.

Maren was six, and she could get as far as ten and then she began again at one. So the counting was Bracken’s. Bracken sat in the grass with her paws tucked and her tail curled round, and everything on the farm was counted by her.

The slate on the door said eleven. That was sheep. Somebody had written it there in March, in chalk, and had not rubbed it out since.

Maren did the sheep first, because the sheep were the point. She counted along the wall, and she got to nine, and then she counted the black one at the bottom again, because it looked bigger than the rest.

“That is a sheep,” said Bracken.

“It is a sheep,” said Maren.

So Bracken did it after her. She went down the row with her nose, all the way to the gate, and then she sat down in the grass.

“Ten,” said Maren.

* * *

The slate said eleven, and there were ten, and Maren did not like a thing that would not sit still like that. So she started on the clouds.

Bracken counted the clouds. She sat in the middle of the top field with her head up, and as each one came up over the hill she said a number at it, and then she said the next number at the next one.

None of them looked like anything in particular, and there were more of them than Maren had numbers for.

“Two,” she said.

“Two,” said Maren.

“Five,” said Bracken. “Six. Seven.”

Maren put up her fingers with her other hand. Seven. She looked up for the next one. Then she looked again, and the sky had gone on without them.

“Eight?” she said.

Bracken did not say eight. Bracken sat in the grass and waited, and she did not look at Maren, and she did not look at the sky.

* * *

The sun came along the hill. Maren had one finger left up.

“You are not counting,” she said.

Bracken sat in the grass and waited.

“You count slow,” said Maren.

Bracken turned her head. She got up, walked to the fence, and went along it with her tail straight out. She stopped at the corner of the barn and sat down and looked at the ground.

Maren came to look too. There was a white shape on the grass behind the barn, flat and round, with two little points at one end. The wind moved it, and the points did not move, because they were not cloud-points. They were ears.

“That is the eleven,” said Maren.

* * *

They worked it out with a stick in the wet grass. Ten sheep, and one behind the barn, and the one behind the barn was a cloud the shape of a sheep lying down, waiting to be counted.

Then Maren got the carpenter’s pencil off the shelf, and she turned the flour sack over on the kitchen table, and she wrote on the back of it.

“Bracken counts,” she said. “And I write. Then it is not gone when we look away.”

She wrote the date at the top, and then the sheep: ten, and one more. Then she wrote the clouds. One, two, three, four, five.

Bracken jumped up on the table and sat on the sack, and looked at the pencil, and looked at the window.

* * *

They did it every fair day after that, and then on the wet days as well, because there is more to count when it rains.

Bracken sat on the sack by the window. She named a cloud, and Maren wrote it down, and the number sat still on the sacking where anybody could come in and read it. When a cloud got away over the fields Maren had to lean out of the window to see it, and Bracken waited for her to find it first, and then she said the number.

By autumn the back of the flour sack was full, in two kinds of pencil, because the barn man had borrowed it in June. The last line of the autumn list said forty-one. At the top of the sack, under the March date, it said ten, and then one more.
