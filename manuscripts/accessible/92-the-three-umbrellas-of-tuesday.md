# The Three Umbrellas of Tuesday

![Cover art: The Three Umbrellas of Tuesday](../art/accessible/92-the-three-umbrellas-of-tuesday.webp)

It rained on Tuesdays.

The cloakroom had three umbrellas on the pegs by the door. It had had them since the school opened, and the paint on the pegs had gone the same colour as the paint on the floor. On a wet Tuesday all three went out in about four minutes, and then three hundred children stood in the doorway and got wet in turn.

Rhys was six, and he was last.

“Third one,” said the cloakroom lady, and she put it into his hands.

* * *

It was not a worse umbrella than the other two. It was blue, and the wood was good, and there was a nick in the handle where somebody had once stood on it. It was the size of the other two. It was simply the third.

Rhys stood on the step and looked back at the cloakroom. Two girls had the first umbrella between them. A boy with a bag of golf clubs had the second. The third one was the one that got handed along, hand to hand, all the way to the door, and by the time his class came down the stairs it was leaning in the corner with a puddle under it and nobody holding it.

Nobody chose it. Nobody said no to it either. It just went round.

“You could have the first one,” said a girl behind him. “You can ask. Mrs Ottery lets us.”

Rhys shook his head. The third one was his.

* * *

Behind the bucket at the bottom of the cloakroom there was a hole in the wall, and the hot pipe ran past it, and a cold draught came in and spread along the floor to the door. Under the hole sat a grey mouse with her feet tucked up under her.

Rhys put the third umbrella down and held the open side of it over the hole.

The mouse looked at it. She came out from behind the bucket, went under, and sat down.

“That is the draught,” said Rhys.

The mouse was not arguing with him about the draught.

* * *

He picked her up and she rode inside his coat, and on Thursday she came too, and on Friday as well. On the fourth Tuesday the cloakroom lady told him that the mouse had a name. She was called Tuesday, after the third peg.

Nobody had ever named the other two, because nobody had ever needed to keep anything in them.

* * *

On the Thursday Rhys found a stick of chalk in his pocket. He had put it there in the yard.

He wrote at the top of the cloakroom door, in big letters: THIRD UMBRELLA, TUESDAY.

And under that he made a list, and it was the best thing he had ever made, because it filled up all by itself. He wrote down who the third umbrella was for today, one name under another, down the door, in chalk that was not quite white.

Megan. A boy called Peter who had lost his shoes in September. The two Halls. A girl who had come in from the car park. A man who came to fix the pipes and did not have a coat. Children Rhys knew by their coats and not their faces, and two that he had to ask about.

* * *

By Friday the list had gone from the top of the door to the hook rail, and the names were tight together at the bottom like seed in a packet.

“Nobody asked me to do it,” said Rhys, when the cloakroom lady asked him what all the writing was, “and on Friday the list is so long that the third umbrella is gone before the bell.”

The cloakroom lady looked at the door for a while. Then she wiped her hands down her skirt and went back to the pegs.

* * *

The list stayed up all term. Rhys washed it off on the last Friday and it came away like a dream, and the door underneath was plain brown wood, and nobody could believe it had ever been covered in names.

The caretaker started it again on the Monday. He got the chalk from the art room and he drew the line at the top himself, in a shaky hand, and he made a new column. He never said anything about it, and he was never once asked.
