# The Queen of the Second-Best Chair

![Cover art: The Queen of the Second-Best Chair](../art/accessible/84-the-queen-of-the-second-best-chair.webp)

The good chair in the hall went to the head teacher. Everyone knew that. It had a wide seat, a red cushion, and a bit of shine on the arms where everybody’s coat went.

The chair next to it went to nobody.

It was too short for the head teacher by a hand’s width, and too plain for a visitor. People moved it out of the way when they wanted past. It had a scuff on the front leg and a blue sticker on the back that said LIBRARY in letters that had gone pale.

Pip looked at it for a while on the Monday. Then she dragged it into the corner by the radiators and stood her pencil box on the windowsill, and that was the beginning of the court.

* * *

“There is a rule,” said Pip.

Twenty-two children came to hear the rule.

“You may hold court about anything that is not important.”

That was the whole of it. Pip wrote it out on a piece of brown paper and stuck it on the wall, and the first case that morning was a football match, and the second was whether the bins went out on a Friday, and the third was a lizard that three boys had found in the yard and could not find again, which made a great deal of noise.

* * *

By Thursday there was a queue along the wall.

They had one question a day, and it had to be a real question. On the Thursday it was a boy called Tam, who wanted to know whether his brother could come to court when he was little. Pip said anybody could come. He wrote his brother’s name on the brown paper in pencil and stuck it on the wall next to the rule, and then he waited by the door to see if anybody looked at it.

The court was the corner. Under the table was the folding chair from the storeroom, and the table was the plant table from the corridor, and the plant in it was a cactus everybody had given up on. At ten past ten a boy in a green jumper said the best biscuit in the school was the one in the tin behind the kitchen door, and Pip said it was not, and made him say why.

The head teacher came out of her office to find out what the noise was. Pip stood up on the chair to be heard over all of it.

“It is going well,” she said. “People like it here now. They come to the corner instead of going to the hall. That is because of the court.”

“Thank you,” said the head teacher.

“I have not finished.”

“I am sure you have not.” She looked at her watch. “We are all very pleased. Keep it down by the drum.”

Pip sat back down. She waited until the head teacher had gone, and she waited again until the Wednesday, to say it a second time.

* * *

Ada came on the Monday. She had not been at that school before. She was small, and she sat in the hall with her hands flat on the red cushion, because the office had put her there and she thought that was the rule.

Pip came over with the folding chair under her arm and set it down beside the good one.

“That one is not yours,” she said. “This one is mine. It is a queen’s chair.”

“It is better than that one,” said Ada.

“It is the one nobody wanted,” said Pip. “That is what makes it a throne.”

Ada moved across onto the folding chair with her knees up, because it sat lower and you could be brave in it. Pip sat on the arm of the plant table and put her boots on the seat of the good chair, which was not allowed.

“All right,” said Ada. “What is not important today?”

“Anything you like,” said Pip. “That is the whole rule. Start anywhere.”

* * *

By Friday the good chair in the hall was empty most of the day. The head teacher came out at lunchtime, and there was nobody waiting to be told off, and she went back in again. Nobody said the word court to her. Nobody had to.

Pip never found out about that part.

Ada told her on the Thursday, at the end of the corner, at the same moment the clock went.

“This is the best bit of the whole school,” she said.

“That is the paint,” said Pip. “The caretaker did it on Friday. Two coats, and he did the legs upside down so the brush marks would not show.” She hopped down off the table. “Court is over. Tomorrow, lizards.”

* * *

The folding chair was painted again, and Pip asked for it herself, and the caretaker got the school colour out and did it properly with the legs the right way up. The blue LIBRARY sticker came off in one piece, and Ada put it in her book like a bookmark.

Pip is still queen. The good chair is still mostly for visitors. It gets sat in twice a week, and it gets polished before anybody comes.
