# The Wolf Who Needed a Nap
![Cover art: The Wolf Who Needed a Nap](../art/accessible/17-the-wolf-who-needed-a-nap.webp)

Red found the wolf asleep beside a crooked pine tree.

His ears twitched. His paws were curled under his nose. A leaf stuck to one whisker. He did not look fierce. He looked like a coat forgotten on a fence.

Red had bread, a red string, and a much larger plan for meeting the dangerous wolf. She lifted the string so it flashed between the trees.

The wolf opened one eye. “Is that the brave girl in the red cloak?”

“It is,” said Red. “Are you the wolf who eats people?”

The wolf sighed. A warm puff of breath stirred the grass. “I wish you would ask a kinder question.”

He tried to stand. His back leg shook, and he sat again with a soft thump.

Red looked closer. His fur was rough and thin along his spine. Several burrs clung to his tail. The wolf smelled of dust, pine smoke, and the sharp tang of rain.

“You look tired,” Red said.

“Most people do not notice. They want the teeth.”

* * *

The wolf’s name was Rowan. He lived near a spring on the far side of the village.

“The sheep used to keep the grass short,” he said. “Then the farmer put up a silver-wire fence. It has little bells. The bells ring when I move. Every time I walk toward the spring, the sheep think I am hunting them.”

“What happened?”

“Nothing,” said Rowan. “That is the problem. The sheep never look up. The farmer never asks where I drink. I only walk, walk, walk.”

Red heard the sharp click of a bell in the distance. A dog began to bark.

Rowan tried to rise again. “I cannot nap. Every time I close my eyes, I hear the village children shouting, ‘Run, wolf!’ They play the old game. They chase a stick through the trees, but I cannot tell them that the sounds make my ears burn.”

Red sat beside him. “What would help?”

Rowan blinked. No one had offered the question before.

“Water without bells,” he said. “A path that does not cross the sheepfold. And one quiet hour when nobody pretends I am chasing them.”

Red thought of the shepherd, Mara’s father. He worried about every missing lamb. She also thought of the children. Their game had never hurt her, but it wore a tired wolf’s patience thin.

“I cannot make everyone unkind again,” she said. “I can ask them to try a kinder game.”

* * *

They found the spring first.

Red tied the red string around a low branch. Rowan showed her a place where the silver wire sat close to the ground. He could step over it, but a thirsty fox could not. The spring’s bank was bare, too, because animals had to step in one small place.

Red sketched a plan in the wet soil. They would move the fence back. The farmer would give the spring a wider path. Water would carry a wooden marker so everyone knew the boundary.

Then a thorn caught Red’s skirt.

She pulled. The cloth held tight. When she stepped, the red hem snagged in a buried loop of wire. It tightened around her ankle. Red tipped backward into the grass.

The wire hummed. Rowan sprang forward, though his tired legs shook. He caught the loop in his teeth and tore it away from her shoe.

Red gasped. “My ankle hurts.”

“Then we do not pretend it does not.”

He pulled the loose wire into a circle with his nose. Red could slip her foot free. The dog barked closer. Red had a choice. She could run and leave the wire for someone else. Or she could stay and finish the job.

She stayed.

The dog was Mara’s dog, Blue. Blue stopped when he saw Rowan crouched beside Red. He sniffed the torn wire.

“Blue, fetch the shepherd,” Red said. “Bring the little hammer.”

Blue vanished into the green dusk.

* * *

The shepherd came with a lantern. He kept one hand on his staff and one near the bell cord at his belt.

Rowan lowered his head. “You may be afraid,” he said. “I will not come closer.”

The shepherd looked at the bent fence, the torn wire, and Red’s scraped knee. “My sheep are not toys for stories.”

“No,” Rowan said. “Neither am I.”

Red stood between them. “Let him drink first. Then you can ask your questions. I will stay.”

At the spring, the wolf drank without bells ringing. Water moved over his nose. He drank slowly, as if afraid to waste even one cool mouthful.

The children came after supper. They stood at the safe side of the newly marked path, clutching their sticks. One girl lowered hers first.

“Can we play a game where you are tired?” she asked.

Rowan considered this. “You may play a game where the runner is a hare. Or the pilot. But do not need me to be dangerous.”

They chose the pilot. One child guided the hare with hand signals. Rowan slept against the crooked pine while the game went on without him. His ears twitched at every laugh, but his body stayed loose and still.

The farmer moved the silver wire. He left a wide path down to the spring. He still watched the meadow, but he watched it as a keeper, not a judge.

Red returned to Rowan at moonrise. He lifted his head.

“Did you need a whole day?” she asked.

He blinked sleepily. “I needed one place where no one decided what I was before I spoke.”

Red smiled. “Tomorrow I may tell the story again. But I will tell how you asked for a nap.”

Rowan yawned. “Tomorrow I may listen.”
