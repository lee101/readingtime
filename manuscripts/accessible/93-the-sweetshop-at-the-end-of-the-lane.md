# The Sweetshop at the End of the Lane

![Cover art: The Sweetshop at the End of the Lane](../art/accessible/93-the-sweetshop-at-the-end-of-the-lane.webp)

There was a shop at the end of the lane that was not there all the time.

It came in the week of the school play and shut the night the play was over. Ida found the locked door on the Monday and the bare windowsill, and the lane was only a lane again. She had been in it three times.

The shop was narrow and warm and it smelled of clean paper. There was a bell on the door and a woman behind the counter in a cardigan with a pencil through her hair.

The jars stood in a row along the windowsill, and every jar had a label.

ONE MINUTE OF NOT MINDING THE WIND. ONE MINUTE OF NOT WANTING TO BE SICK. ONE WHOLE JAR, SIXTY SECONDS OF COURAGE, NO MORE.

“What does the last one cost?” said Ida.

“A whole jar is a great deal,” said the woman. “I do not take money in this shop.”

The woman put down the key she was holding.

“Everybody who comes in here has got something they are not saying,” she said. “You give me that, and I give you the jar.”

* * *

There was a boy at the counter behind Ida, reading the labels all the way along the shelf.

“They are not sweets,” he said.

“No,” said the woman.

“Then what are they?”

“They are what they say on the tin.”

“My dad says we do not do sweets,” said Tam, “so I have not got any money anyway. But I have got a secret, if a secret counts.”

The woman said that everybody had one of those.

* * *

Ida bought the whole jar of courage.

The woman took the lid off with a cloth, and there was a noise like somebody drawing a long breath.

“Tell me something you are not saying.”

“I had the last apple in the kitchen in March,” said Ida, “and I put the skin in the bin so that nobody would find the core.”

The woman wrote it on the pad beside the till and pushed the jar across.

“Sixty seconds, no more,” said the woman, “and I am shut on the night of the play itself, because the ones who can already do it do not need me.”

* * *

Ida had never wanted anything so much. She got the lid off behind the boiler room, with Tam holding the jar.

Then it came.

Her heart went slow and heavy and easy, like a hand laid flat on a horse. Somebody had put a coat round her shoulders and the damp on her neck stopped being damp. The pipes knocked away behind her, and it was a nice sound, and it was hers.

She looked at the jar and counted on her fingers.

Thirty. Forty. Fifty.

At sixty she stopped counting, and it was still there.

* * *

“Go on, then,” said Tam.

Ida went along the corridor, up two steps and onto the little stage, and said her lines to forty empty chairs, and none of it frightened her, not one bit of it.

At sixty it went, all at once, like a lamp going off in a room.

“That was a minute and a half,” said Tam.

“You counted wrong.”

* * *

The night came, and the shop was shut.

Ida walked down the lane at half past six with a jar of nothing in her pocket. There was a card in the window that said SHOPED OUT. The jars were gone. The door was locked and the bell did not ring.

* * *

The play was The Hen and the Fox. Ida was the Fox. Tam was the Fox.

The lights went down. Somebody’s elbow caught Ida in the ribs at the second curtain, and out in the dark she heard Tam’s voice, clear as a bell, saying the words he had learned in a corridor from her.

He said them better than she had ever said them. He did not miss one. Not the bit about the ring under the sack. Not the bit where the fox stops in the middle. The hall made a noise at the end that Ida felt in her teeth.

* * *

She went out into the corridor and sat down with her back against the warm pipe.

That was the whole of it. She had not been frightened of the hall. She had been frightened of the words. They had been hers since the first day, and if she said them out there they would be hers, and hers, and hers, and nobody else in the school would ever have them.

And if she did not go out there, nobody would ever have known that she had them. It was a very small thing to be frightened of, and she had put a wall in front of it, and there were three hundred names on the other side of it.

Tam came out and found her and sat down on the other side of the pipe.

“You were the best thing in it,” said Ida.

“You taught me the words.”

“You knew them.”

“I had never heard them before you said them in the corridor,” said Tam. “There is a lot you do not hear, because you are in the room.”

* * *

The next year the shop was at the end of the lane again, and Ida went in on the Monday.

She had a pound in her hand, and the woman would not take it. The card said sixty seconds and no more. She took a secret off Ida, and Ida carried the jar back down the lane herself.

Tam was the main that year, and he did not need it, and he was never going to need it in that hall.

Ida put it on the shelf in the little room behind the stage where they kept the cups, and wrote his name on the label.
