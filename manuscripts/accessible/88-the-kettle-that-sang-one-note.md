# The Kettle That Sang One Note

![Cover art: The Kettle That Sang One Note](../art/accessible/88-the-kettle-that-sang-one-note.webp)

Rafi knew the kitchen by the sounds it made. The fridge went bump in the night. The tap dripped on a Sunday. And on the hob there was the kettle, which made one sound, the same one, all day long.

That was the trouble with it. Music wants more than one sound.

Grandma had had the kettle since before Rafi was born. She had never once said a word about the note. She filled the kettle, and she set it on the hob, and she poured the water out, and she did the whole thing again in the morning.

“Is that a song?” Rafi asked her.

“It is a note,” said Grandma. “A long one. Put your coat on.”

* * *

Rafi got a sheet of paper and a stub of pencil and sat down on the kitchen floor with his back against the warm cupboard.

He filled the kettle. He waited for the note. He wrote on the paper, he wrote and he listened and he wrote, and then he held the paper up and looked at it.

There was no way to write a sound down. He had known that before he started. He had not known it before he started.

So he tried other things instead. He tapped the table with a spoon. He clapped his hands. He ran a wet finger down the banister and it went a long way down in one note. He rang the little brass bell that still hung on Grandad’s old coat, and it rang beautifully, and he felt bad about it afterwards and had to decide not to.

Pudding the cat lay in the warm place by the fridge and did not lift her head for any of it.

“Every one of those was nice,” said Grandma. “But nice is not the kettle.”

* * *

The note came at the end. Rafi had heard it a thousand times without hearing it. It was a hiss, and then a lift, and then nothing at all, which meant you could take the milk in.

He did not want the end of it. The end of it was only the kettle saying now, now, now, hurry up.

So he stayed down on his knees by the hob and listened to the part he never listened to, the slow part in front.

The note did not stay on one note.

It came up. It was low, and then it was a little higher, and then it was higher still, and it went on coming up while the water was only just water, and it never once sat still. Rafi counted it on his fingers. Eight, and then a ninth, and the ninth was the loud one, and the ninth was the kettle saying the kettle is ready.

Pudding’s ears went straight up off her head.

* * *

Grandma’s piano stood in the front room under a cloth. It had not been played since Rafi could remember, and when he pulled the cloth off, the dust went up into the lamp light and made it look like weather.

He sat down. He put his fingers on the white keys and found a D and an E, and then went back for the low one.

He played the whole rise the way the kettle did it, all the way up and not only the end, and the nine notes went out into the front room, and they were the kettle, and the kettle was real.

He played it again. Then he played it wrong on purpose, which made him laugh, and then he played it right.

* * *

Grandma was standing in the doorway. Rafi did not know how long she had been there.

“You have been listening to the kettle,” she said.

“I have.”

“Not just the shouting at the end.”

“Not the shouting,” said Rafi. “It was doing the whole time. You just cannot hear it if you only wait for the boil.”

Grandma came in and sat down on the stool at the side, the way she sat when the wireless was on. She put her hands in her lap and looked at the keys.

“It is a tune,” she said. “I have known that for fifty years, and I have never once had anybody to play it for, because there is nobody else in this house who hears the thing it is doing. The kettle has been practising since before you were born, and I am glad you are home to hear the whole thing.”

Then she did not say anything else about it. She sat on the stool with her hands in her lap and let Rafi play it nine more times, and the kettle came out of the piano and went all the way up to the ceiling.

* * *

So now there is a cloth on the floor of the front room that nobody steps over.

Every Sunday after that Rafi takes the cloth off, and sits down, and starts with one finger. Then two. Then all ten. Grandma on the stool with the wireless off. The jar of buttons on the piano lid that Grandma has not needed since 1968.

Pudding lies in the warm place by the piano and waits for the ninth note.
