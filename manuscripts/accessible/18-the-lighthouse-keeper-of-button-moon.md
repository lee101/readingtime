# The Lighthouse Keeper of Button Moon

![Cover art: The Lighthouse Keeper of Button Moon](../art/accessible/18-the-lighthouse-keeper-of-button-moon.webp)

The Marigold came backward through the night.

Mara caught the ship in the lighthouse mirror just as its nose swung toward the moon. White fire flashed over the cargo deck. Its engines burned blue, but the ship moved away from them, sliding sideways as if the sky had become a steep glass floor.

“Wake Pip,” Mara said.

Pip was already at the stair, one shoe off and his red cap hanging by its chin string. He dropped to the lower rail and slid to the gallery. Below them, Button Moon shone in a ring of light from the great lighthouse.

The moon was round, flat, and only a hundred paces across. Four deep holes made it look like the silver button on a palace coat. Its pale surface was not bare rock. Moonkeepers had poured the outer shell in coarse concrete, layer by layer, while the sky was still new. At night, the thin mortar between the stones glowed.

The Marigold passed behind the first hole. Then it came out moving backward again.

Pip pulled the tide bell.

One note. The note sank into the concrete floor and spread out under Mara’s boots. The moon felt it too. All four holes began to hum, low and round, like empty jars struck with wet fingers.

“It has found us,” Pip said.

Before Mara could answer, the council horn opened above the gallery with a bark of brass noise.

“Button Moon, this is the High Watch. Hold your stations.”

Mara touched the horn rim. Static rolled and popped in the narrow tube.

“Your tide is hauling down the shipping lanes,” the voice said. “Four vessels are off their course. Three carry people. One carries seed for spring planting.”

Pip looked over the rail. Far below, silver lights slipped between the holes. One had gone as still as a fallen star.

“Release the docking arms,” the High Watch continued. “Clear the west rim. Emergency Launch Order Six is in force. At the third blue stroke, strike every ivory key. The well-charges will throw Button Moon clear of the tidal lane.”

Mara stared at the moon beneath her. She had scrubbed its concrete steps. She had polished the lighthouse mortar until it shone. She knew every hairline crack around the four holes.

“If the charges lift the moon,” she said, “what happens to the ships?”

A pause came through the horn.

“They must cut their lines.”

“And if they can’t?”

The brass voice hardened. “Save the moon, Keeper Mara. That is the order.”

The horn shut.

Six ivory launch keys waited in a black box beside the lamp. Mara’s fingers knew their smooth weight. She had oiled them for this exact morning. She lifted the lid.

Pip pulled at her sleeve. “All four vessels are coming close. None can hold its course.”

“Then they have no firm lines.”

“They have lines. We kept them in the low locker.”

Mara stared at him. The mooring lines were a faded blue rope braided with a strand of old light. They were meant for ships that came too close to the moon. Four coils lay in the locker, one for each well. Pip had helped wind them every seventh day since he was seven.

A horn note sounded inside Mara’s chest.

The High Watch thought of the moon as a stone to push. She could not think of it that way now. It was a button. Its holes were empty, but its edges were ready.

“Pip,” she said, “how would you sew a star into place?”

He blinked. Then he pointed down at the moon. “With a stitch.”

“Show me.”

They raced to the old repair cupboard. A storm had split its hinge, so Pip had to kick the door. Inside lay greasy tools, wooden moon maps, and a blue metal book. Mara had told him not to touch the book because its pages came loose in damp weather.

Pip opened it to a silver plate showing four ships and four holes.

“Button-stitch,” he read. “When a sky path catches on a moon, bind the loose craft to steady ground.”

Mara scanned the rest. Each ship sent two cords. The moonkeepers tied them in an over-under pattern through the capstans around its wells. The capstans were spool-shaped turning wheels. The turning moon would not drag the ships down. Its pull would become the road around which they traveled.

Pip traced the final line with one dusty finger. “The lamp feeds the cords with light. The holes share the strain. Four ships, four holes. Stitch the orbit closed.”

“It will pull the capstan teeth loose,” Mara said.

“They are iron.”

“And these walls are concrete.”

Pip’s hand curled into a fist. He had wanted to be a keeper since he was small enough to sleep in an empty coal scuttle. Until this moment, Mara had seen him mostly as the keeper who fetched clean cloths and sharpened pencils.

“I thought of it,” he said. “I have thought about the lines since the spring ship rubbed a scratch in the west rim.”

Mara looked at his tight face, then at the silver pattern.

The first blue glow crossed the gallery tiles.

“We may have one chance,” she said. “If the plan is wrong, I will not blame you. I asked for it.”

“Do it, then.” Pip pulled the book free. “Before I do.”

Mara seized the council horn.

“Button Moon Station, do you hear?”

Four signal lights blinked in answer. Even in the confusion, the crews had seen the moon.

“Do not cut your lines. Send down two mooring cords apiece. Mark the ship’s first line red. Use your engine only to hold still.”

A red spark swung from the Marigold. Two more appeared around the moon.

Mara rang the tide bell twice: *Begin.*

The crews began to lower their cords. The Marigold’s first line came down through the north hole, trailing a soft red flag. Pip caught it, fed it around the north capstan, and turned the iron wheel. The second line came through the south hole and crossed the first above the lighthouse.

Mara set the great lamp to moon-blue.

Its light poured down the tower. The white concrete blocks became cool gray, and the mortar flared into thin blue lines. The two cords lit along their whole length. One glowed as hard as a steel cable. The other trembled like a river seen from far away.

The third and fourth ships sent down their lines.

The first blue stroke swept the gallery.

Pip climbed onto the north capstan and added his weight to the wheel. It would not turn. A sound like a cracking stone ran through the floor.

Mara bent over the well. A fine fracture opened along the capstan’s concrete base.

“We are pulling too hard,” she said.

“No,” Pip said. He pointed to the silver plate. “We are pulling in the wrong order. The plate says the over cord takes the weight first. We did the under cord.”

The council horn banged open again.

“Keeper Mara, the second blue stroke approaches. Confirm launch.”

The moon shuddered. Beyond it, the Marigold tilted and dropped a little closer.

Mara looked at the ivory keys. One hard turn would fire the moon from the tangled lane. The ships might fall. The well-charges might tear this patched shell apart. The command had logic, but it ended with four vessels loose in the dark.

Pip had given her a plan, not a miracle. It could be tested, one thread at a time.

“Correct the stitch,” she said.

Pip dropped from the capstan. They loosened the first wheel, fed the north cord beneath the east one, and turned the handles in the order shown on the plate: north, east, south, west. Mara worked beside him. When her wheel jumped a tooth, she caught it with both hands. Pip shouted the count so she would know when to stop.

The crack stopped growing.

Moon-blue light climbed the cords. Across the dark sky, the four ships began to travel around the moon instead of sliding out of orbit.

The second blue stroke crossed the lens.

“Button Moon Station,” barked the council horn, “strike the launch keys.”

Mara pulled the black box onto the gallery floor. She turned it upside down. Six ivory keys rattled against the concrete. Then she set the empty box over them and sat on it.

“No,” she said into the horn.

Muffled alarm came through the brass. The High Watch ordered her to clear the rim. They said the moon was cracking. They said every second of delay made the ships less likely to survive.

Pip did not look away from the wheels. He counted with his lips while Mara pulled. Sweat rolled into his eyes, but he kept counting.

The outer concrete groaned. Long seams of light opened around the four holes. The moon turned beneath the lighthouse, and the buildings on its surface slid one careful step around the rim.

The Marigold came free first.

Its red line slackened as the ship swung onto a steady path. Then the green-lamped Mercy Bell stopped sliding. Next came the Apricot, heavy with seed tins. The tiny Cloud Kite came last, a pale paper shape trembling between two larger hulls.

When the last cord tightened, the four ships made a slow square around Button Moon.

The tide bells sounded one note, then stopped.

Mara released the capstan. Pip sat on the step beside her. Neither spoke until the moon stopped trembling.

At last, Mara opened the council horn.

“Four vessels are holding,” she said. “Button Moon is holding. Cancel Launch Order Six.”

“For the record,” said the distant voice, “you disobeyed.”

“For the record,” Mara replied, “the sky is still in one piece.”

* * *

The clouds thinned before dawn. By morning, the shipping lanes lay straight again. The Marigold crossed the northern light with all forty-two of its passengers. The Mercy Bell carried three doctors to a fever village. The Apricot protected its seed tins in a crate lined with dry moss.

Button Moon had four dark holes at its center. Around them now ran four blue seams where the mooring cords pressed against concrete. From a distance, they looked like stitches holding the moon to the night.

Pip kept the lamp silver after sunrise. Mara gave him the south capstan and the brass key to its weather cover. He was still Pip, and he still dropped things, but he no longer slept in the coal scuttle.

Mara kept the ivory launch keys under her chair. The High Watch had not called for them again.

That evening, the four ships came around at the same time, tracing the seams Pip had sewn. Their running lights joined the blue mortar of the lighthouse.

The old moon had been a bright button loose in the dark.

Now it was fastened there, with four small vessels and one true keeper holding it in place.
