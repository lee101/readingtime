# The Doorknob With a Name on It

![Cover art: The Doorknob With a Name on It](../art/accessible/83-the-doorknob-with-a-name-on-it.webp)

The door came on a lorry, which was the first thing that was wrong with it.

It was a brown door with nothing on it, and it leaned against the garage wall for a week while the floors dried. It was a second-hand door. That was all it was. Doors do not come with a history written on them.

Except that this one had a doorknob on it. A brass one. And on the flat back of it, where nobody would ever see it unless they unscrewed it, there was a name.

Bramble. Written in a shaky hand, with a wobble on the B.

* * *

Ivo was seven. He got the screwdriver out of the box and turned the knob until it came off in his hand, and then he put it in his pocket.

“Mum,” he said. “This house has a Bramble in it.”

“It has a knob in it,” said Mum. “Second-hand just means that.”

“Somebody’s name is on it.”

“Somebody’s house is on it,” said Mum. “That is what a house is.” And she carried the box of plates indoors, which is what she did when a thing was finished.

* * *

So Ivo went out to find Bramble.

He asked in the shop, where the woman wrapped his bread. He asked in the park, where the man feeds the ducks. He asked on the bus, and the driver said it was not his stop. He asked the girl with the hoop in the school yard, and she thought about it hard and said it sounded like a pirate, and then she said it was not a pirate she knew.

He said the name out loud everywhere he went, in case the wind took it. The wind had taken a sock from his mother’s machine once, and he had not been sad about that at all.

* * *

On Tuesday he was in the garden and he heard somebody in the next garden.

“You have got the wrong door,” said the girl next door. She was about his age. She had a hose and a jar of pond water. “Our knob went weeks ago.”

“I have not got your knob,” said Ivo.

She looked at the brown door in the garage.

“Yes, you have,” she said. “Look at the hole in ours.”

* * *

The hole in the next door was round and dark and a little rusty, and Ivo put his fist into it to see how big it was. It was exactly the size of his fist.

The family before them had bought this house cheap. That much was in the agent’s paper, in the box Mum threw away. They had bought it cheap because Bramble’s family had gone, and they had taken the house next door cheap as well, and one of them had had a door off, and had never once thought about the knob, and had put it on the next door they bought. On the back. Where it was hidden.

Ivo had carried that knob in his pocket for six days, through four streets and one bus.

* * *

“You took my name off my house,” said Bramble.

“It was in a garage.”

“It was on my door.” She set the jar down very hard. “My dad put that on. My mum said it was silly. My dad did it anyway.” She had gone the colour of the jar water. “Then we left, and your family bought our house, and now you have got my name in your pocket and you did not even ask.”

“I am asking,” said Ivo. He put it down on the flat wall, the wrong way up, like a coin. “I am sorry. I did not want to take a thing that was somebody’s.”

She did not touch it. She looked at it for a long moment, and then her face went and came back.

“Somebody kept my name on this the whole time,” she said.

* * *

“Here,” said Ivo. “It is yours. Take it home.”

“No.” She pushed it back towards him. “You keep it off.”

He turned it over in his hand. The name was on the back, and it was a great thing to be trusted with, and it did not fit a house that was not hers.

So he fetched the screwdriver, and he sat down on the step by her door, and he put the knob into the round hole, and he turned it until it was tight. The name finished up under the latch, where it had been for years.

She tried the door. It went into the house and shut, and turned, and the knob was warm from the sun.

There are two doors in those two gardens now. One is brown and new and has nothing on it at all. The other is green and old and has a brass knob, and on the flat back of that knob, where nobody can read it but the two of them, there is a name in a shaky hand.

That evening Ivo’s mother hung the hall lamp on the new door, because there had never been anywhere to hang it. Across the path, under the green door, there was a light on as well. Two lamps, one for each house, and neither one of them borrowed.
