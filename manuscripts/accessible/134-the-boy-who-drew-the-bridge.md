# The Boy Who Drew the Bridge

![Cover art: The Boy Who Drew the Bridge](../art/accessible/134-the-boy-who-drew-the-bridge.webp)

Dax was eleven years old and he drew in a school exercise book with a blue cover and his name in the corner where the label had been.

Anything he finished drawing stood up for sixty seconds. Not a picture of it. The thing itself, standing where he had drawn it, the size he had drawn it, for one minute, and then not there.

It cost him a page. The page went white, and whatever had been on it went out of his head as well, and he could not get it back by looking, because looking gave him white paper. He worked it out in the second term with the clock on his grandmother's dresser.

The Halversgate road crosses the Cut on a stone culvert, and the water comes down off the moor and took the road out in February, and it had done that three times in nine years. The men dug a way round through the allotments and everybody over seventy walked.

The committee that meets about it is the Cut Committee. It has six members and no money, and Bev has taken the minutes for eleven years in a hand so small that people put their glasses on.

Noll read the minutes out because he liked it. The committee had asked for a bridge four times in nine years. The reason given each time was money. The reason not given was that the road belonged to the council and the council to the county.

* * *

On the Saturday Dax took the book down to the Cut. The first page had the times tables on it, and he looked at them a while before he started.

He drew a footbridge across the Cut, grey planks with nine boards and no rail. Then he shut the book and it was there, a yard wide, standing four feet above the mud on nothing at all. Dax put his weight on it, and on the sixty-first second it was gone.

The times tables were gone too. He knew he had had them the way a person knows they had a coat.

* * *

Joel came down on the Tuesday with a barrow of sand and watched a bridge stand up out of a book. Bev came on the Thursday, took the book away, and on the Monday brought a man from the paper with her. None of the six men in that room believed a child.

* * *

The county sent a man in April because of a photograph. The county would supply the iron and the cement and the town would do the labour.

Bev read the small print off the sheet, put it flat on the table for all of them, and read it out. “If the work stops and begins again,” she said, “it is a new job, and the estimate begins again from nothing.” Joel said the far side was nine feet of mud and no barrow was going across it.

So Dax drew his bridge again, and Joel went over with the barrow and came back for the second one.

* * *

He drew it four times over four days, once each morning, because that was all he had.

The page he used that first April day had a poem he had to learn for the school reading, and he knew it the way a person knows the words to a song, and then he did not. The next had his street on it, drawn by himself in September. The last page he had been keeping, and his mother's telephone number was on it in her writing.

On the fourth morning Joel needed one more minute with his back against the rail holding the shutter open, and Dax turned to the last page of the book and shut it.

Afterwards he could not do arithmetic at all, and his mother wrote the numbers on cards and stuck them on the fridge. He could not find Shingle Row in his head when Bev asked where it was. And when he telephoned her from the box at the corner he could not say a single digit. “Read it to me again,” he said, and the woman at the other end read it twice.

* * *

The bridge was finished on the Friday. There are iron letters on the parapet that say CUT BRIDGE.

Bev's minute for that April went into the book. It said the Cut Committee wished it to be known that the bridge at Bridge Foot was put up in April by the labour of the town and the county paid for the materials. And it said that a boy of eleven had drawn it four times, once each morning, and that the pages he was charged for were never described to the meeting.
