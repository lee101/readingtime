# The Sundial at the End of the Yard

![Cover art: The Sundial at the End of the Yard](../art/accessible/41-the-sundial-at-the-end-of-the-yard.webp)

At the end of the school yard stood a stone circle with one stick set in its center. A tiny sun-spirit slept in the chalk marks, waking whenever sunlight touched the stone. The stick was crooked, and a bird had built a nest around its base.

On the morning the school clock stopped, the children gathered beside it.

“Can we fix it?” asked Ben.

Teacher Ms. Vale shook her head. “We can use sunlight for now.”

She drew a chalk ring around the stick. The children pushed it upright and tied a red thread from its top to a small nail, making a triangle so it would not lean.

At the first bell, the shadow stretched a long way across the circle. Ben wrote: 9:00, long shadow.

At the next observation, the shadow pointed in another direction and was shorter. Near the middle of the day, it was shortest. Afterward, it lengthened again.

“Earth turns each day,” Ms. Vale said. “That changes the Sun’s apparent position. The shadow indicates local solar time.”

* * *

The children made another sundial with a paper plate and a pencil through its center. They marked the shadow every ten minutes. The marks crowded together near the middle of the day and spread apart near morning and evening. The shadow was generally shortest toward solar noon, then grew longer.

“Does the school clock lie?” Ben asked.

Ms. Vale pointed to her wristwatch. “A civil clock keeps standardized time for people and places. A sundial follows the Sun’s apparent motion here. The two can differ because of longitude, time zones, and the way clocks are set.”
The groundskeeper brought a calendar. Their records showed that the Sun’s path and the noon shadow changed with the seasons. At this schoolyard, one season had a lower Sun and longer shadows; another had a higher Sun and shorter shadows. The pattern could differ in another part of the world.

Ben drew Earth with a tilted axis. “Earth also travels around the Sun. Tilt and orbit change the sunlight angle through the year.”

Lila traced a classroom window on a chalkboard. Sunlight entered the same window at different times of year, making the room bright in one season and dim in another.

* * *

At lunch, the children compared watches. Ben’s was fast, Lila’s was slow, and the wall clock had been set in the village hall years before. None matched the sundial’s shortest shadow exactly.

Ms. Vale drew a circle on the yard. “The sundial tells us about the Sun’s local day. A civil clock divides time into standardized hours so people far apart can arrange meetings.”

Rain hid the stick. A cloud covered the noon shadow. The children waited for clear sunlight instead of guessing. On the next clear day, Ben observed at 8:00, 10:00, 12:00, 14:00, and 16:00. Morning shadows were long and angled. Around solar noon, the shadow was shortest. Afternoon shadows grew longer and pointed across the circle.

“Like a giant hand,” Lila said.

“Earth’s daily rotation makes the Sun appear to move across the sky,” Ms. Vale said. “The gnomon stays still, but its shadow changes.”

Ben returned the next week. The sundial and his civil watch stayed close, but they were not identical.

* * *

A week later, the school clock’s chime fell silent. The children could not tell when to come in from the garden.

Ben pointed to the stick. “We can ring when the shadow reaches the solar-noon mark.”

“Not every day will the watch agree,” Ms. Vale said. “Use the sunlight, and let the other classes use their clocks.”

The children waited. The shadow moved inward as the Sun climbed. When the edge reached the chalk mark, Ben pulled the bell rope.

Clang!

Every child came in from the garden. Ms. Vale told them both kinds of time had a purpose.

Over the next weeks, the children recorded date, weather, and shadow length. In summer the noon shadow was short. In winter it stretched across much of the circle. The sundial did not break. Its daily pattern stayed visible whenever sunlight touched the stick.

A younger child asked why the shadow moved while the gnomon stood still.

“Earth turns each day,” Ben said. “From here, the Sun seems to travel. As the Sun changes position, the gnomon blocks light in a new place.”

* * *

At the end of the term, the school installed a battery clock. It agreed with civil time in the town. The sundial stayed at the end of the yard. Children still visited it with cards for date, weather, and shadow length.

One morning they found the noon shadow longer than the week before. Another day it was shorter. They compared their records with the tilted-Earth model and the calendar.

Ben’s old watch finally stopped. He left it on the teacher’s desk and used the school clock instead. When the bell rang, he walked outside to see whether the sundial agreed. It was late by a few minutes. Ben watched the shadow edge pass the chalk line.

“Local solar time,” he said.

The stick cast its narrow shadow across the stone. Above it, the Sun continued its apparent path. Around it, Earth turned, carrying the schoolyard through morning, noon, afternoon, and night. The powerless stick had kept a different kind of time.
