# The Girl Who Waited for the Dark

![Cover art: The Girl Who Waited for the Dark](../art/accessible/171-the-girl-who-waited-for-the-dark.webp)

Nell Farrow was eleven, and her job that evening was a stopwatch.

The school had walked up to the field above Exford in the dark, forty children and six adults, to count stars before the light came back. Nell had been given the job of timing the waiting.

You cannot count stars straight out of the day. It takes about twenty minutes for a pair of eyes to get used to a dark sky, and until they have, there is almost nothing there at all.

They were told the rule at the field gate. No torches past the gate. Torch in the box, box on the wall, box opens when the count is finished.

Nell's group was at the top of the field with their backs to the light from the car park below, looking north, where the sky was cleanest.

* * *

The stopwatch went round twice before anything happened at all.

Then a few stars came out, and more came out, and the children began talking about the shape above them.

At six minutes and forty seconds, Teo Vance put his hand up.

“I left my gloves in the car.”

Miss Armitage said they had five minutes left in the watch and he should sit still.

“The path’s black,” said Teo. “Two minutes.”

* * *

She said yes.

That is the whole of it. She had no reason to say no about a boy looking for a pair of gloves on a path he had walked up an hour before.

Teo’s torch was small and white and very bright, and he shone it down the track while he walked, sweeping the grass from side to side.

Forty children had their eyes on it.

Somebody said, “Ouch,” in the dark, and then nobody said anything, because by then there was nothing to look at. Every star that had been there had gone, and the sky where the stars had been was only grey.

Nell watched the little dial. Her eyes were wide open and stinging and completely useless, and she had to stop looking at the sky altogether.

* * *

Teo came back with the gloves and put them on his knees and said sorry.

Miss Armitage wrote something in the box on the sheet for interruptions, and then she told them what it meant.

It meant the watch was not a watch any more. It meant the whole square was blank on the night, and there was one more try left before the bus came.

Nell set the stopwatch again without being asked. Her hand was not steady. Her eyes had to start the whole twenty minutes from nothing.

* * *

The second wait was longer than the first, because nobody would speak.

At about twenty-two minutes Nell said, “Now.”

Then they counted.

The faint ones came out first, one at a time, and then all at once, and the sky stopped being a black lid and became a field with things in it. The pale band of the Milky Way came up over the hedge, wide enough to put your hand across.

Seven stars sit in the little patch above the shoulder of Taurus that people call the Pleiades. Six of them come easily. The seventh one does not.

Nell found it three times and lost it three times. When she looked straight at it, it went. When she looked just to the side of it, where the light fell on the edge of her eye instead of the middle, it brightened and sat there.

That is what the rod cells do, the ones that do the seeing at night. They are thickest away from the middle of the eye, so the trick is not to stare.

The card from the dark sky group said that on the best night of the year, seven of the Pleiades show from Exmoor. Not nine. Not twelve. Seven.

* * *

They took the count to the gate, and the sheet had a line for interruptions and a line for the sky.

Miss Armitage wrote the torch in the first line, with the time on it, and then she wrote the word VOID after it, because that is what it was.

Six went in the box for the Pleiades.

Nell held the pen before she handed it over and thought about the seventh star, and about the three seconds in the middle of the wait when the bus lights swung over the hedge from the lane and took the top of the sky away.

Six is not wrong on the sheet, because six is what she could stand behind.

Six is also not right, and there is nobody on that field, and there never will be, who can tell her by how much.
