# The Name on the Plaque

![Cover art: The Name on the Plaque](../art/accessible/102-the-name-on-the-plaque.webp)

Rain was coming sideways off the water at Tidetown Landing, the way it does in Brinehold about one day in five, and Neel Ferrin was scrubbing the First Lamp with her hood up and her sleeves down over her hands.

She was eleven. She trimmed every wick on the coast shorter and straighter than anybody else alive, and she carried a lamp nowhere.

“Listen —” she said to the post, “the water is not going to stop for anybody.”

Then, because that had been a bit honest, she changed it.

“The water never stops for anybody.”

* * *

The First Lamp stood at the harbour end of the Ridge Line, the nearest one to the town, the one that held the whole chain of the other eight. Its post was iron, its lantern was brass, and its plaque had been polished with a chamois by a hundred lamplighters since the Winter of Lost Boats.

Neel had it in her hand that morning, and half of it was beautiful.

The left half shone like a looking-glass. The right half was a flat dead grey, and the letters that should be cut into it were only ghosts of letters, little round pits where the brass had gone soft.

* * *

She tried the chamois. Then the wire brush, which is for lamp posts and not for plaques and which she was not supposed to have.

Nothing. The grey was not dirt. Dirt comes off. This was the brass itself, eaten away, thin as the shell of a hake’s egg.

So she sat on the edge of the weigh shed in the rain and worked out who was supposed to do this job.

Not her, and not anybody’s. Letters were cut with the Chief’s brass punches, and a wick-trimmer did not get that far. Somebody had meant to do this, had got as far as the right half, and had gone away, and now the whole coast had a First Lamp with half a name on it and nobody who owed it a finished job.

* * *

The box of brass letter punches lived on the second shelf of the Lamp House, and Neel Ferrin had watched a man cut a name in brass four times in her life and had counted every blow.

She had the box in her slicker before she had finished deciding.

* * *

This is the whole of what she did, and she did it quickly.

She laid the plate on her knee, set the I on the shallowest pit, held the punch square with the heel of her hand, and hit it once with the small hammer she kept for wick work. Eleven times.

The punch rang. The plate got warm under her knee, and one by one the letters of a name stood up out of the grey again.

She got all of them before the rain had got through her hood. Then she read the plate, because she was eleven and she could not help it.

She said the name out loud, once, quietly, to herself, over the sound of the rain.

* * *

The bell on Black Spit stopped.

Neel did not know what that was, but the man on the weigh shed roof did, because his hammer went still.

Every so often, in weather like this, the bell rang three slow notes by itself, and the whole bay went quiet enough to hear the eiderdown on the water. It stopped in the middle of the second note.

* * *

Then the water went black.

Not dark. The water at Tidetown Landing had been a pale jade that morning with the cloud on it, and now it lay in the lanes a hard flat black with the ripples standing on it like grease.

The fog came off the harbour in one breath, and Neel saw further than she had ever seen. She saw the Outer Stacks lying out there like the backs of sleeping animals, and she saw the whole of the bay at once, and it was the most frightening thing that had ever happened to her.

Her hood was up. It was always up, and she had never asked who told her to keep it up.

“Listen —” she said, to nobody, “I am not frightened.”

And then, because that was the truth and she was eleven and there was nobody to hear it, she let it stand.

* * *

The eiderdown came up off the water next, in pale slow patches, thicker than feathers should be, the way it comes when a bay has lost its heat. The ice in the crates on the landing began to sweat, though it was cold and raining.

And the First Lamp above her head, warm and yellow-brown and pleasant all morning like any lamp burning anything at all, gave a small cough and went on burning exactly the same.

* * *

Pim Sabiston came up the last of the steps at four hundred steps an hour, holding the Chief’s key in both hands because it was brass and it was heavy.

“The First Lamp is making a noise like a person who has eaten fish, and Neel is standing under it not moving. Please be quick about it, the sooner, the better.”

“Go up and get the Chief. He will be the one who is not asleep.”

* * *

Hadren Vole came down in a coat over his nightshirt, with the rain going off him in steam, and there was lamp-black in the crease of his right thumb that had been there so long it had become part of him.

He did not ask what had happened. He went straight to the plaque.

“Where is your box.”

“In my slicker.”

“Give it to me.”

* * *

He held the plate in one hand. It was warm, and the letters stood up bright and square, and the new brass was already going dull at the edges, the way fresh-cut metal does in salt air.

“The I is good,” he said. “Your hand is good. Eleven blows and not one of them crooked.”

“Thank you.”

“Do not thank me yet.” He turned the plate over. “Neel. What is the name on that plaque.”

She knew it. That was the worst part. She had read it four times on her knee.

She opened her mouth to say it, and stopped, because something in the back of her head had gone very cold and very clear, and she had never asked where that place was.

“I cannot say it,” she said.

“Because you are not the lamplighter for this lamp. The name on a plaque is a signature. A signature is not a name. A name on a plate is a shape in brass, and brass does not pay.”

* * *

“Then why did it go out?”

“It did not go out. It never went in.” He turned the plate back over. “You cut a good name, correctly cut, and it is worth exactly nothing, because the name is only half of it. The other half is an hour of somebody’s own life, given freely, with that name in their mind.”

“Look at it. Warm. Burning away beautifully. But the letters are not filling. Do you know what a lamp is when the letters fill?”

“No.”

“A lamp is lit. What you have up there is a fire in a glass box, and the sea counts it as zero.”

* * *

“And the bell stopped,” said Neel.

“Because the bay is giving something up. You spoke a name with somebody else’s mouth on it, and out there something that was not asked to wake has begun to lift, and the lifting takes the heat out of the bay. The sea is not angry. It is doing arithmetic.”

“Is somebody hurt?”

He did not answer in words. He took the plaque off the lantern body and laid it flat in his left palm.

“Watch my hand,” he said.

* * *

He was a long time.

Neel had trimmed a hundred and forty wicks in her life and had never seen a lamp lit by a person. Now she watched a man of sixty-one take an hour out of his own life and put it into a piece of brass.

He did not make a sound. His hand did not shake. The rain went on going off his coat.

What she noticed, and did not understand until much later, was his face. By the time he had finished, the skin at his temples had gone a soft loose grey, and the white stubble that had been the pale green of sea foam was the colour of wet ash.

He had not got ten years older.

He had got about five, in a minute, and he kept them.

* * *

Then the plaque filled.

She saw the moment it happened. The cut letters took the light and went bright and gold from the inside out, and the flame overhead went from brown to amber.

Out in the bay, three notes. The bell on Black Spit finishing the tune it had stopped in the middle of.

The eiderdown lay down flat, and the black went out of the lanes, and the jade came back up under the cloud.

Hadren Vole handed the plaque back.

“Put it on. Two screws, bottom one first, so it hangs true. You do it like somebody who will be allowed to do it again.”

* * *

Then she had to hand the name back.

The Harbour Council keeps its tide board on the door of its own house, and on it is a line of chalked strokes recording what the coast has spent this month, which is the Blue Tally. The board is nailed by every door in Brinehold too, so anybody can see whether the strokes are honest.

Neel Ferrin stood in front of them with the box of brass letter punches in her hands. She was eleven, there were nineteen people on the step, and the rain was still coming sideways off the water.

* * *

Three times she started a sentence and finished a different one.

“Listen —” she said, and her voice went up, and she brought it back down. “I only did a small thing.”

“Listen — I meant to finish a job that was not mine, and I have got it wrong, and it will not happen again.”

That one was not true either, and everybody could tell, and it made it worse.

So she said the third thing, flat and plain, in front of all of them.

“Listen. My name is Neel Ferrin and I cut the First Lamp’s name this morning with the Chief’s punches, because I thought somebody had left it unfinished, and I said the name out loud when I had finished, and the bell stopped, and it was me.”

* * *

Nobody shouted at her. She had braced for it the whole way down the steps, and it did not arrive.

The chair asked whether she had thought about what she was doing before she did it, and Neel said no, and then said she had thought about it afterwards for a very long time.

Then the chair gave her the chalk. Not as a punishment. As a job.

“For the month,” said the chair. “You will do it in the open.”

“Yes,” said Neel.

* * *

The first thing she chalked that evening was a stroke for the day.

The second was a stroke for the hour the Chief Lamplighter had put into the plaque, because it had been an hour, and it had gone into the coast, and it counted.

After that she sat on the step and looked out at the black water and the eight small orange flames going up along the cliff.

The name on the plate was bright and gold and full of light.

But the glass of the lantern had gone cloudy.

Not scratched. Not dirty. Clouded, all of it, from the inside, with a soft grey bloom on the inside of the chimney, the way a jar goes cloudy when something sweet has been left in it too long.

Neel Ferrin looked at it for a long time.

Then she went home, and she did not take her hood off, and the next morning she was on the landing at six with the chalk in her pocket and the wire brush in her hand, because that was the whole of her trade, and finishing the job was the whole of everything.
