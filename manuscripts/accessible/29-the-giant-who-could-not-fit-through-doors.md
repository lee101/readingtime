# The Giant Who Could Not Fit Through Doors

![Cover art: The Giant Who Could Not Fit Through Doors](../art/accessible/29-the-giant-who-could-not-fit-through-doors.webp)

The storm dropped a chimney tile on Mr. Alder’s blue hat.

Bree saw it happen from the school steps. The tile bounced off the brim, spun twice in the muddy air, and landed in the town hall flower tub. Mr. Alder stared up at the broken roof.

“That will be the last tile to move itself,” he said.

It was not.

A crack raced across the ceiling. The tall clock beside the hall doors gave a metallic squeal. Everyone ran into the square while the storm wind pushed hard against the shutters. Then a beam shifted above the council chamber, dropping dust and splinters beside the front steps.

“Stay back!” cried Mayor Senn.

A broad figure rose from behind the hall. It was taller than the clock tower, though not by much. Mud coated its green coat from shoulders to boots. A red neck scarf streamed in the wind. The giant rubbed his soot-black nose with one finger.

Bree’s best friend, Niko, whispered, “That is Fen.”

Fen looked at the crooked beam. “It is trying to fall the wrong way.”

Mayor Senn folded her arms. “Then you can lift it, can’t you?”

Everyone cheered, but Fen did not move. He stared at the hall doors.

“Will you fetch the ladder?” Mayor Senn asked.

“I can fetch it,” Fen said. “I cannot come through.”

He pointed to the ordinary doors. They were wide enough for three adults standing shoulder to shoulder. Fen’s shoulders needed a stall’s width. Fen’s head nearly brushed the stone arch above them. He took one careful step toward the entrance, then stopped.

“I bend my knees, and my head touches the arch,” he said. “I lower my shoulders, and the door catches my scarf.”

His ears turned pink. Bree had seen giants laugh over breakfast tables taller than houses. She had never seen one try to hide.

“The hall is open,” said Mayor Senn. “Everyone can enter.”

“Not Fen,” said Bree.

A council clerk lowered his eyes. The baker folded his arms tight. Fen began to turn away.

“Where will you go?” Bree called.

“To the quarry until the rain stops.”

Bree looked at the beam. One end rested on the council chamber wall. The other leaned across the front steps. If it fell, it would crush the doors and block the cellar arch beneath them. Most of the town’s winter food was stored down there.

* * *

The children drew a plan on the wet steps.

The beam could be lifted, Mayor Senn said, but Fen needed room above his head and enough distance to pull it free. The hall roof had cracked, but the beam was sound. The front doors were useless to him.

Bree pointed at a broad chalk square beside the doors. “What if we make a new entrance here?”

“Not if it is wide enough for Fen and everyone else.”

Niko added, “We can keep the wall and take down the middle bar. Then hang the two doors from the sides.”

“If they open sideways, his scarf is safe,” said Fen.

“If they open sideways, everything is too wide for carts,” said the baker.

“Then make two entrances,” Niko said. “The old doors stay. Fen uses a wide opening beside them.”

A small hand went up. Tova, the hall’s six-year-old page, wore a mustard dress and had a smudge of ink across one cheek.

“Windows are holes in walls,” she said. “We can take out the left-hand ground-floor window and the wall below it. Then clear a broad stone apron so his feet have somewhere to land.”

Everyone looked at the window. It was the town’s tall meeting bell window. On important days, the bell rang from the empty room behind it.

“The bell can be moved,” the mayor said slowly. “Not lost.”

Bree grinned. “We can hang the meeting bell in the middle of the hall. Bigger.”

* * *

The work began before the rain returned.

Mr. Alder and the bricklayer removed the window panes and the low wall beneath them while the children fetched rope and tools.

Bree stood beside Fen while the mayor tied a safety rope around his waist.

At the count of three, Fen lifted the beam. It rose with a deep scrape. Bree’s feet slipped in the mud. Niko caught her arm. The beam stopped above the new opening.

Then a rotten window beam cracked and fell between the entrances.

Everyone scrambled back. Fen caught it on one bent knee, holding it above their heads, but he could not lower it without bringing his weight onto the cracked paving. A crack opened in the stone.

Bree looked at the proclamation bell frame above the square. Two tall iron posts held the bell between them. Below them stood a wide wooden platform used for proclamations.

“The proclamation bell can help,” she said. “We move its frame and platform to the gap and hold the beam there.”

“No pulling on the beam,” said Mayor Senn. “Children, step behind the old doors. Grown-ups, take hold of the guide ropes. On my signal, we will move the proclamation frame and platform across the wet stones.”

The baker and clerk pulled. The iron frame scraped and jumped. Fen shifted his great knee and pushed the beam higher. Mayor Senn and two townspeople pulled the safety rope tight. Mr. Alder shoved from behind them, his hat safely tucked into his coat.

From behind the old doors, Bree took the proclamation bell rope. When the frame reached the gap, she gave it one firm ring.

The note burst across the square.

Fen pressed his shoulder against the rotten window beam. With a deep grunt, he lifted and stepped it onto the platform. The adults held the guide ropes. The beam rolled safely away from the entrance.

Then everyone cheered.

Fen lowered the rotten beam onto the waiting cart. He did not enter. He did not have to.

* * *

By evening, the market notice board stood beneath a new roof, and the old doors opened wide for carts. Beside them was the new entrance: a deep opening with sliding panels, a broad stone apron, and enough clear height for a giant to stand upright.

The meeting bell hung in the middle of the hall. Its great note made the windows tremble, but everyone could see it.

Mayor Senn lifted a small brass key. It was the size of her palm.

“This opens the side entrance,” she said, offering it to Fen. “It will always be wide.”

Fen carefully took the key between two fingers. “I will need to be more careful with the lock.”

Bree looked at the cut stones, the clean hinges, and the bell swaying above them.

“No,” she said. “The town will be more careful with the door.”

Outside, the last tile shifted back into place.
