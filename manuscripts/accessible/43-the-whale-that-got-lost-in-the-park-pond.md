# The Whale That Got Lost in the Park Pond

![Cover art: The Whale That Got Lost in the Park Pond](../art/accessible/43-the-whale-that-got-lost-in-the-park-pond.webp)

The pond in Elm Street Park usually held only ducks, dragonflies, and the reflections of clouds. On the afternoon the sky whale arrived, it held a whale.

Mina saw the first ripple while she was tying her bright yellow boots. The water curved upward in a shining hoop, and a small blue back slid beneath the surface. Then the pond burst into spray.

A whale no longer than a bathtub nosed through the reeds. It had a fin like a crescent moon, a tail striped in silver, and eyes the color of sunset. Its breath smelled of rain and cinnamon.

“Are you lost?” Mina whispered.

The whale blinked. A low hum trembled through the park bench, the swing chains, and the little silver bell on the duck house.

Jun came running from the path. He was nine, wore a green cap turned backward, and carried a box of chalk in one hand. “Mina, did the pond grow?”

“No. I think it invited a whale.”

Before they could decide what to do, the park’s oldest weatherkeeper came through the gate. Her name was Mrs. Iona Finch. She had retired from keeping the neighborhood weather, but she still carried a brass compass in one hand and a small weather book in the other. Her gray coat had brass buttons polished by many seasons.

Mrs. Finch stopped so quickly that her hat tilted over one eye. She peered at the whale, then at the pond.

“The sky has sent us a visitor,” she said. “A very small one.”

The whale splashed. Water touched Mina’s cheeks. She laughed, but Mrs. Finch’s face was serious.


Mrs. Finch opened her weather book to a page filled with leaf shapes. “Sky whales follow a path made from falling leaves. The leaves float on a pond until starlight touches them. A whole constellation of leaves guides the whale home.”

She pointed. A loose ring of leaves circled the pond. Some were gold, some green, and some were brown. But four spaces between them were empty.

“The path is broken,” Mrs. Finch said.

The whale made a worried sound. Its small fins beat the water, sending ripples toward the reeds. Above the park, the blue sky seemed to tremble at the edges.

Mina looked toward the trees. “We can find more leaves.”

Jun held up his chalk. “I can mark where they land.”

Mrs. Finch nodded. “Bring leaves with strong stems. The constellation must be steady, and the wind is changing.”

* * *

Mina and Jun gathered strong leaves near the swings, picnic tables, and hedge. Jun marked the piles with chalk, then stopped when Mina laughed.

“Stars don’t need boxes,” she said.

“Neither do leaves,” he replied, coloring one of the chalk boxes green.

They carried armfuls back to the pond. The whale watched them, blowing gentle bubbles that smelled of rain. Each time a leaf touched the water, it glowed with a thin silver line.

Mrs. Finch showed them how to fit the leaves into the empty spaces. “A star rests on the vein. A leaf’s stem must point toward the sky.”

Mina placed a maple leaf into the first gap. Its silver line curved toward the evening star. Jun placed an oak leaf in the second. The path brightened, but a sudden breeze lifted it away. The leaf skittered over the water and lodged beneath the little wooden bridge.

Jun reached after it, but the bridge supports were too narrow.

“I can get it,” Mina said.

She lay on her stomach on the dock and pushed one yellow boot beneath the bridge. Her fingertips found the leaf’s slick stem. She pulled it free, but the current swept it toward the reeds.

Mrs. Finch caught it with the end of her scarf. The scarf was old and red, its fringe beaded with rain. She tied the leaf to a length of blue kite string and handed the string to Jun.

They stretched the string across the empty space. Mina guided the leaf while Jun held the loop steady. For one moment it turned in the water, refusing to join the constellation.

Then the whale sang.


Three spaces remained. The sky had turned the color of a plum. Inez Finch—the retired weatherkeeper—squinted at the first pale star.

“Quickly,” she said. “The path must be complete before that star brightens.”

* * *

The children ran to the hill, where the wind had shaken leaves from an old oak. Mina shook the lowest branches while Jun held his box beneath them. They gathered every strong leaf they could carry. At the same time, Mrs. Finch pointed out which stems were straight enough to hold a star.

By the time they returned, the first star shone above the pond. The whale’s song had become a series of soft clicks, like pebbles knocking together underwater. Its eyes followed the children.


Only one space was left.

The missing leaf lay high in an oak tree. Its stem glowed faintly, but the branch was too high to climb. Jun handed Mrs. Finch the kite. “Can we use the wind?”

She nodded. “A kite can carry a leaf, but the wind must be invited.”

Mina ran across the grass while Jun held the reel. The kite climbed, dipped, and climbed again.

Mrs. Finch watched the grass. “Now, Mina. Let out one breath.”

Mina opened her hands. The kite surged upward. The glowing leaf slid free from the branch and hooked itself onto the tail.

“Jun, pull gently!”

The kite came down in a long blue curve. Its leaf landed in the last empty space, exactly where the silver line pointed. The constellation flashed from edge to edge.

The pond lifted.

Water rose in a smooth wall around the whale, carrying it upward without spilling a drop. The little whale shook droplets from its fins, then rose higher and higher. Its silver tail touched the evening star, and the star opened into a long glimmering path through the blue.

The whale paused above the park. It looked down at Mina, Jun, and Mrs. Finch, then blew one last cinnamon-scented breath. The leaves spun in a perfect ring, sparkling.

Up it went, joining the glimmering path until it became a bright point among the constellations.

The pond settled. A single blue scale rested on the dock, no bigger than a dinner spoon. Mina picked it up. It was warm from the whale’s sky.

Mrs. Finch closed her weather book. “Well done. The leaves held.”

Jun tucked the blue kite string around his wrist. “Will the whale come back?”

“Perhaps not this way,” she said. “But it will know the pond is a safe place.”

Mina placed the scale in the middle of their three hands. Above them, the first stars appeared. The leaves kept their faint silver lines for a little while, then faded as ordinary leaves should.

Before they left, Mina looked back. The whale was gone, but the pond shone with a clear reflection of the evening sky. Jun raised his hand in a cheerful wave. Mrs. Finch lifted her brass compass, and somewhere far above, a whale answered with one clear note.
