# The Paper Boat That Learned to Swim

![Cover art: The Paper Boat That Learned to Swim](../art/accessible/85-the-paper-boat-that-learned-to-swim.webp)

Every spring the stream above the village came up out of its banks and put brown water in the lane. The children put their paper boats in it anyway. Every year they did.

Wren was eight, and she folded better than anybody else in the village. She had made eleven boats that winter, and the eleventh was on her mother’s kitchen sill with Wren’s name on the side of it in pencil.

“That one will not last,” said her brother Ollo.

“It is not meant to last,” said Wren. “It is meant to be watched.”

* * *

In the flood the boats came apart. Wren stood on the bridge and watched a whole paper fleet go past her, and every boat of it was torn to bits before it reached the weir. Some went under and came up flat. Some caught on the gate and stayed there in pieces.

“There,” said Wren. “The stream does not like them.”

“You have folded them wrong,” said Ollo.

“I have not,” said Wren. “I have watched it do it. The problem is the stream.”

That night she wrote it in the back of her book. The problem is the stream. Ollo read it over her shoulder, shut the book, and left the room.

* * *

On the Monday she folded a boat with no corners in it. She made the bottom round, like a bowl, and she sloped the sides, so there was no sharp place for the water to get hold of.

She set it in at the top of the lane.

“Will that one hold?” said Gil, who was nine and liked watching things fail.

“Watch,” said Wren.

The boat went down the lane and past the bridge. It came round the stone by the weir, and it did not tear. It went soft and slow in the mill pool and lay down flat on its side, like a hat in a doorway.

“It lasted,” said Gil.

“It got past the weir,” said Wren. “That is the whole of it for now.”

* * *

The stream did not like corners. Wren proved that with a square boat and a boat with a sharp nose, and both of them came apart before the bridge. It liked round things. It turned them well.

It would not carry heavy things. She put a big flat stone in one boat, and the boat sat down and turned over and went under with the stone still in it. She put a small stone in the next one, and the boat went on down the lane as if nothing had happened.

“A stone,” said Gil. “Why put a stone in a paper boat?”

“Because paper wants to be empty,” said Wren. “And something has to sit down in it, or it only spins.”

* * *

Then there was the speed of it.

Wren set two boats in together, and the second one caught the first one. It came down the lane like a crow, and it struck the stone by the weir, and it was pieces by the time it got there.

“That was quick,” said Gil.

“That was very quick,” said Wren. “It has not got anywhere to be. Put one in front of it and it will go again.”

She sat on the wall and looked at the water for a while.

“The stream is not the enemy and never was,” she said. “It is the fastest road in the village and nobody had asked it for help.”

* * *

On the morning the water came, Wren carried sixty boats out on a board.

She had folded them in the week before, sixty round-bottomed boats, and she had a heap of small stones in her pocket from the wall along the lane. One small stone went into each boat. She set them down the lane with a space between them, the way a hen walks in a yard.

Ollo carried the second board. Gil carried the third.

“They will all go at once,” said Gil.

“No,” said Wren. “They go one after another, and every one of them goes after the boat in front of it.”

So she let the first one go, and then the second, and then the third. And the stream did what it had wanted to do all its life. It ran. It ran so hard it climbed the sides of the lane and put cold water over Wren’s knees.

“It is showing off,” said Ollo.

The front boats were tearing at the weir, and the ones behind them had already gone round it.

* * *

Old Marda kept the mill, and she had a hook on a long pole. On the Monday she lifted a wet paper boat out of the pool and held it up to the light.

“Nobody has ever brought me one of these,” she said.

“Sixty,” said Wren.

Old Marda looked at the boat, and then she looked at Wren.

“Two hundred years,” she said, “and this village has been blaming the stream.”

* * *

Wren never told the village that she had worked out the rules. She folded boats, and she showed other children how to fold them, and by the next spring there were six of them on the wall doing it wrong and arguing about it.

Every spring the village holds a launching day, and the boats always make it to the mill.
