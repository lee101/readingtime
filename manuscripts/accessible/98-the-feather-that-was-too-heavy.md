# The Feather That Was Too Heavy

![Cover art: The Feather That Was Too Heavy](../art/accessible/98-the-feather-that-was-too-heavy.webp)

Merrick was fourteen, the tallest boy in the parish by a head. He could take a sack of flour up to the mill loft on one arm, and he could not go up the ladder without counting rungs.

His aunt kept a feather. It was longer than her arm, and grey-green, and it did not look like it came off a bird.

“A carter from over the water put his hand on it and went up off my yard like a scudding swan,” she said. “He landed in a field nine miles off and walked home. A girl of nineteen went over the church and landed on the far side of the wood. The feather has never failed.”

It hung on a nail by the mill door, on a string through the quill, so anybody could try it.

“You do not have to use it,” she said. “It hangs there.”

“I know,” said Merrick.

* * *

The smith’s boy said it first, and then it got about the village.

“That is Merrick’s aunt’s feather, and Merrick will not touch it.”

“I do touch it,” said Merrick. “I dust it.”

“You have never once been up.”

“I have been up,” said Merrick. “Once.”

“A long time ago,” said the smith’s boy, and went off down the lane. Merrick stood by the mill door with his hand on the feather, and it was warm.

He was not afraid of the height. Nobody could get that through. He had flown over the parish, the river and the wood, and it was easy, and the wind held him under his arms like a hand.

It was the coming down that he could not do.

When you came down with that feather, you came down by yourself. Nobody else in the valley had one. They came to the fair along the road, in a cart or behind a dog, and in through the gate together. He came in over the hedge, alone, out of the sky, with nowhere to walk.

* * *

On the day of the autumn fair he made a paper aeroplane.

He folded it six times and pressed the creases with the back of a spoon. Then he cut a notch in the quill and pushed the feather into the nose, so the plane would fly straight.

He threw it off the cart ramp at the mill, with his back to the wall, because that was as high as he went.

The wind took it and would not let it down. It went over the mill, the river and the wood, and lay in a field three miles off like a nail.

He walked to fetch it. Two hours each way, and when he reached the fair he was alone in the road.

“Two hours,” said the man at the gate. “You could have flown it down.”

“I could,” said Merrick.

Everybody else was already inside.

* * *

He left the feather on the wall at the fair and stood by the horse trough, as he had for three years.

“Three years now,” said his aunt, coming up behind him. “And the feather on the wall again.”

“You could be over that tower and down in the water meadow before the fiddle starts.”

“I could.”

“You will not.”

“No,” said Merrick.

“You do not have to tell me,” she said. He was grateful, and sorry about it at once.

* * *

At the end of the summer she gave him a pencil.

“A good one,” she said. “I got it at the fair from a man who sells nothing but pencils. Try that.”

“Thank you,” said Merrick.

“Write something.”

He wrote for a while. The aunt came and stood behind his chair.

“The men at the mill,” she said. “What is owed for the threshing. And the year the river came up the church steps.”

And it was no harder than that. It was the easiest thing he had ever done with his hands. He could have done it with the mill over his head.

He put the pencil down on the table.

“Writing does not count,” he said. “Nothing I can do counts. The feather is no good to me. It would be the first thing in my life that was hard.”

* * *

The aunt picked up the pencil, put it in her pocket, and looked at him.

“You land by yourself.”

That was the first time anybody had said it in those words, and he could not answer.

“Yes,” he said at last.

“The cart goes in at the gate. So do the walkers. You come in over the hedge, every time, with nobody at the other end, and you never got used to it.”

“It is not the flying,” said Merrick. “It is that everybody else arrives the same as everybody, and I am the only one who does not, and I have had enough of it.”

“Then fly it, put it down in the top field, and walk in with the cart, like a man,” she said. “No rule says you have to come down inside the gate.”

* * *

He did it at the next fair. He put the feather down above the mill, waited for the wind, and went up over the parish, higher than he had ever been, and the county lay below him like a tray.

Then he came down in the top field, put the feather in his coat, and walked in through the gate beside the cart.

“You walked the last hour,” said his aunt.

“I did,” said Merrick, and bought a bun off her.

“I have sold the feather,” she said. “To a boy of nine from the next valley who is frightened of heights, and it took him at once, which annoyed everybody.”

* * *

Merrick writes. The feather is with a boy who needs it.

He is still the tallest man in the parish, and he still arrives on foot, and he is the one they send for when the parish has to write something down, and he does not mind.
