# The Golden Key Was Actually a Seed

![Cover art: The Golden Key Was Actually a Seed](../art/accessible/96-the-golden-key-was-actually-a-seed.webp)

The row of cottages was numbers nine to nineteen, and Mr Adeyemi owned all of them.

In March he left a gold key in number fourteen, wedged under the door with a card round it. He told nobody, and it was gone by Friday.

Rhian was nine. She lived at number thirteen.

She picked it up. “It is warm,” she said. “And there has been no sun on that step all week.”

It had a round head and three teeth, and it weighed nothing.

“I found a key,” she said to Pips at number twelve, whose front window looked down the steps of thirteen and fourteen.

“Give it back to Mr Adeyemi.”

“He said I could have a shilling for finding things.”

“He said a shilling for finding things he had lost. That is not the same thing.”

* * *

Pips said a gold key only opens chests. The boy from sixteen said it opens nothing. The girl from seventeen said it opens a door with something terrible behind it.

“One of you is wrong,” said Rhian.

So she went along the row in the dark and put it in the lock of number fourteen, out of spite.

The lock turned a quarter turn, and that was all. Then it made a small noise like a boot in gravel.

“That is not a lock,” she said. “That is a door being talked to.”

Then a hairline crack opened in the wood beside it, and something green pushed out.

“Oh,” said Rhian.

She took her hand away. The green pushed out another inch, and went back, and pushed out again.

She went home and had her tea. Then she came back with a spoon, dug a hole in the crack, put the key in it, covered it, and watered it.

* * *

On the first day nothing happened.

On the second day a green point came out of the middle of number fourteen’s step and split the stone in two.

On the third day it was as tall as the door, and the man from number sixteen came out to see it.

“It is a weed,” he said.

“It is a tree,” said Rhian.

“My name is Rhian,” she told it. “Rhian with an R. If you are going to lean at somebody, lean at me.”

On the fourth morning it was over the roof, and the row had started taking things in off the sills.

“It should be cut down,” said Mr Adeyemi. “It will cost me to get it out of the step, and I shall put it in the rent.”

“It is not even mine,” said Rhian. “It grew out of your step.”

* * *

Pips was the only one in the row who did not think it was a weed.

“It is not a weed,” she said. “Weeds do not do that.”

“Do what?”

“Only look as though they are being polite.”

Then a glass marble came down with a small knock, like a coin in a tin. Blue, with a green twist inside, and there had been a search for it since Easter.

“That is Tommy’s from number nine,” said Mrs Okafor from number eleven. “I have told that boy for months.”

“It came out of the tree,” said Pips. “It did not come out of anybody’s pocket.”

“Whose tree is it, then?”

Nobody could say.

* * *

Then a tram ticket, folded in four, missing since the winter, and it belonged to a man at number nineteen who had not lived there a year.

“He has not lived here a year,” said Pips. “So it came off a roof, not out of a pocket.”

Then a small green ring, off the finger of a man at number nine, and everybody said it was his wife’s, and he said she had put it in his tea tin on purpose, which she had.

“It is a wishing tree,” said Rhian. “It is a rubbish wishing tree.”

Then a speckled brown bird’s egg, which the street kept on a windowsill in a cup of water until it hatched.

And on the ninth morning there was her own front tooth in the leaves, clean as a spoon.

She had lost it in June, and told the row that Pips had taken it.

“That is mine,” Rhian said. “I lost that in June. I said you took it.”

Pips picked it up, gave it back, and said nothing at all.

Rhian stood in the road and could not look at her.

“I am sorry.”

“You said it to me, and to the milkman, and to my mother. For three months.” Pips folded her arms. “You are nine. You get one stupid thing, and I have had mine.”

* * *

That night Rhian sat on the step and worked it out, then went up the row knocking until the street woke.

“The tree only drops things that belong to the row,” she said, “and only once, and it does not say who they belong to.”

Nobody argued. That was the part they did not want to sit with.

* * *

The next day Mr Adeyemi put it to them in the yard, and said it was an experiment, and it was for a week only.

“For one week, nobody in this row says that anybody else in this row has lost anything.”

“And if we do?”

“Then you are back to where you were, and so is the tree.”

It was the worst week of the summer. By Wednesday there were two rows about a purse and a shouting match over a gate, and nobody could be cross about a lost thing.

Nobody said the week was over, and nobody started it again, because it was not needed.
