# The Alphabet of the Hedgerow

![Cover art: The Alphabet of the Hedgerow](../art/accessible/89-the-alphabet-of-the-hedgerow.webp)

Bramble was in the puddle doing sums.

The puddle was the big one by the farm gate, the one that holds the sky in it, and a small brown shape at the edge was going in and out and in and out.

Sami put his bag on the wall and crouched to see better.

“Are you counting?”

“I am naming,” said Bramble. “There is a difference, and you are too young to be told it twice.”

“What are you naming?”

“Everything in the puddle. In order.” She put her nose down to it. “Apple. Bramble. Cloud. Ditch. Apple. Bramble. Cloud. Ditch.”

“You have got as far as the fourth one and you have started again.”

“I am at D,” said Bramble. “I have been at D a long time. I lost a bootlace on Tuesday and had to go back to the A to say where it was.”

Sami said the true thing, as he always did. “I cannot read.”

“I know. You do the mouth thing at the letters on the shop wall and nothing comes out.”

* * *

“Why in the alphabet and not any order at all?” said Sami. “Why not just the things you like.”

Bramble thought about it properly, and her nose went left and right like a brown boat.

“Because if you can say what is around you in order,” she said, “then you cannot lose track of where you are. A is first, so if you have got to the P’s you know you have come a long way. And if you are back on the A you know you have slipped, and you go and find where the slip was. It is not the things. It is the order.”

* * *

The wind came round the barn and went up in one piece, and the rain came after it, not drops but the whole sky letting go at once.

Sami did not know the road. He knew the shape of it, the way you know a room in the dark, but his ears were full of rain and every gate was the same gate. The sign at the crossroads had a name on it, and the name was water.

He got his coat over his head and shouted into the hedge.

“Bramble. Bramble.”

“Here,” said the nettles.

“I cannot read the signs.”

“No,” said Bramble. “You cannot. But you can talk. So do not ask me for the way. Ask me for a letter, and then go and find it, and then say it to me, and I will tell you if you have got it.”

“What letter?”

“H,” said Bramble. “Ask me for the H, and then go and look for it.”

* * *

Sami went along the hedge with his hand on the wet branches, and called everything out loud so that Bramble could say no.

“A is an ash tree. No, that is a letter on a post. A. Fine.”

“C is a crow. There is no cow. There is a dead crow by the drain, so C is a crow.”

“D is a dog. There is a dog on the wall.”

“That is a cat,” said Bramble, from under the leaves.

“It has a very long tail for a dog.”

Sami stood still in the rain. “D is a cat,” he said. “Fine. D is a cat.”

He went on. H was a hand, and Sami had a hand, and he said it out loud.

“Not in the world,” said Bramble. “It is on you. Keep going.”

He got round the end of the hedge and out into the field by the allotments, where the ground went soft and the wind dropped, and there in the dark, the whole length of the field, was a hedge.

“Oh,” he said. “Oh, that is a H.”

“What did I tell you,” said Bramble.

* * *

They went along under it with the rain going through, and Sami said the letters out loud as they came. I was a jam. K was a kettle. M was Mrs Vinge’s gate, which is not a letter, and Bramble let him have.

The allotments ended. A low wall came up out of the dark, and a house behind it, and the gate.

Sami nearly went down in the mud.

“That is our gate,” he said. “We come in under that.”

“Yes,” said Bramble. “That is what a hedge is for.”

Sami stood in the rain with his arms out wide, the way you do when you are very glad about something.

“I have got the whole alphabet,” he said. “All of it. I have got it badly, and I have got it in the wrong order, and I am keeping it anyway.”

Bramble came up out of his sleeve, where she had been riding, and shook the water off, and said nothing about it, because she was busy.

* * *

Sami hung his coat on the hook and stood dripping on the mat.

“What are you doing tomorrow?” said Bramble.

“Coming out to the puddle at ten. And you are starting at the A. At the top. First thing, before anything else.”

“But I told you, I lose my —”

“You lose your place because you go too quick,” said Sami. “A. B. C. D. Then you stop. I will count them out until you have got them, and then you say them, and then we go on to E. You will get past D in a week. I am doing it with a bell.”

Bramble looked up at him for a long moment. She was a hedgehog, and she was not going to say that this was the best news she had had since the summer. So she said instead:

“You have got your boots on the wrong feet.”

Sami looked down. She was right.
