# The Map of Sounds

![Cover art: The Map of Sounds](../art/accessible/28-the-map-of-sounds.webp)

Leila pressed both boots to the flagstones of Bell Square and held still. Market crowds moved around her: cart wheels rattled, coins clicked, and feet tapped. Underneath all the movement, the bell tower should have hummed.

It did not.

Leila was nine, and she made maps for the town. Her maps showed where things shook. She marked busy gates with tight black lines and quiet lanes with pale ones. Customers carried trays of colored chalk for her. Now every tower line on her newest map ended in a blunt point.

“Grandmother,” she said, pointing upward.

Grandmother Mara turned from the fountain. A ring of brass weights hung from her belt so Leila could follow her through a crowd. The weights clicked in her palm as she signed, Have you felt the tower?

Leila shook her head. Grandmother passed her a written note. The morning bell and the spring warning bell had both failed. Water was leaking around the eastern drain, and nobody knew when to move uphill.

At the tower door, Rafi knelt beside an open toolbox. He was nine too and built bells too heavy for either of them to lift alone. He was deaf, like Leila. Other people wrote questions on his slate while Leila drew maps on hers. A red measuring cord ran from his wrist to the tower door.

“I can feel the hinges,” he said, his deep voice swallowed by the square. “They are still. The whole tower is still.”

Leila pointed down. The square might be hiding what the tower had lost.

They searched the stones. Rafi tapped each with his chisel. Leila rested her palm against the next. Near the east drain, one stone gave a tiny tremor.

Rafi drew a chalk circle around it and added a line toward the tower.

The east drain was clogged, but water had already risen around its iron bars. A culvert beneath the square ran toward the hillside spring. Before a heavy rain, the tower sent one low note through it. The vibration warned people uphill. Now that note was somewhere below them.

“I know that pattern,” Leila said.

She remembered last winter’s storm, lying on the map-room floor with her palm against the wall. Grandmother had tapped five slow groups: tower, tower, tower, pause, two taps.

Leila tapped the rhythm on the stone. Rafi put his hand beside hers. Far below, something answered with a light push.

The trapped note was under the square.

They showed Grandmother three possible routes: down the drain, beneath the fountain pipes, or along the old bell path. Grandmother Mara pointed to the last one. Rafi wrote, THE BELL PATH IS WEAKER.

They found the route with water. Leila filled a copper bowl at the fountain. When Rafi touched its rim above the right flagstone, tiny rings spread through the water. The circles pointed toward an arrow hidden beneath the market scale.

Behind it was an iron hatch. The warning note buzzed against Leila’s ankles whenever she stepped close. A thin strip of buried iron ran from the hatch toward the tower: the bell path. At its middle, two ridges met.

While they worked, water crept around the eastern grate. The market people grew quiet.

Rafi drew the lifting frame that could raise the tower’s inner gear. Its rope lay under a bench, stiff with age. The frame needed balance, not one person’s strength.

Leila drew two chalk lines showing how weight must shift. Rafi held up both hands, pointed to the crowd, and made a strong arm. Grandmother understood.

Soon the frame stood in the square. One rope ran from the buried gear to the bell wheel. Another crossed the shoulders of six market guards and tied to a water barrel. The barrel filled through a siphon, making steady weight.

Leila returned to the arrow and knelt with one palm on each buried path. Rafi tapped the warning rhythm on the frame. His pick made a clear knock. One line sent a hard tremor through her left hand. The other sent a soft one through her right. Both trembled together.

She scraped away loose soil. Beneath it lay a bronze tooth no larger than her palm. The final note had knocked the bell’s inner gear free. The tooth had fallen into the flooded junction and wedged across the drain, trapping the warning note and blocking the culvert.

A hook curved from the tooth. Rafi tied a cord to it and passed the cord through a crack in the hatch. Grandmother and the guards drew it over the lifting frame.

Leila knelt beside the hatch, clear of the turning frame. She tapped directions on its stone rim: one for Rafi to steady the wheel, two to lift, three to pause.

At the first lift, the tooth stayed tight. Water spread across the east side of the square.

At the second, mud squeezed from the paving and a finger-wide crack opened.

At the third, Rafi called, “Stop!”

The rope pulled unevenly. The strong pull traveled through the left path into the tower, but the right path had gone weak. The frame was turning.

Leila tapped twice, then four times. Left. Shift. Grandmother felt the frame’s pull with her palm. She pointed to the guards. Four men on the left loosened their rope. Two on the right took up the slack. Rafi rotated the frame until both chains lay level.

“Again!” he called.

The guards pulled together. The barrel dropped lower. The frame creaked, then the buried gear rose. The loosened debris dropped into the deeper culvert, and the water carried its silt away. The tooth came up black with mud.

Rafi wiped it clean. Its rim shone dull gold.

Grandmother lowered it into his hands. Then he climbed the tower ladder while everyone held the frame steady. Leila felt the bell wheel turn. Once. Twice.

On the third turn, Rafi fitted the bronze tooth into place. A strong vibration passed down the tower, through the bell path, and across Bell Square. Every chalk tray jumped. The fountain bowls shivered. The great bell spoke one clear, low note that rose through Leila’s boots and settled in her bones.

Water drained away. No paving broke, and the spring remained contained.

The next morning, Rafi made a small bronze hand for Leila. It rested on her map table above words describing the tower. Beneath it, her new map showed the old bell line, the drain, the fountain pipes, and the place where the final note had waited. Her name was written in the corner beside a circle meaning a place watched with care.

At the dedication, Grandmother tapped five groups against the tower wall. Rafi added a final pair. Leila finished it with one steady tap.

The town had not lost its warning. It had gained a way to check it, too.
