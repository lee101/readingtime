# The Bear Who Lent Out His Winter

![Cover art: The Bear Who Lent Out His Winter](../art/accessible/99-the-bear-who-lent-out-his-winter.webp)

The bear woke in a hurry and found the year wrong.

He woke at four under a wet stone. The stream was not frozen, and a thin crust of snow lay on the ground, the first of a winter nobody asked for.

He lay on his side and got it out at once, because he had thought about nothing else since then. A year ago a boy slept the night by his fire and went without a word.

So the bear had slept through his winter because of it. A week and a day of grass, and rain, and the noisy part of the year gone past the cave.

The bear got up, dug out birch bark, made a fire, and did not eat. Then he went to the corner for his scroll.

* * *

Osvald’s grandmother was making bread when the boy came in, and she did not stop.

Snow ran off the cuffs of his third coat. He was seven. The woodpile was a third done, the snow was early, and there was no room for a week.

Grandmother looked at the woodpile, and at the sky, and went on with the bread.

“You will want the fire,” Osvald said.

“Mm,” said his grandmother.

He went out. The snow had started again.

* * *

A mile down the wood his face went numb and his boots filled. He thought about the shut mill at Rhun, and then he thought about the cave.

He was shaking as he came up the bank, but he knew the stones. The cave mouth stood open, the fire had not gone out in a year, and a bear sat by it.

Osvald stopped and could not get any further.

“Sit down,” said the bear.

Osvald’s teeth were going.

A scroll lay on the bear’s knee, unrolled to the middle.

“You slept in here a year ago. You slept the whole night and did not ask. A bear does not get a night in winter, and that night was gone.”

“I am sorry,” he said.

“You are not sorry, you are cold. Sorry will be later. I have thought about this a year and written it out, so you agree to it and I do not lose my nerve. You owe me a week and a day.”

“All right,” said Osvald.

“Do not say all right yet. That is the wrong word. The first term: you wake me.”

“...I am trying.”

“I can see it. That is not trying, that is a noise. Second. You bring me my winter, mine, from me. If a man cannot do that, I do not know what can.”

“You cannot give a day to anybody,” said Osvald. “There are not that many days.”

* * *

“Third. You bring me firewood, five armfuls, before I wake.”

Osvald looked at his hands. They were two small pink things at the ends of his sleeves.

“That is not five armfuls for me,” he said.

“Then the terms do not suit you. They are fair, but you may go to Rhun.”

“Fourth. You do not speak. Not a word, from the time you sit down until I sleep. Your voice in a den in winter takes a week off me.”

Osvald shut his mouth.

“Fifth. You do not move.”

“I cannot not move for a week.”

“That is your business, not mine.”

“Take it,” said the bear. Osvald could not, being small, so his two cold hands went to the far end and held on.

* * *

By the middle of the afternoon the terms had eaten the scroll.

The bear added a seventh about not opening the door, an eighth about lying on the stone, and a ninth about waiting properly. Each was good, and each took time out of his week.

The bear came to the end and found nothing left to add. The boy could not meet one of them, not the first and not the last, and it came out where he was going to be cold anyway.

“Well,” said the bear. “If you are going to be cold, be cold on purpose, in a den, with a fire, and learn it properly. I will lend you my winter.”

* * *

“Your job is to be still, which is the one thing you are bad at, and my job is to not be disappointed, which is the one thing I am worst at.”

Osvald thought about that, and then said, “How long is a week for a bear?”

“Half a minute,” said the bear. “Go on.”

* * *

“Still,” said Osvald.

“I went still on the second night,” he said. “In small pieces. In the back of the head first.”

A week and a day is a long time to be awake beside somebody who cannot be got wrong. Twice the boy spoke out of turn, and the bear said nothing, and it cost him something he had not got.

* * *

The thaw came in the last week of the month. The bear came down out of the wood on the first warm afternoon, a week and a day late. Nobody had a plate ready.

Osvald’s grandmother was in the yard with a basket on her hip.

“There he is,” she said. “You can have him for the wood. I will not ask how.”

So the bear got the wood. He carried it up and down from the birch until it was stacked, and split it, and did the low fence, and ate nothing out of the house, and at the end they let him lie in the sun.

* * *

His scroll is framed on the wall of his cave, at the height where a bear can see it.

It is a very long scroll. There are twenty-nine terms on it, the last is scratched out, and the one under it too, and nobody has read all of it.
