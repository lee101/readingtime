# The Firefly Post Office

![Cover art: The Firefly Post Office](../art/accessible/45-the-firefly-post-office.webp)

The summer sky had gone so dark that stars looked like salt on a black table. At the end of Juniper Lane, a blue-shuttered post office grew inside an old cedar tree.

Nia Venn entered through a door only three inches high.

Inside, a thousand tiny drawers climbed the walls. Letters rustled in their slots, stamped by moth wings, frog feet, firefly light, and human thumbs. Nia’s brass-buttoned coat reached her knees. She rolled its sleeves above her wrists and sorted with her chin level and shoulders square.

A letter intended for the moon went to a paper bag.

“Air mail,” Nia murmured, laughing. “Still counts if it arrives above the clouds.”

At the sorting desk, Postmaster Bramble adjusted his spectacles. He was a broad frog wearing a waistcoat stitched from brown seed leaves. His throat puffed as he read the dark-summer ledger.

“Bad night,” he said. “The moon moths will not fly. The frogs are sleeping. The fireflies are sheltering under mushrooms. Human mail has tangled with beetle mail.”

“What do we do?” Nia asked.

Bramble tapped his blunt finger on the ledger. “We keep promises. Letters are not merely paper. A letter is a promise someone made to someone else.”

A soft chime came from the cedar’s deepest drawer.

Nia opened it. Heat rolled over her hand, carrying the scents of ginger, wet wool, and a lit chimney. Inside lay an envelope no larger than a thumb joint. Its paper shimmered between gray and gold.

Bramble became still. “A memory letter.”

“Can fireflies carry it?” Nia asked.

A small green glow lifted from the desk. Fennel the firefly hovered there, wings beating too quickly to see. He had a red postal cap, which kept sliding over one antenna.

“Certainly,” he said. “I carry invitations. This may be an invitation.”

He tucked the letter into a sling he wore across his thorax. His light advanced, but the envelope did not move. Its weight pulled Fennel down like an anchor.

“Moth light to guide, frog strength to carry, human hands to choose,” Bramble recited. “That is a full-post memory. Bring a moth and a courier frog.”

Outside, water slid from every leaf. A charcoal-gray moth named Veil waited under Nia’s windowsill. Four wings were missing, and she wore a scarf stitched from spider silk.

“You look severe,” Nia said.

“I am,” Veil replied in a whisper like dry pages turning. “Severe moths are harder for rain to drown.”

A delivery frog appeared on the gutter. His name was Toll, and his mail satchel was as round as a melon. A feathered cap shaded his eyes.

“I deliver only the first attempt,” Toll said.

A postal rule Nia did not like.

The memory letter began to glow. On its face, dark letters wrote themselves.

“For Tali Grey, in the house where the blue rain boots wait. Please come home. The moon is almost out.”

Nia fastened her satchel. “Then we had better hurry.”

They left the post office by the cedar’s high door. Fennel flew before them, lighting dew on the grass. Veil balanced on Nia’s shoulder, protecting the letter beneath one wing. Toll squatted in the lane, surprisingly light on his long feet.

Nia raised the letter. “Home first. Then the door.”

Toll nodded. “If the home is wrong, the memory will stay shut.”

Fennel led them past rattling cottages. He paused at a blue door, but the letter went cold. At an apple tree crowded with moth lanterns, nothing changed. A pale window reflected a pond that was not there, and Toll stamped nervously.

“False memory,” Veil said. “Some things look like what we hope to find.”

They crossed a meadow where the grass shone wetly beneath a moonless sky. At last Fennel hovered over a house near the river. One warm yellow window shone. Blue boots stood by the back step.

The letter beat like a tiny heart.

Toll crouched. “That is the home.”

A floorboard creaked inside. Tali Grey hurried to the door, saw the small figures on her step, and froze. She was nine years old, dressed in yellow pajamas beneath a red wool coat. Raindrops shone in her short black hair.

“Is my mother coming?” she asked.

Nia’s stomach tightened. The envelope had not brought a person. It carried a moment, not a rescue.

“We have a letter for you,” Nia said gently. “It says to come home.”

Tali shook her head. “I live here.”

Veil glided down and pointed with a wing. The last line on the envelope was melting into the paper.

A memory letter must be received before the moon rises, Toll explained. If the recipient touched it after dawn, the memory would vanish back into the night.

Nia looked at the river. The storm had torn a willow loose. Its roots had filled the footpath, and black water rushed through the low place beyond it.

Toll peered over the mailbag. “No frog may leap a river while holding another creature’s promise. The current steals what the moon hides.”

“I can cross the upper stones,” Nia said.

“Too small for Toll,” Veil warned.

Toll lowered himself. “I can carry the letter. Not you.”

The envelope pulsed once and dimmed.

Then the yellow window went dark.

A bolt of thunder shook the house, though the sky held no clouds. The first gray hint of morning touched the eastern trees. Dawn was coming.

Nia took Toll’s long mail strap in both hands. “Fennel, show us where the water is shallow. Veil, stay over the letter. Toll, trust the stones I choose.”

“That is not in the rules,” Toll said.

“It is in the address,” Nia replied.

She stepped into the river.

Cold water slapped her knees. By Fennel’s trembling light, Nia found flat stones and laid the loose end of the strap across them. Toll hopped onto the first stone. Veil flew low, sheltering the memory letter from spray.

“Wait,” Nia said when a branch rushed past. “Now.”

Toll leaped. The strap snapped straight. Nia slid, mud sucking at one boot, but she held on. Veil’s wing broke the spray that struck the envelope. Fennel lit the next landing.

One step. Two. Three.

On the far bank, Toll landed among blue flowers with the letter held high. Nia pulled herself after him, soaked to the shoulders.

The post office clock sounded one last chime.

Tali’s back door stood open. Warm air spilled out, carrying the scent of ginger and a wool blanket. Behind her stood her mother, Mrs. Grey, still tying an apron over her work clothes.

“Tali?” Mrs. Grey looked at Toll, at Nia, and at the glowing envelope. “What happened?”

“I was afraid of forgetting,” Tali whispered. “Since the sky went dark, I can’t remember our first summer.”

Toll placed the letter gently on the doormat. “A letter must be received by the person it names.”

Nia took off her red cap. She had used it to keep rain from the letter, though the cap had not protected Nia at all.

Tali knelt. “Will it hurt?”

“No,” Veil said. “It will only remember for you.”

Tali touched the paper.

Gold light poured around her hands. Little Tali in blue boots held a soup-ladle like a telescope, chasing rainbows as sunshine crossed the wet yard. Mrs. Grey stood behind her, dripping coat and all, making thunder noises until they laughed so hard they sat in the puddles.

Tali began to laugh now. Mrs. Grey covered her mouth, then folded her daughter into a tight embrace.

“I remember,” Tali whispered. “I remember your terrible thunder voice.”

“That is my finest thunder voice,” Mrs. Grey said.

The memory settled into Tali like a warm light finding a window. The child put on her blue boots and stepped outside, wearing one on each foot.

Nia’s companions waited while Mrs. Grey wrapped the sogged apprentice in a spare blanket.

“Tell the post office I am writing tonight,” Mrs. Grey said. “A thank-you letter is a promise too.”

Nia nodded. “We will deliver it safely.”

On the walk back, Fennel rode on her cap, Veil sheltered beneath the brim, and Toll carried the empty satchel. By the time the blue-shuttered office came into view, one golden edge of summer sky had brightened.

At her sorting desk, Nia found a fresh sheet. Before the postmaster could stamp it, she wrote a thank-you letter, sealed it with three drops of pond water, and placed it in the drawer for Juniper Lane.

Bramble adjusted his spectacles. “One memory delivered before dawn.”

Nia hung her dripping coat by the tiny door. “Three carriers and one wet apprentice.”

“Four,” Fennel corrected.

From her warm mailbox, Mrs. Grey’s letter began to glow.
