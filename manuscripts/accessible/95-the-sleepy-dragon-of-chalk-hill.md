# The Sleepy Dragon of Chalk Hill

![Cover art: The Sleepy Dragon of Chalk Hill](../art/accessible/95-the-sleepy-dragon-of-chalk-hill.webp)

Under Chalk Hill there is a dragon. He is green, and he is curled round in a circle, and his nose is tucked under his own tail.

Every breath he takes throws a spark. The spark goes up out of the hill and comes down on the roofs of the village, and the village learned to live with it the way you learn to live with a bell going all night.

The rule is simple. Out after dark and you will not get a spark on your head.

Nobody has ever woken him.

* * *

Bo was eight. His gran had told him the dragon was a nuisance, and so had the man in the shop, and so had the boy who was nine and knew more.

“Keep off that hill, Bo.”

“Why?”

“Because of the dragon.”

“Is he still at it?”

“He will never stop.” His gran set the basket down on the step. “It is not him, Bo. It is what he does. There is a difference.”

But it never did sound like a difference to him.

* * *

In the autumn the school wrote to the village about the hill.

They had walked up it with a tape, all of it, the long white slope and the flat top. They wanted it for a playground, with grass, and a fence, and a gate.

The village said no that same afternoon, all together, and then felt bad about saying it.

“He will not move,” said the man in the shop. “Nobody can move a dragon.”

“Nobody can wake one either,” said Bo.

* * *

So the school went on waiting, and the letter came back a year later with a date on it.

Bo went up on the Sunday with a sandwich in a paper bag. He carried it down the shaft in the chalk, because the shaft is where the dragon sleeps, and the shaft is where you go if you mean it.

He was not sure about it at the bottom. The spines went up past him, further than the church tower, and the near wing was folded like a shut door.

He set the sandwich on the scale above the eye and sat down on the chalk.

A long time passed. A lark went up the shaft. A spark came out of the dragon’s nose and drifted away from him.

Then one eye came open.

“You are not asleep,” said Bo.

“I am conserving.” The eye turned along him slowly, the way a lamp moves across a shed. “There is a great deal of me and there is not much hill.”

“How long have you been here?”

“Two hundred years and a bit.”

“Are you asleep now?”

“I am resting my eyes. It is not the same, and if you say it is the same I shall be cross.” The chin shifted a little. “You have brought me a sandwich.”

“Cheese and pickle.”

“Good. I have not had one since the tiles.”

* * *

“The tiles,” said Bo.

“I was flying a cargo of tiles,” said the dragon. “Free. Over this village, because the man at the mill had none and could not pay for them, and I was not going to let a roof go unthatched because a man had no money. A gust came up out of the valley. The tip of my wing came off. It came down and stuck in the field by the mill, and it cracked when it landed, and I have not been the same since.”

“Did it fall on somebody?”

“It fell on a field.” The eye rolled up the shaft. “Then the man at the mill went out and looked at it, and then he had an idea.”

“You have a plaque.”

“I have a stone with a lie cut into it. It says the dragon struck here, and lost his wing, and that the village was in great danger, and that God was thanked. Two hundred years old. It is the only thing anybody in this village has ever wanted from me.”

* * *

They took it up with the school on the Monday, in front of all the children, and the caretaker, and the head teacher, and Mr Adeyemi, and the vicar. It rained a little. Nobody could think of a thing to say for a long time.

Then Mr Okonkwo, who had used the word nuisance more than anybody else in the village, said it out loud.

“We were wrong to blame him for two hundred years, and we knew nothing about him, and we are sorry, and the stone can come down.”

That was the whole of it. The children said it after him, all together. The head teacher wrote it down. The vicar said that was proper, which from the vicar was a lot.

* * *

The hill stays a hill.

The school got the field behind the church instead, with a fence and a gate and a strip of grass, and a boy with a clipboard, and they were pleased with it, though it is low and it holds water.

The stone came down on the Thursday. The mason had to take the letters off with a cold chisel, because they were in the stone and not on it, and the last one took him all afternoon.

The dragon slept properly after that, deep in and all night, with his nose under his tail. The sparks stopped coming up the shaft, and the roofs went quiet, and the woman in number nine said her chest had been better that week and she did not know why.

Everybody was disappointed, and everybody said so out loud.
