# The Kitchen Under the Hill

![Cover art: The Kitchen Under the Hill](../art/accessible/77-the-kitchen-under-the-hill.webp)

The good spoon was gone. So were the ball of string and the tin with the buttons, and by six o’clock three people were certain Bryn had taken them.

She had not. Her brother said, “You and your stories, Bryn,” and afterwards everybody looked at the stories instead of at the shelf.

On Monday she had said there was a door in the pantry. On Tuesday she had said she could smell bread under the house.

The pantry was cold and white. The flour bin stood in the corner, and had never once been moved, because it was heavy.

Bryn put both hands on it and pushed. It went over with a scrape, and behind it was a little door, no taller than a chair, painted the same green as the wall.

She got the latch open with a hairpin. Behind the door a staircase went down a long way, and the smell of bread came up it.

“Are you the small one at the top of the stairs?” said a voice.

“Yes,” said Bryn. “I am. I am sorry about the string.”

“Never mind the string,” said the voice. “Come down. Mind the fourth step, it is a liar.”

* * *

She was the size of a piano standing up.

She stood in a kitchen as long as a church hall, with a hearth you could park a cart in. In the black stone above her head a great wheel turned slowly by itself, drawing the smell of the cooking up through the hill.

Three long tables ran the length of the kitchen, all of them set. Nine hundred white plates. On one lay a loaf of bread the size of a table.

“Are you cooking for the school?” said Bryn.

“For the whole valley,” said the cook. “The gas is off in the valley. Nine hundred for supper.”

There was one place set, on a three-legged stool at the cook’s own table.

“Is that mine?” she said.

The cook picked up a board the length of her arm, covered in writing.

“You are not on the list,” she said.

* * *

Bryn’s stomach went down through her boots.

“Oh,” she said. “I did not know. I will go up again and say nothing.”

“You came without being asked,” said the cook, “so that is a whole pot of trouble. But you are not going up hungry.”

“But I am not on the list.”

“The list is for bread,” said the cook. “The list is only for bread.”

Bryn’s mouth had gone dry. “What is my job, then?”

“Sit on your stool,” said the cook, “and say thank you properly.”

“For what?”

“For the supper in front of you, before you touch any of it,” said the cook. “You stand up and say thank you properly, and you do not stop until it is done.”

“How long is that?”

“Longer than you think,” said the cook. “There is one thing I ask in my kitchen, and a guest who says thank you properly is remembered forever.”

* * *

Bryn stood on her three-legged stool and said, “Thank you.”

“That is a noise,” said the cook. “That is what a gate says. Go on.”

“I am thanking you for the supper,” said Bryn.

“That is the bit before it,” said the cook. “Look at those tables, then tell me, and leave nothing out.”

So Bryn looked.

She thanked the cook for the hearth, which was warm through the stone. She thanked her for the warm stair, and for the fourth step, which had lied and been kind. She thanked her for the good spoon, and the string, and the tin with the buttons.

“They are in the pot,” said the cook. “I am glad of the string. I sew my own aprons and I have never had a button in my life.”

“That is the good spoon,” said Bryn.

“It is the best spoon under this hill,” said the cook, “and there was a hole in the butter this morning.”

Then the cook went on cooking, and never told her to hurry. The oven door was as tall as Bryn.

It took an hour.

An hour is a long time when your legs go cold on a stool. The cook counted out the loaves in batches and did not go away, which was how Bryn knew she was right.

* * *

Then there was the extra.

By the hearth, at the far side of the table, there was one more loaf, small and hot, and it was not on the list.

“You are a guest,” said the cook, “and there was only a little supper, and you have stood on my stool for an hour being polite, and so I am baking a loaf for the valley. A valley ought to smell something good.”

Bryn thought about that. “Everybody in the valley?”

“Everybody,” said the cook. “The wheel will see to it.”

The cook put the small loaf in with the others and shut the door. The wheel turned faster, and the smell came down off it in a thick rope, up the stair, through the green door, and into every house in the valley by six o’clock.

* * *

The pantry door is still there.

Bryn’s father put the flour bin back, and Bryn moved it again the same evening, and left it against the wall.

She goes down every Sunday. She sits on the stool. And she says thank you properly, and it still takes an hour, and the valley still smells of baking by six o’clock, and nobody has ever asked her how it gets there.
