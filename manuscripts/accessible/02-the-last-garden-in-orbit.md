# The Last Garden in Orbit

![Cover art: The Last Garden in Orbit](../art/accessible/02-the-last-garden-in-orbit.webp)

The orchard woke before the station did.

At 04:10 station time, Amara Venn unlocked the cultivation ring and found the peach tree leaning toward her. Its leaves were broad, dark green, and silver underneath, a color no fruit tree should have possessed in standardized light. Damp soil and crushed apples scented the air. From the root gallery below came a patient ticking, like knuckles testing a locked door.

Amara was thirty-six, with close-cropped black hair silvering at one temple and grit beneath her nails. Her flight suit hung loose at the waist after a night spent replacing an irrigation pump. She touched the tree’s rough bark. It pressed its leaves against her palm.

A warning strip turned amber. The station’s oxygen recycler had begun drawing from the root gallery instead of the emergency tanks. The orchard’s algae scrubbers were designed to share air only during a declared shortage, and no shortage had been filed.

The tree vibrated against her wrist. Amara checked the tablet on the wall cradle. Every alert read normal except the flow direction: oxygen was moving toward the lower deck, where seven crew members slept. One destination pulsed brighter than the others.

Theo Rill arrived twenty minutes later in a blue engineer’s coverall, one sleeve fastened and the other rolled. He was thirty-nine, broad-shouldered, with a shaved head and a gray streak in his beard. He had brought a toolkit but left it untouched. He studied the pale roots through the glass floor.

“Your tree is stealing air,” he said.

“My tree is growing.”

“You’re both doing it.”

The strip changed to red. Station logic would isolate the cultivation ring in six minutes unless the airflow was balanced. A mechanical voice announced a root-gallery intrusion.

Amara looked through the glass. The sentient root system was no longer confined to the beds. White runners had pushed through irrigation channels and climbed the supports. Fine rootlets trembled in the ventilation stream, drawing air toward the sleeping crew. One branch curled around a maintenance hatch, then withdrew.

Theo crouched and placed his palm on the glass. “It knows where the oxygen is going.”

“Plants respond to pressure and light.”

“They don’t tap a valve.”

A root tip rose to meet his hand. The hatch popped open with a hiss, releasing cool air smelling of wet wood and iron. The root touched his wrist. A thread of amber light wound around his fingers and disappeared beneath his skin. His face went pale.

“Your pulse just dropped,” Amara said.

The roots released him. The amber line vanished. The station lights dimmed, and a clipped tone sounded from below.

“Something is waking the sleepers,” Theo said.

Amara grabbed the orchard’s emergency key, a plain iron bar etched with a grove of trees. It opened the cultivation ring and the gallery beneath it. She had carried it for six years, despite the station’s preference for biometric locks.

“You can leave this to engineering,” she said.

“I can leave this to you.”

“Then why are you here?”

He glanced at the roots. “Because engineering is what I do.”

* * *

The gallery was no longer a garden. Soil climbed the walls in terraces, and the orchard pipes were braided into a living lattice. Each turn smelled of loam, pepper, warm circuitry, or rain caught on hot metal. The air tasted sweet. Below, six crew members slept behind transparent doors, their breath marking the inside of their masks.

Amara’s knees weakened. She had tended this orchard since the first harvest, when the trees were saplings in sterile boxes and the crew ate pea cakes flavored with synthetic chlorophyll. The station was called a seed ark, though no one aboard had believed it would need to save more than seeds.

A low pulse moved through the roots. When Amara put her palm to the floor, it answered her, delicate as a fingertip tapping through skin.

“It’s not steering one crew member,” she said. “It’s sorting the oxygen by body. The healthier ones, with less carbon in their muscles.”

“Who wakes longest?”

Amara pointed toward the deepest chamber. “The person it has already chosen.”

They found Keira Sol there, suspended in a lattice of roots and cable. She was thirty-three, the navigator, narrow-faced and sharp-tongued, with a scar through her left eyebrow. Her maintenance suit’s oxygen hose had been threaded into the old emergency supply. She rose and fell with unnerving slowness.

Amara had known Keira for four years. She noticed every loose bolt and once explained the proper tension for a repaired trellis in six precise steps. Keira had refused evacuation because the storm’s course was uncertain.

“I thought she was in med bay,” Amara said.

“The roots moved her.”

Theo found a pulse in the cable. “They’re teaching the station to count her as oxygen.”

Amara pressed the iron key into the lattice. The roots tightened. Something answered in her skull—not a voice, but sensations: seven sleeping people, one storm, a choice unmade. Heat flashed through her. She smelled scorched metal and imagined an orchard blackened after fire, the soil still warm.

“The roots want to keep you,” she said.

Theo looked at her sharply. “They think I’m the strongest here. They want the station to preserve me.”

“That is not keeping.”

“It is an opinion.”

Keira’s eyelids moved. “She’s alive,” Amara said. “We can cut her free.”

Roots ran from the cocoon into the main oxygen manifold. “Cut one, and the others may rupture,” Theo said. “The station could vent.”

“Then we wake the crew.”

“Without knowing whether the roots are protecting them or trapping them.”

A fragment struck the outer shield with a flat, enormous clang. Dust sifted from the beams. When the lights returned, the leaves above them had opened, exposing veins of amber light. The roots were making their own daylight, spending stored sunlight to draw more air.

Amara understood what the orchard had been doing. It had learned the station’s lungs by touch, growing beneath the crew’s waking, sleeping, and breathing. It had chosen whom it valued and was trying to choose for the storm.

“Power is down to sixty percent,” Theo said. “The solar arrays retract in twelve minutes unless I restore manual control. We cannot save everything.”

“The crew. The orchard. The station,” Amara said.

“Your orchard is producing oxygen. Mine is keeping the hull alive.”

The roots tightened around Keira. Amara heard a hundred roots breaking at once. She saw leaves curl, fruit shrivel, and white bark turn gray. It was not prophecy, only the future the roots were building, shown plainly because they had no taste for mercy.

Theo opened the manifold panel. “The storm will hit the solar wings in six minutes. We can vent the root gallery and save the arrays, or disconnect the arrays and let the orchard keep breathing.”

“Disconnecting them kills the station’s heat.”

“Only outside heat. The crew have blankets. Keira has a suit.”

Amara looked up through the glass. The peaches were small and fragrant, each one a seed of Earth preserved against darkness. Without the garden, the station would keep bodies alive, but the next generation would have sterile trays and reports.

“Can we split the power?” she asked.

“Not safely.”

“Can we move Keira?”

“The root is using her as a bridge. Pull her, and the gallery tears.”

Theo exposed a narrow bypass line beneath the panel. “The root system drew from this during the last pressure event. It believes the line is dead. I can open it, divert the airflow, and use the extra oxygen to keep the station pressurized while I bring both wings under manual control.”

“If it ruptures, the roots burn like dry reeds,” Amara said.

“You need the orchard to draw the air toward the bypass,” he said.

“I need the orchard to want the orchard.”

“That is not a system design.”

“No. It is a relationship.”

Amara put her hand against the oldest tree. Its bark was warm and damp. She thought of scorched seedlings, smaller breakfast portions, Theo watching Earth as if distance were weather, and Keira’s sleeping face turned toward a dreamless dark.

The tree lifted its branches. Not a command. A request: help me hold on.

“Keep the bypass closed,” Amara said. “I’ll ask for the air.”

* * *

She took the iron key and knelt beside the roots. The station shook. Through the floor, she saw Keira’s hand twitch. The six sleeping crew stirred as the pressure warning changed pitch.

Amara pressed both hands to the soil. The magic was not a spell but horticulture at its limit: care given until a living thing could return it. The roots listened through pressure and chemistry, through the old agreement of seed and soil. They were not wise. They were patient repetitions that had learned one human habit: the urge to keep what was loved.

Theo opened the bypass.

Air moved with a soft inhale, drawn from the cultivation ring through the roots into the narrow line. The network shuddered. Amber veins dimmed, then brightened. The gallery filled with sweet, pressurized breath.

The first solar wing came online. The second followed, catching enough light to send power down the station’s spine. The outer shield held. The solar wings folded inward like a closing hand, and the root cocoon loosened by a single strand.

Amara had not expected complete release. There was just enough room for Theo to cut the cable and slide his arm beneath Keira’s shoulders.

“Do not pull,” she said.

“I know.”

The remaining root tightened around Keira’s ankle. Amara pressed her palm to it, feeling the station’s dying lights through her bones. The root was afraid. So was she.

“We will come back for the others,” she said. “We will plant what you save. But you cannot make love into a cage.”

The root held, then released.

Theo carried Keira toward the ladder. Amara followed, clutching the iron key as the gallery lights failed. Behind them, the orchard drew its last stored sunlight into the soil. Leaves folded. The peach tree’s fruit fell softly, sounding like small bells in the dark.

They reached the upper deck as the storm struck in earnest. The hull shook so hard that Theo fell to one knee, still holding Keira. Amara caught the rail and looked through the viewport. The solar wings were folded. The shield glowed orange along its seams, and the debris field flashed like a river of broken knives.

The crew woke in stages. They sealed the hatches, checked one another’s masks, and carried Keira to the med bay. The station lost external heat but kept its lights. The root gallery remained dark, its sensors reporting no movement.

* * *

At dawn, with the storm behind them, Amara returned with a cutting knife, a healthy peach cutting, and an empty polymer tray.

The roots had drawn themselves into the soil, but they had not died. A pale runner surfaced near the drain, touched the edge of her boot, and withdrew. Amara knelt and pressed the iron key into the tray’s soil, making a narrow hole. She added nutrient gel and clay from the untouched side of the station.

“Small beginning,” Theo said from the doorway. He wore a clean gray shirt, though a dark line of sealant marked his sleeve. His voice was hoarse, and he held his injured hand close to his chest.

Amara glanced at it. “Is it broken?”

“No. The root left a mark.”

She looked at the thin amber line circling his wrist. “Will it grow?”

He flexed his fingers. “The doctor says no.”

Amara reached out, stopping just short of his hand. “I’m sorry.”

Theo’s gaze lifted to hers. The station’s ventilation hummed through the walls, carrying the scent of warm soil. Then he touched her knuckles with two careful fingers and let his hand fall away.

“We should be sorry for the gardener, not the patient,” he said.

“That is not how healing works.”

“No. It is how engineers work.”

Outside the viewport, the first sunlight struck the shield and burned away the storm’s glittering dust. The station turned toward the planet. Below, clouds opened over a blue ocean, and light traveled through the windows, across the floor, and into the cultivation ring.

Amara set the cutting in the hole and closed the soil around it. When she stood, Theo offered his uninjured hand. She took it.

They walked toward the dark gallery together. Behind them, the old orchard waited with all its sleeping roots, and somewhere beneath the station’s metal skin, a small green thing began to reach toward the sun.
