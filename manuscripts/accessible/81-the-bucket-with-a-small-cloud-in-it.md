# The Bucket With a Small Cloud in It

![Cover art: The Bucket With a Small Cloud in It](../art/accessible/81-the-bucket-with-a-small-cloud-in-it.webp)

Jonty carried a bucket.

He had carried it for as long as he could mind, so he could not say when he started. It was blue with a red handle, and there was a grey thing in the bottom of it, and the grey thing was a cloud.

“How big is it?” said his mam.

“About two fists side by side,” said Jonty.

The cloud sat in the bottom of the bucket and never went anywhere.

“What is in the bucket?” said his mam.

“Nothing,” said Jonty.

“You carried it in the snow.”

“It is the same bucket.”

* * *

“Is it going to rain?” said Jonty, on the first day of the hot week.

“No,” said his mam.

He took the bucket to the shop, and to the yard, and to the field where the hens were, and put it down beside him.

“Is it a rain cloud?” said the boy at the mill.

“It is not a rain cloud,” said Jonty. “A rain cloud is a thing that is about to do something. This one never has been.”

“It is a bucket with a red handle,” said the boy.

* * *

Bryn was eleven and thought she was too old for cloud talk. She had told her friends she had a brother with a bucket, and not what was in it.

On the Monday of the third week she came out to her pots and sat down on the step.

“They have cracked,” she said. “All of them. From the inside.”

Jonty looked at the pots. He looked at the sky. He looked in the bucket.

“The pond is dust,” said his mam. “There is a ring of brown round the edge, and boots and sticks at the bottom that are not ours.”

“The water comes on Thursday,” said his mam. “I will get two buckets.”

“One for the pots,” said Jonty.

“It is not for the pots. It is for drinking.”

On the Wednesday the sky went the colour of a coin, and the village stood in the road saying it was coming. It did not come.

Jonty went out into the yard and looked down in his bucket, because that was a thing he did. The cloud was exactly the same size it had always been: two fists side by side, in a blue bucket, in the driest week anybody could remember.

* * *

Bryn found it on the Thursday, while Jonty was in for his tea. The bucket was on the step, and she was looking down into it.

Jonty stopped in the doorway.

“That is a cloud.”

“Yes.”

“I am eleven,” she said, “and I do not talk about clouds. I told my friends I had a brother with a bucket and not what was in it. Stop looking like that.”

“I am not looking like anything.”

“You are.”

She got down until her eyes were level with the rim of the bucket. She put her hand flat on the ground beside it, the way you do to a dog, and took it away.

* * *

“Does it know we are here?” she said.

“It does not know anything,” said Jonty. “I have had it as long as I can mind and it has never said a word.”

“I am asking anyway,” she said. “Would it mind raining on my pots?”

“You would have to be told,” said Jonty.

“I am telling it,” said Bryn.

“You are not.”

“I am now,” said Bryn, to the bucket.

The sky went from grey to a colour like the inside of a shell. One long drop sat on the rim of the bucket, then went over the side and hit the cloud in the middle, and the cloud moved.

It rained for nineteen minutes.

* * *

They got the pots under the eaves, and the water came off the roof edge in a rope and went into the trays. Half the village came out and stood in it.

Then it stopped, the way it had started.

“The pots are all right,” said Bryn.

“They are,” said Jonty.

“You knew it would.”

“I did not know anything,” said Jonty.

Then the cloud was the same size. Jonty picked the bucket up when the street was quiet, and it sat in the bottom where it had always sat, not bigger and not smaller. It had rained and it was the same size as before. It was not a cloud saving it up. It was a cloud that was that cloud.

“I am not going to tell anybody about this,” said Bryn.

* * *

“We had beans in those pots in the autumn,” said Bryn, “and they were all right.” She told two people about them, and neither of them knew where the rain came from.

“You have carried that bucket since Easter,” said Bryn, on the last day of that summer. She took the handle off and gave it to him, and took the bucket on her other arm, and he took the handle, and they went down the lane together.

And from then on they carried the bucket between them, and the cloud in the bottom of it never got any bigger or any smaller, and it was still there in the winter.
