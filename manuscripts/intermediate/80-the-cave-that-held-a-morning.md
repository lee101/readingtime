# The Cave That Held a Morning

![Cover art: The Cave That Held a Morning](../art/intermediate/80-the-cave-that-held-a-morning.webp)

Delys was fourteen years old and she had been inside the cave since she was six, and in all that time it had never once been a cave when the fog came.

The fog was not the remarkable part. It came in off the sea most mornings at seven and filled the place like a bowl filling with milk, and anybody on the clifftop path could watch it sit out over the water. The remarkable part was what happened next, twenty paces in. At ten past seven the chamber stopped being a chamber and became a morning.

Hester Vane came up the cliff path at ten to seven with a tripod over one shoulder, a plate camera under her arm, and a canvas bag that clinked when she walked.

“You’ll be Delys.”

“You’ll be the one who says I’m lying.”

Hester set down the bag, took out a notebook and a stopwatch, and wrote the date and the temperature before she answered.

“My department calls it the draught hypothesis, and I am obliged to establish whether there is a draught. If there is one, the fog has an ordinary cause, and everyone can go home.”

“And if there isn’t?”

“Then I shall have to write something worse than a draught. The grant is three weeks and I intend to use all of it.”

She said three weeks. Delys did not correct her. Nobody ever corrected her. They said two weeks, or six months, or nothing at all, in the voice people keep for a girl who has told them a thing about the sea.

* * *

The crack was in the roof of the second chamber and it made a sound like a kettle coming to the boil, if you put your ear close to it. Fog came down out of it in a rope, and the rope hit the floor and spread.

“Ten past,” Delys said. “Some mornings earlier.”

Hester had a plate out of the changing bag before the rope had finished coming down.

The rope reached the walls and the walls stopped being walls. It was not light. Light you can point at. It was that the chamber became a room with an outside in it, and the outside was a field of wet grass, and a hedge at the end of the field, and past the hedge a low sun coming up over a horizon, and larks going up out of the grass in threes and fours, and dew standing on all of it, and the cold of outside, which is cleaner.

Delys had stopped breathing at the change, at six, since she was six years old. She breathed.

“Thirty seconds,” Hester said, to nobody. She was on her knees in the grass that was not grass, the tripod legs spread, and the sun was very low.

“Take it anyway.”

Hester took it. She took four in the fifty minutes, the last at ten to eight with the sun just clear of the hedge, and by that one her hands were shaking, and it was not the cold, because the cold was July coming in off a field.

Hester made her say where it was. A field, the fourth of August 1851, a hedge at the end of it, dew heavy, larks up. Hester wrote it all down, read the whole of it back, had her say it again exactly the same, wrote the second version down too, and then she had two that matched.

At ten to eight the sun went into the hedge, and the field stopped being a field, and the larks stopped.

“You have lost them,” Delys said.

“The birds,” Hester said, and then, “No. I have lost the light. The grass is still here.”

She was on her knees with her hand flat in the wet that was not wet. “The grass is warm.”

* * *

The plates came back three days later from a chemist in Truro, and they were not the black of a cave. They were grey, with a white crack in the top corner where the fog came in and a smear down one edge that was the changing bag, and if you looked at them long enough you could talk yourself into seeing a bird in the middle.

“The fog scatters,” Hester said. “Forty lux, half a second, wet floor, you get this.”

“Thirty-one mornings. I have a table.” She tapped the notebook without looking at it. “My table says the fog comes in at walking pace, the chamber has no source of light, and my meter reads four. All three are true. So I have photographed a room with a great deal of light in it, and that cannot be done.”

“The plate is right and I am not. I will not write that you are unreliable. I will write that I could not repeat it, which is a different sentence, and I cannot shorten it.”

“I have sixty-one accounts, every one consistent, and consistency is not evidence. It is a habit people have with each other.”

“It is a habit they have because it is true.”

“Seventy-four, now. The Penrys had a woman die in there in the spring.”

“Trev Penry,” said Hester, without looking up. “Barron, thirty-one. Carried in on a barrow at six, out at eight, died at ten in his own bed with the tide going out. His brother said it was the tide.” She wrote it down. “I wrote down everything. That is not the same as believing it.”

* * *

On the ninth morning she asked Delys to wait at the mouth.

“You are eleven feet inside my working area and you are breathing on the plate.”

“You want me outside. I want one fewer observer. You believe a thing, I do not, and there is no way for me to tell which of us is doing the seeing and which is doing the wanting. Stand at the mouth.”

Delys stood at the mouth for thirty-one more mornings, counting gulls, which did not help, and smelling the field come out past her, which did. Dew, cow-parsley, a gate shut all night. She stood in a cave doorway in the middle of a smell from a field a mile and a half away and three days off.

On the thirty-ninth morning Hester came out with her hair wet to the jaw.

“The chamber was a field at ten past seven and a chamber at ten to eight, and I was in the middle of it, and I saw a hedge.”

“You told me in May.” She closed the book. “This was the last grant of its kind. If I hand them a fog and a roof crack and a girl’s word, that is the end of it. Nobody pays for a girl’s word twice.”

She had not said it unkindly, which made it worse.

The February wedding they did by report. Meryn and Crow, the twelfth of February, in their good coats, standing in a field with the sun on them at eight in the morning in the worst month of the year. The plate of it hung in the village hall, and it was grey, and it had been grey for eleven years, and nobody in that hall had ever thought it a lie.

* * *

They wrote the report in the village hall with the plates laid out in a row under the football medals, and Delys read it over Hester’s shoulder because Hester said she might. Then Hester read the findings out for the room.

“Entered and observed on thirty-nine mornings between the eleventh of May and the nineteenth of June. On thirty-one the fog arrived through the roof crack at seven minutes past seven, and on eight at six. The effect described by local witnesses was observed by the surveyor on no occasion. Attempts to record it photographically produced exposures consistent with an unlit chamber. The account is not reproducible.”

“What does it say about the grass?” Delys said.

“It does not mention it.”

“You wrote down the dew. You wrote it on the thirty-ninth.”

“That was my notebook. If I put a hedge in there, a man in Utrecht will spend a year hunting my hedge and will write that I have described a wall fungus.”

At half past eight, with the medals behind her and her fingers white round the pen, Hester cut the whole of it to four pages.

Tam Tregorran came in with haddock in a newspaper and put it on the table by her, the way he had done for nineteen years.

“It is finished.”

Tam looked at the four pages, and the grey plates in their row, and the frame on the wall with Meryn and Crow in it.

“I have watched you give that woman a hard six weeks,” he said. “Tell me one thing and I will go home. What would you publish it as?”

She opened her mouth, and then said nothing for long enough that the fire shifted.

“That is not a fair question,” Hester said at last.

“No,” said Tam. “It is the only one worth asking.”

He took his fish and went out into the rain, and Hester sat with an unfinished sentence on the page and did not finish it, and did not tell Delys what she had thought of.

* * *

The paper came out that autumn on pages nine to twelve of a journal of coastal geology, and it is the shortest thing in four years of that journal. It has been cited twice, in the footnotes of a Dutch paper on chalk caves, and both times the same: “Vane, V., who reports a phenomenon this reader has been unable to confirm.”

The village was delighted and did not care. Nan Coombes read the abstract aloud and said the lady had come all this way and could not find what Delys had found at six years old, and everybody laughed, and then they went home. Two hundred years of seven o’clock does not need a footnote. It needs a girl and a cave and a barrow.

Delys had a place at a school in Truro from September and had told nobody. Hester went on the packet on the Thursday with the bag and no tripod, and Delys walked her to the bus stop, and neither of them said the thing, and the sea did all of it, coming in and going out for four hundred feet of coast.

Tam Tregorran had one more thing, and he said it on the Friday. He had been in the cave twice in fifty years and had never told anybody, including a magistrate, once, in a worse coat.

“Forty years I thought it was a door. It is not a door. It is a room. A room belongs to whoever is in it, and that is the whole of it, and you can write that on the gate.”

* * *

She left on the noon bus on the last Friday in August, which was not till ten, which gave the morning to the cave. She went down the path at twenty to seven with a packed lunch and no coat, because the only weather in that county is what you brought with you.

Hester Vane was standing in the mouth with the bag at her feet and the notebook under her arm and no tripod, because a tripod needs three hands and one flat floor, and there was neither.

The tide was out. There were gulls on the sand. Somebody had chalked three marks on the rock at the mouth in June and the sea had taken two of them back.

Neither of them said anything about it.

At ten past seven the rope came down out of the roof and hit the floor and spread, and the walls stopped being walls, and there was a field with a hedge at the end of it and the sun coming up over a horizon, and dew on all of it, and larks going up in threes and fours.

They took their boots off, because there was nowhere in a field like that to put wet boots, and they sat down in the grass side by side and let the sun come over the hedge onto the front of them, in a field of imaginary sun inside a real cave in Cornwall.
