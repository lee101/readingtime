# Bellweather Deep

![Cover art: Bellweather Deep](../art/intermediate/07-bellweather-deep.webp)

At thirty-five, Lio Maren moved underwater as other men moved through crowded rooms: with economy, every wasted gesture belonging to somebody else. He worked the drowned terraces of Sant Vey in a patched charcoal coat, copper rebreather, and thigh-sheathed knife. Two mute bells hung at his belt.

People bought the bells because everyone said the city’s great bells had drowned the monsters in its streets. Lio had salvaged enough fragments to know the saying was incomplete.

A great bell did not drown anything by itself. Eight bells, each tuned with a different note, made a ward. A creature crossing that completed circle was forced to hear the sound of its own death. Water remembered the last breath belonging to that body, and the body remembered no shape afterward. One loose note was worse than silence. It left a road.

The rules were iron wrapped in wool, pinned beneath Lio’s collar: never touch a working bell bare-handed. Never speak inside a ward whose upper note was missing. And when a voice came through the water, never answer.

Lio had broken the first two rules. He was down to one.

He was lifting a cargo net from the ruined apothecary when the dead diving bell surfaced.

It rose nose-first, trailing kelp and bubbles. No winch turned it. Twenty years ago, the Cradle had gone down with the Fishmonger’s Stair and never returned. Lio knew its black copper flank, blunt horns, and saint’s eye above the hatch.

The Cradle hung upside down in the surge, then rolled upright. Its hatch opened from inside.

A woman climbed through, water pouring from her dark green dress. She had their mother’s square jaw and their father’s deliberate hands. Her shoulder-length hair was wet, though when the flood came she had been eleven.

She was thirty-one now.

She stood with one hand braced against the bell, swaying like a mast in a gale. Then she looked at Lio.

“Lio,” she said.

He had spent twenty years telling himself Mara was not dead, but never letting that belief possess him. Now it looked at him from a stranger’s adult face.

He cut the net free. The Cradle smelled of hot iron and winter mud. Mara walked into the ruins as if the streets had merely closed for noon. Every turn was correct.

At the surface, she wrapped herself in oilcloth while Lio fed a coal stove. She used the broth to warm her palms, counting every sound: drip, hinge, kettle, drip.

“You were here all night,” he said.

“There is no other time here.”

The words seemed delirious until Mara touched the stovepipe. A clear note rose from the iron. A drowned bell answered below, half a tone flat.

“It is using my count,” Mara said. “Every time I remember a sound, it moves the bells.”

“What is it?”

She looked down the street, toward the cathedral’s shattered spire. “The city.”

* * *

The first Drowner came before dawn.

Standing water rose around a market table, gathered into a woman’s outline, and borrowed their mother’s face. Seaweed hung beneath the jaw.

Mara froze.

The Drowner wore a wedding dress sodden to the skin. Lio had seen its dead remains tangled in the choir loft. Now it stepped with the loose-jointed grace of something remembering knees.

“The upper note is missing,” he said.

He struck the belt bell with his sheathed knife. Three notes rang cleanly. The last required a full lung and a copper tongue.

The Drowner crossed the incomplete circle.

Mara began to hum.

Her worn voice carried the lullaby she had taught him. The Drowner’s borrowed face softened, its black dress filling with clear water.

Lio felt the tune in his teeth. It was wrong. Mara’s first word sat where the final note belonged.

“Stop.”

She could not stop. The tune pulled from her like rope over a winch.

Lio caught her jaw in his wool-wrapped hand and shut her mouth. Then he gave the Drowner what it had come for.

He struck the last note against the bell’s own bronze shell, using the dull clapper instead of the missing tongue. The sound was graceless but complete. Eight notes struck the Drowner at once.

For an instant it stood serene. Then the wedding dress collapsed into foam, and the remaining notes scoured the street.

Mara bent over the blanket, gasping. Red crescents marked her cheeks.

“Who taught you that?” he asked.

“I did. Before the flood.”

Below them, an eighth bell answered from the cathedral. Its note was flat. The city had retuned the ward to let the Drowner through.

* * *

They took the Cradle down the following evening. Its pipes still worked, though the divers who had entered twenty years earlier had not. Lio packed rebreathers, wool, a bone clapper, and a signal pipe connected to the harbor’s surface bell.

Mara stood between the air pots, pressing two fingers against the lead pipe. “The first bell is under the fish market. Left of the statue.”

The fish market lay in the opposite direction. The statue was the gilded king who had sold false storm permits before the disaster.

The Cradle sank.

Green turned gray. Windows appeared, rooms open to the dark, beds hovering beneath chandeliers. The deeper Lio descended, the less like ruins it seemed. A yellow chair waited behind a butcher’s porch.

“There,” Mara said.

The Cradle turned left before Lio touched the helm.

She guided them through streets that had changed twice since the flood, then remembered a house where no house had stood. The city was not remembering. It was being remembered, one fact at a time.

At the fish market, eight small bells hung upside down in an empty octagon. Seven were silent. The eighth was ringing so deeply that the water around it shone in concentric hoops.

Lio cut the Cradle free and descended alone.

A vast shape moved behind the stalls, the market’s tiled ribs showing through its body. The bells were fixed to its bones, their conflicting notes keeping it folded thin.

Lio touched the ringing bell through wool. Pain raced up his arm. He saw the full pattern: every note bent inward, not outward.

The wards had been retuned into a mouth.

He struck the silent bells in order. The tiled shape thinned. When the last note fell, the market wall became transparent, revealing a thousand drowned faces with mouths open in one unfinished syllable.

Mara’s voice came from all of them.

“Come down,” they said.

He did not answer. He returned to the Cradle and showed her the bells.

“The city is the Drowner now.”

“No. The city is what the Drowner lives inside.” Her gaze went distant, counting. “The bells made it. They remembered everyone who died speaking through them. Fear, orders, prayers. When the wards began to fail, the city gathered those voices. It needs a living voice to tune them again.”

“You.”

She looked at her wet hands. “I was the last child it heard answer.”

* * *

At the cathedral, the great bell hung upside down in a shaft of trapped air. Its clapper was gone. Every smaller bell could be heard from here, though none could be struck.

Voices filled the empty shaft: men, women, children, each fragment in a different accent. In the middle, Mara said Lio, twenty years younger and afraid. He kept his mouth shut.

The city spoke with Mara’s adult voice. “Finish the eight notes, and the streets will keep their dead safe. The city will have a voice again.”

Behind the bell, a black outline rose higher than the cathedral, carrying streets in its sleeves. Doors burst open. Bells answered in frightened, wandering notes.

Lio’s portable octaves could not cross that breadth. One ward would be torn apart before the eighth strike. The rule still made him helpless at the scale of a city.

Mara climbed onto the rim. The bronze hummed beneath her knees, and every distant bell found its pitch at once.

She began to sing. The shape leaned toward her with the patience of a parent over a child’s drawing.

“It is feeding,” Lio shouted.

“She said she would keep the dead safe.”

“Mara, you are the note. If you finish, it knows where every living voice is.”

His wool-covered hand met her cold shoulder, and the bell showed him the flood night. Eleven-year-old Mara trapped in a stairwell. Fifteen-year-old Lio on a rooftop. He had called her name, and she had answered, “Here!” The answer traveled through the bronze and was kept.

For twenty years, the city used her breath to turn that answer into every tone. It could not release her while her voice remained its last living sound. Another answer would turn the bells toward the path it traveled.

Not home. Out.

The signal pipe beside the Cradle’s hatch connected it to the harbor’s surface bell. His answer would carry the dead choir out along the pipe, beyond the water.

Windows opened in the shape’s face. His father spoke from one. A school friend called from another. Their mother asked him to come home.

Mara clawed at his coat, but the singing held her fast. The city offered her voice: “Here, Lio. Here.”

He had spent twenty years refusing that answer.

“Mara, I hear you. Come home,” Lio said into the brass. He answered his sister’s buried voice, not the city’s invitation, and the clear noon note traveled up the pipe to the harbor bell.

Every bell in Sant Vey turned toward it. The black shape followed its echo toward the quay, shedding streets like burning banners.

Lio pulled Mara into the Cradle and drove the helm down, racing the departing shape. Her breath came in shallow, deliberate pulls. The choir’s last voice faded through the hatch above them.

The wards rang true.

* * *

Lio woke on the quay with wool around his hands and Mara asleep beneath his coat. Salvagers had cut them free. The harbor reflected one clear evening star.

He tried to tell the waiting warden what had happened. His chest moved, but no sound passed his lips.

Mara woke in the sunlight and reached for his scarred hand. “Did I come home?”

He pointed to his throat, then to the drowned terraces. She touched his cheek and mouthed, “Brother.”

Later he learned that she had not aged a day while the bells held her. Twenty years occupied the space between one answer and the next. She remembered the flood, isolated hours, and the number eight in every direction.

She remembered his face. She spoke to him for hours, filling the room with the voice the city had used as a door. On paper, he answered: I am Lio Maren. I am your brother. I brought you home.

Outside, the old bells answered across the flooded city. Their wards held through the night over water and broken roofs. People would return to salvage timber, never knowing Mara’s voice had anchored them.

Mara read his last note twice. Then she reached across the table, took his hand, and patiently began to teach him a new language they could share without being heard by anything else.
