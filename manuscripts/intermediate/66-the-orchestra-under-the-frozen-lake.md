# The Orchestra Under the Frozen Lake

![Cover art: The Orchestra Under the Frozen Lake](../art/intermediate/66-the-orchestra-under-the-frozen-lake.webp)

Nobody can tell you what the sound under Lake Vell was, because it was always there.

It was not music as far as the surface was concerned. It was a pressure in the ice, more felt than heard: a hum in the skate blade, a tremble in the pail when you drew water, a bad tooth in a cold head. The old people called it the tuning.

On the fourteenth of March it stopped at ten past four in the afternoon, in the middle of a bar.

Nobody saw anything happen. Tamsin Ferrow was coming off the ice with her case under her arm when the tuning went out of the world, and the silence after it was so complete that a gull over the harbour sounded rude.

She was thirteen. She played the cello, and she played it badly in front of anybody, and she had known since she was nine that she could not do the other thing. So she played to the ice, alone, in the dark hour when the whole lake was indoors.

“That is the weather,” her grandmother said, when Tamsin got to the door.

“It is not the weather. Weather is a thing that stops.”

Her grandmother sat down by the stove and put her boots up, which she was not supposed to do. “Then somebody will want to open it,” she said.

* * *

By the end of the month Reeve Hallyard had called a meeting in the schoolroom with the stove going and the village in it, and put three motions to the room.

The first was that the lake be opened. The pike had gone down deep and the nets came up empty, and two families had sold their boats to men from the Marrow, who were still arguing about the price.

The second was that the hall be used. Hallyard had a man up from the Marrow to sound it, and the man came back with one number: nine feet of ice over the hall, the deepest water on the lake, and the hall dry down there and forty degrees warmer than the top. He said it would make a fine parish room, a schoolroom, a cold store for anybody’s turnips.

“And the tuning?” said a man at the back of the schoolroom. “What is the tuning doing about it?”

“That,” said Hallyard, “is the question you should have asked me in November, and nobody did, and we shall take it up at the third motion.”

The third motion was that nothing be touched at all. Granny Sedge moved it, it got four hands, and she said she expected that and would like a hearing anyway.

She got one. She was born on this shore and had skated on this ice as a girl, at night, in a coat two sizes too large, with her grandmother holding her scarf. You could feel the tuning through the blades and think the ice was coming up. It was not coming up. It was only there.

* * *

The first line of frost came on the second of April, out by the deep hole, the same way every night, and by the eighth it was a sentence.

WE ARE OWED.

Nobody could get near it. The frost came up thin and black underneath the letters, and where the writing was the ice would take a man’s weight without a sound, and men who walked to the edge of it stopped and turned around.

Granny Sedge walked right up to it. She was the only one who could. She stood with her toes on the last letter and read it out twice for the schoolroom, and after that it came the same every night, in the same careful hand, and it always finished the same way: the box is under the desk. the desk is behind the stand.

“Who writes a thing like that on a floor?” said Tamsin.

“Whatever is in the hall,” said Granny Sedge. “And we shall find out whether it can be asked, and we shall ask it civilly, and if it cannot be asked then we shall dig.”

“There is a desk,” Tamsin said.

“There is a desk,” said Granny Sedge. “There has been a desk under that stand since before the ice, and a box under the desk, and the last man to see the box was my grandmother’s brother and he told me it was not worth the trip.”

* * *

Nine feet of ice takes a day to cut and a week to regret.

They got the men from the reeds, the two Marrow men with their sounding gear, and a boy called Pike who was too pleased about it all, and a smith’s boy called Corry with the bad chest, who stayed on the surface and wound the rope. They cut in shifts from eleven o’clock, and by the second evening there was a hole the size of a punt with a ladder in it, and a wind came up out of the water that took the skin off your face.

Tamsin went down on the fourth day.

Then the voice came up out of the hole.

It was not a voice that shouted, because shouting would have wasted paper. It arrived the way a thing arrives when it is being read aloud two floors below you and a hundred feet away.

“Stop the cutting,” it said. “The last measure is not for the surface.”

Pike put his head over the edge and shouted down, “Can you play us something else?”

“No,” said the conductor. “I have no opinions. I am only what is written on me, and there is no other page.”

Nobody up top said anything for a while. The smith’s boy kept winding the rope, because stopping was not part of the job.

“Then stop asking,” Granny Sedge said to Tamsin. “Put your case down there. Be a musician today and be a lawyer tomorrow, and do not try at once to be both in front of them.”

* * *

The hall was a room built for sound. It was low and long, with a brick floor and no mortar in the courses, and four ranks of empty seats.

“Corry,” Tamsin called up the hole. “How deep are you?”

“Nine feet of ice and a bit of water over a floor, so mind how you go.”

There were instruments. A double bass the size of a door standing on end. A harp with forty-seven strings. Four timpani with skins like drumheads. A piano with the lid up and the keys white as teeth.

“They have players,” she shouted down.

“No,” said the conductor, from a low platform at the front. “They have themselves.”

He was a roll of manuscript paper nine feet long, bound with a bootlace, with a face on the outside of the roll, standing with four inches of himself already unrolled, the way a man waits with his hat in his hands. He never once turned round.

The violinist was made of a violin, hollowed out and left, and it had a boy’s mouth and a boy’s chin and no body at all, and the bow went through where the right arm would have been. The trumpeter was dented on the left side, which is where a trumpet gets dented. The cellists were three, and the third one, at the end of the second rank, was made of wood the exact colour of Tamsin’s own instrument, and it was a head taller than the others, and it was a bad copy of her own, down to the notch in the side.

Then he played.

The cello got in before Tamsin could decide anything about it. It was her own playing, in a room built for it, at the speed she had been nagged at since she were nine, and it was the best it had ever been, because there was nobody listening. She played all forty-one measures. Corry said afterwards that he had lost the count twice and had to start again, and that the last measure took a very long time.

When it stopped, the conductor unrolled four more inches of himself.

“The last measure is not begun,” he said. “The box is under the desk.”

Under the stand at the end of the third rank was the desk, and behind it a small deal box with a brass clasp, and inside one folded sheet, damp along the folds, brown at the corners, and four hundred years of damp through the middle of it.

She read it twice and read it out loud to the ladder, and Corry wrote it down on the back of his hand, because there was nothing else to write it on and he was nineteen.

Received of the town of Vell, for one winter’s music, to be worked off against that winter.

Valentine Ambrose, fourth of January, 1846.

And at the foot, in a different ink, pressed hard enough to emboss the sheet above it: The season ends with its last measure. That measure is not begun.

* * *

There had been nothing to burn and nowhere to sit and a great deal of debt, Hallyard said, and the people of Vell gave what they had, which was not money.

The list was made out on the back of the receipt, because the receipt was the only paper to hand, and went up on the church door with a pin and a piece of blue ribbon for the rain. Everyone in the village wrote something on it. The clerk, who was nineteen and enthusiastic, ruled a column in the church book and wrote the total at the foot, and the total was one pound, two shillings and something, and the something was a bad halfpenny nobody would give up.

“You cannot put a bad halfpenny in a book,” said Hallyard, who had the book. “You can put the halfpenny in. It is a coin. It is a poor one, and it is a coin, and it will do.”

The Marrow men put in a month of wages each, which came to more than everything else together. The smith gave a pair of new tongs, worth eightpence and honestly valued at nothing. The two Sealey boys gave nine days of weeding between them, entered as work. Widow Quilling gave her wedding ring, which nobody had asked her for, and the clerk had to borrow scales to weigh it and wrote the weight down as though that settled anything.

Granny Sedge gave her grandmother’s skates. They were worth nothing at all. She was extremely pleased with herself, and wrote them on the line in full, with the maker’s name on the sole.

Tamsin gave the cellists’ rosin, which she had been saving for two years and would have cried about.

The ring, the tongs and the bad halfpenny went down the hole in a bread tin on a rope, and the boy on the ladder called down that the box was open and the sheet was in, and then there was nothing for a long time, and then the four of them came up the ladder, all of them perfectly dry.

* * *

Tamsin Ferrow conducted the closing eight bars of the season on the fourth of April.

She was bad at it. Her left hand did the beat for the first four bars and then thought better of it and did something else, and there was nobody in the hall to tell her, because there were four ranks of empty seats. The bad cellist up the row followed her faithfully, which made it worse and at the same time made it a team effort.

The last bar has one note in it and the note is a low D, and the hall had been holding that note for a hundred and eighty years without being asked to, and it came up through nine feet of ice and out along the shore far enough to be heard at the harbour, where two men loading a cart stopped with a rope in their hands.

Then the ice closed over the hall in one night, with a noise like a door shutting a long way off, and there is one hole in Lake Vell that does not freeze.

Granny Sedge was the last one out on the ice that spring. She went out to it in the skates that were not hers and stood with her toes on the edge.

“Are we not going to put a hole in it?” said Corry.

“We leave that one,” said Granny Sedge, and would not give a reason, and nobody ever found one that made better sense.

The pike that used to be down deep have never been caught in Lake Vell since. The fishing over that hole is better than anybody has a right to expect, and the Marrow men put a marker stake beside it in 1889, and the stake is still standing, and nobody has ever dug there.
