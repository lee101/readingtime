# The Queen of the Willow Marsh

![Cover art: The Queen of the Willow Marsh](../art/intermediate/89-the-queen-of-the-willow-marsh.webp)

Ruth put her boots on the kitchen table to get the mud off, and her mother told her not to.

“You’ll bring the whole marsh in with you,” her mother said, not looking up from the accounts.

“The marsh does not come in,” Ruth said. “The mud does.”

She had walked this path since she was six. Two hundred yards of mown strip, then the first willow, then the ground that everybody on both sides of the fence called worthless and her grandmother had called by its own name, Achanar, which meant the place that holds. The farm ditch ran along the boundary. The ditch was nine years old and full of good water, and the good water came from the marsh, and Ruth could do that arithmetic better than her father could.

The Queen’s house was not a house. It was a boat turned over with a chimney pushed through it, tarred black, a bench outside, a stovepipe that leaked whenever it rained.

The Queen was on the bench. She was sixty if she was a day, and she had been Queen since she was nine years old, which was four hundred years, and the marsh had never once asked her to prove it.

“You’ve come about the ditch,” the Queen said.

“I’ve come about the ditch,” Ruth said.

The Queen laughed, a short sound with a duck in it.

“Sit, then. I’ll tell you what the ditch has done, and you can tell me what your grandfather did about it.”

What the ditch had done was easy to see and hard to say. Where the marsh used to stand chest-deep in the wettest weeks it stood in nine inches of brown with a ring of dead alder round it, and a badger sett that had been there before Ruth was born had been filled from above and drowned. She had dug the last of it out herself with a spade that belonged to the Queen.

“You did not object,” Ruth said.

“I have never objected to anything,” the Queen said. “I will hold a thing as long as it wants holding. That is the job. A Queen who objects is a Queen who is arguing, and the moment you argue with water you have lost your grip on it, and nobody gave me the grip. I have kept it by being sensible.”

Ruth thought about her father, and how he had never once said the word marsh. He said low ground. He said the back forty. He had a way of naming a thing that made the naming look like his own good sense, and he had used it every day for eleven years.

“They will take the low ground out in a fortnight,” she said.

“Yes,” said the Queen. “They are patient, and they are not in a hurry, and nobody in the whole of it is doing anything wrong. Come and look at the cut with me. Bring your boots.”

* * *

Ruth’s grandfather had come to the bench in the March of that year, nine years back, with his cap in his hands and a bad cough.

The ditch was cut in nine days by two men and a hired machine, and it went in a straight line because the ground was easy, and the Queen walked down to the top end of it every evening for a month to watch it fill. That was the whole of her objection. Ruth’s grandfather had stood beside her on the last of those evenings and said nothing, because he had understood that she was not asking him to stop, and that she was not going to say it twice.

“You did not take it from me,” the Queen said. “You asked me for a ditch, because the ground was wet and you had a bad year, and I said yes, because I have said yes for three hundred and seventy-five years and it is the one thing I am for.”

Her grandfather had put his cap on his knee where she could see it and not on his head, and had waited out the whole silence, and then had said the sentence Ruth had heard in her head every day since.

“I hope you know what I’m sorry for,” he had said.

The Queen had looked at the water coming down his new ditch, brown for the first week and then clear, and she had said, “So do I,” and had meant it about exactly one of the two of them.

Eleven years now. Ruth had been seven when the signing happened, old enough to remember the shape of the room and nothing in it, and the old Queen had died in a room with the shutters open. Ruth remembered the smell of it, hot linen and pear drops, and her father standing at the counter in his good shirt with a paper cup he never drank from.

Two years later he had signed. He had gone into the town with the papers in an envelope and come back with them signed, and put them in the drawer with the fuses, and said nothing, and the ditch was cut in the October. Ruth watched the water go down the new cut all winter and said nothing either, because she was seven, and because it was her father.

* * *

Saye had been at the marsh four days, and he had got his boots wet to the knee and ruined one corner of his folder. He was thirty-four, he had a degree in water engineering, and he had never once been late, and both of these things were going to matter.

The folder was eleven centimetres thick when shut. Section one was the catchment. Section two was the hydrology. Section three was the design. Section four was the statutory correspondence. Section five was the land take and the compensation schedule, with Ruth’s surname in it four times.

Every page in it was correct. Saye checked his work twice, and then a third time out of a habit he had never examined.

“The channel is doing what it was designed to do,” he said. He and Ruth were standing on the bank of a cut that had not existed in April and was now four feet deep, ruler-straight, its sides raw brown, machine tracks still printed in the spoil. “I know what it looks like from your side of the fence. Nothing here is an accident. If it looks like it has gone wrong, that is a coincidence, and I would like that on the record.”

“You write it in the folder,” Ruth said.

“Everything worth having goes in the folder.”

That was true, and she hated it, and she looked down the straight cut anyway. The water had found the low place at the head of it and was sliding in, unhurried, on a slope you could push a wheelbarrow up. It did not look like a machine. It looked like something being persuaded.

“Tell me about the compensation,” Ruth said.

“Tell you about it at the meeting. Everyone gets told at the same time, that is the whole point of a meeting.” Saye shifted his weight. “Your father has asked me three times whether the ditch could be deepened. It cannot. The invert is set. I have written that down for him, in a letter he has not opened, because he does not want the answer to be no.”

* * *

The Queen let her do it, which was the Queen’s way of giving permission without ever being asked.

The old woman waded out into the cut up to the shins, in a coat that was no coat at all, and stood there with the water moving around her legs. She put her hand flat on the cut side and left it there.

“Do you know what water is?” she said. “Everybody thinks it is a thing that goes where it wants. It is not. It is a thing that agrees. It will hold for a person it has reason to hold for, and it has held for me since I was nine because I have been reasonable, Ruth. Four hundred years of reasonable. I have never once taken water off a man who needed it. I have never once thrown a man out of my marsh. I have been the good neighbour for longer than there have been roads.”

“And it is not anymore,” Ruth said.

“It has not been this year. It is not that I have stopped.” The Queen lifted her hand. The side of the cut was damp to the elbow. “This ground was never carrying much. Silt, peat, a foot of it, and then the clay. The water is at the clay now. It is not being taken out of the marsh. It is being found out of the marsh.”

* * *

The meeting was on a Thursday, in a room with bad ventilation above the shop. Twenty-one people, and Saye counted them out of politeness. Mr Petrie from the office had a laptop and a tie bought for a different job, and he had read the file, all of it, and he had come to the part that concerned him.

“Right,” he said. “Section five. Land take and compensation. The offer to the Ruthven farm is ready and it is the standard schedule, and the offer to the Cobbett land is the standard schedule, and we are looking at a hundred and forty hectares of grazing and one working farm, and everyone in this room is owed money.”

“We are,” Ruth said, from the back.

Mr Petrie nodded at her as though she were a chair that had spoken. “The question in front of me is not whether anybody objects. The question is whether anybody objects, and to what, in a form I can put in a paragraph.”

Nobody objected for a while. A man at the back said his gate was standing in a ditch that was a ditch in his grandmother’s time. Somebody’s phone went off. The room was warm and smelled of coats and wet coats.

Then the Queen stood up, and everyone in the room turned around, because she was standing up in a room where nobody had expected her to be standing up.

“You want the paragraph,” the Queen said. “Here is the paragraph, and I will give it to you once.”

“Your Majesty, if this is a matter of—”

“A marsh is not drained, Ruth,” the Queen said. “A marsh is moved. Nobody has asked where.”

She sat down. That was the whole of her contribution, and she folded her hands in her lap like a woman at a bus stop.

Mr Petrie put his pen down. “I can’t put that in a paragraph.”

“No,” the Queen said. “You cannot. That is why I have said it out loud.”

* * *

Saye drove back to the cottage that night with the heater on full and the folder on the passenger seat, and he did not put a tape in, and he drove eleven miles before he noticed.

The compensation schedule ran to nine pages. He knew every line of it and could have sworn to it in front of anybody, in any room, in any weather.

Five pages of it was habitat. A wet woodland, a reedbed, two retention basins, eleven hectares of new marsh, four ponds and a hundred and forty metres of hedge, all of it to be made on the drained ground with a five-year aftercare clause, because the authority had a duty to replace the wetland it had removed, and the file said so in eleven places.

The file was correct. That was the whole difficulty. The file had never once said where the water was going.

* * *

They met on the new section in the March. The water was high and slow at the sides and Ruth was up to the knee in the bypass she had cut with her father and a borrowed machine, and she was the first to see what he did not expect to see.

Reeds. Across nine metres of new channel, in water that had arrived in winter and never left, a raft of dead reed stems stood up out of the current like a fence. Two seasons, a bad harvest and a flood, and they had come in on their own.

Saye put his hand on the section wall and looked at them for a long time.

“The file says this ground becomes habitat,” he said.

“Yes.”

“The file does not say it happens in four years because the ground was good underneath and nobody put anything on top of it.”

* * *

They put the payment to Ruthven Farm in on the first of April, and the cheque was made out to her mother, and her mother made the mistake of saying it was a good offer at the table.

The Queen was on the bench. Ruth watched her mother’s face when the old woman laughed, that short door-closing sound. She was not being polite. She was amused because somebody had finally done the thing she had been waiting four hundred years for, and had done it by accident, out of a schedule, on a Tuesday.

Saye had signed the discharge authorisation. He had put his name on the line in section five, in the last line, above the word of his office, with the date beside it.

“It says the water discharges to the tidal reach of the lower channel,” he told them, “and the lower channel is a marsh, and the marsh is tidal, and it is the correct word, and for once the file is exactly as good as the water.”

Then she stood up off the bench, which she had not done in front of anybody since the Thursday in the shop.

“I have been reasonable for four hundred years,” the Queen said, “and look where it got us.”

Ruth took a step towards her. Saye took a step towards the section, out of pure habit.

Nobody else did anything at all.

The Queen looked at the sea, and then at the water going out across the flats toward the tidal channel, and it was going slowly, and it was going a very long way, and for the first time in four hundred years she did not stop at the end of her sentence.
