# The Library Where the Books Grew on the Trees

![Cover art: The Library Where the Books Grew on the Trees](../art/intermediate/88-the-library-where-the-books-grew-on-the-trees.webp)

Highmere library was a wood, and the books grew on the trees, and the staff were pruners, and the first thing Enid learned was that the people who came through the gate were not staff.

She had been told on the bus that the work was pruning. She had not been told what pruning meant, and nobody corrected her for it, and by the second morning in the wood she had worked out that pruning was not a word anybody used about fruit.

The trees were small, from knee height to the top of a tall man, and they were planted in rows nine feet apart, and the books stood out from the branches on short stalks like pears that had been told what to be. A green one was soft and smelled of apples. A ripe one had hard boards and made a flat sound when you knocked it with a knuckle.

“Any questions,” said Mr Absalom, who was sixty and had been doing it for forty years and had a thumb worn flat on the pad of it, “ask them before you pick anything up, not after.”

“Where do the books come from?”

“They are the tree.” He turned round a book with the flat of his hand so she could see the stalk. “Seed, then shoot, then the same book again. The tree has it in it. If you take one off and put it in the bank, the tree takes it back, and in a year or three it will do it again, once, for anybody who asks. Not the same copy. The same book.”

“Only once?”

“Once for a lifetime, which for a tree is a great deal more than once for you.” He put a hand on the slate by the gate. “A read book is finished. We cut it off and it goes in the bag and it goes in the Bank on the rack, and the tree says thank you. That is the whole arrangement. It works, and it has worked since before I came.”

* * *

The bag was canvas and it came up to her chest when it was full. The shears were long-handled with a hooked blade, and Absalom showed her how to hold them so the spring did the work, and then he stood three feet away and said nothing at all, and waited.

Enid took hold of a ripe book. It was a small brown one, about the size of a hymn book, and it came away from the branch with a sound like a boot on a stair, and it was in her hand, and it was the weight of a small brown object and nothing else, and she could not put it in the bag.

She put it down on the grass. She walked to the toolshed, which was four feet deep and smelled of oil, and she was sick behind it, and then she sat down on the log with her back against the boards and shook for a while.

When she came back Absalom had cut four trees, and he was holding her book out to her by the stalk.

“Brown ones are not the ones to start on,” he said. “Start on the ones nobody minds.”

* * *

The boy came in at ten past three and went to the same tree every day.

Enid noticed on her third week because she was tallying, and a boy who reads one page for six months is a hole in your afternoon you cannot stop counting. He was about eleven. He took his coat off and folded it over the arm of the bench. He stood in front of one particular tree in the fourth row. He opened one particular book. He read for about forty minutes. Then he shut it, and put his hands in his pockets, and went out through the gate without looking at a single other thing in the wood.

The book was blue. She could see the spine from where she was standing at the gate with the tally board.

“Ned,” said Absalom, when she asked.

“That is his name.”

“Ned Hollis, and that is his sister’s book, and it is not his sister’s any more, and he has been standing in front of that tree since the Tuesday before the clocks went back.” Absalom went on with his cutting. “He has never asked for it and he is not going to this year.”

“Why not?”

“Because he is not ready, and I am not going to be the man who decides what that looks like.”

* * *

“What is the hard part,” Enid said, “is not the cutting. The hard part is that everybody decides the person asking is in no state to know what they want, because otherwise they would have asked, and so nothing gets asked for, and then the wood has no reason to do anything at all, and in ten years the third row is going to be a field of nothing with one boy standing in it.”

Absalom put the shears down in the grass.

“A book that has been read is finished, and the wood is planted to be finished, and the whole arrangement stands up because somebody is willing to put the good ones in the bag,” he said. “I have never once wanted to, not in forty years, and I have done it every week of every year for forty years, and I have hated every minute of it, and I have got the hands for it, so nobody has ever had to find out whether I can.”

Enid looked at the brown book in the bag, which was still there, still unbagged, sitting in the canvas like an egg.

Then she cut the rest of it in under a minute, and it made a flat noise, and it went in.

* * *

The far side of the wood was not on any of the maps in the hut, because the far side was not a thing the public was shown. There was a wire fence and a gate with a padlock on it, and the wood on the far side was older and stood closer together and grew up into the light instead of out into it.

She went over on the Sunday, with her father’s binoculars and a bad idea.

There were eleven of the book on the far side, blue spines, in a row along one bank, and they were all the same book, and they were all at a different size. The first was full grown. The second was three quarters. The last was the width of a thumbnail and a fortnight old, green, and still smelling of apples.

Enid sat down in the leaf litter with the binoculars in her lap for a long time.

They were not part of the rotation. She checked the board when she got back. The far side was not on the board. And she went through the Bank ledger, which records the tree, the title, the day it was cut and the day it was asked for, and there was no line anywhere in four hundred entries for The Dark of Ault, asked for, not once, and the far-side trees were not in the cut columns either, because you do not write a book off until it is finished.

The wood had been growing them for six months, slowly, at a rate the near trees do not manage, out of sight round a curve, and nobody had cut them, and nobody was going to, and Enid sat in the leaf litter and worked out the only two reasons there were and they were both unbearable.

The first was that cutting a book somebody is part-way through is a decision about that person, and the wood did not get to make it. The second was that all eleven were Ned Hollis’s sister, and the man who wrote the title on the board was sixty years old, and the girl was thirteen, and neither of them was going to be the one to decide that for him.

* * *

They were in the wood at four o’clock on a Thursday in March, all three of them, because Absalom had been the one to say it and he had said it badly and at once.

They stood on the far side of the wire, in the litter, with the eleven blue books above their heads, and nobody could do anything.

“All I have to do is put them in the bag,” said Enid. “Four minutes. I have done four books in an afternoon.”

“No,” said Absalom. “You can do it. That is not the same as doing it.”

Ned said, “She is my sister,” to nobody, and then again, to the ground. “I have not got to the bit where they come in.”

Enid looked at the smallest one, up in the branch, the green one, a fortnight old. “The wood does it once. For whoever asks, later, when they are ready. It is not a copy of the copy, it is the same book, and the tree makes it out of what the tree already knows, and it will be a different book. It will not be this one.”

“So I have to know I am going to want a different one,” said Ned.

“Yes.”

“That is the whole thing, then. That is what I have to do.”

He stood there a while longer, and then he said, “One page a week,” and Enid, who had been thinking it and had not said it, felt the back of her neck go hot. “One page a week, and I will know by the end of it whether I am going to want a different one, and if I do, I will ask for it, and if I do not, then the book was a book and I will have read it.”

Enid cut the first one off that afternoon, the full-grown one, the size of her two hands together, and it came away with the flat sound and went in the bag, and she wrote TREE 61 in the ledger and did not put ASKED on that line, because it had not been asked for, and Absalom held the bag for her and did not say one word about it.

* * *

She was nineteen before they made her head pruner and gave her the Bank and the shears and the tally board, and by then she had learned to take a book off in one movement, and to look at the green ones last, and to write ASKED in the ledger the morning somebody asked instead of the afternoon they got there.

Absalom retired in the spring and came in every single day after that, which they had not expected, and which they could not have stopped.

He sat under the fourth tree of the fourth row, on the same bench, at the same time, and he did not do anything at all, and after about a month one of the pruners asked him whether he would like a chair brought out from the hall.

“The wood will do it,” he said, and it did, in the way the wood does things, which is that you go back one morning and there is a growth on the trunk where he has been sitting for two hundred days, and a growth can be encouraged or discouraged with a pair of shears, and nobody discourages it.

By the second spring it was a chair. Not a bench. A chair, with a back, on the north side of a small tree, and the seat of it worn shiny in one place only.

He is not staff. He is a reader, and the wood does not keep a tally of readers, and it has never had to.
