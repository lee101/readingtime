# The Dragon Courier on the Longest Route

![Cover art: The Dragon Courier on the Longest Route](../art/intermediate/67-the-dragon-courier-on-the-longest-route.webp)

The courier dragon came over the ridge at four in the afternoon and settled onto the ridgepole of the post house at Ashcombe, which she had not done in three hundred years.

Sef was seventeen and in his second year, up a ladder with a line and a plummet when she came over the ridge, and he came down it faster than anybody should.

The post house of Ashcombe is one long room with a counter, a stove, a rack of pigeonholes, and a book. The book is the sky book: every letter that has come in over the roofs for four hundred years, in the order it arrived, in a hand that is not English.

“You are the long route,” Sef said. “Second page. You are AUR— ” and he stopped, and rubbed the smudge off his thumb. “I cannot read that column.”

“How do you copy it?”

“By shape. That one has a tail on it.”

Postmistress Bram came in from the yard, looked up at the roof, and said something short in a dialect. She had been postmistress for thirty-one years and had spent them trying to get the sky post wound up. There is one person alive who can read the sky book, and she is on the roof, and she will not help.

“Nine hundred and three letters,” Sef said off the manifest board. “Two hundred and fourteen stops on the route.”

“Four of them are real,” said Bram.

“Say that again.”

“I did not say it to be repeated. Four.”

Aurelle put her head down and looked at him through the doorway, which is a thing she does, and Sef felt it behind him like a lamp being carried in.

“Off you go, then,” Bram said, and went out.

“Miss Aurelle,” Sef said, to the tail of her. “How is the wing?”

“The left one.”

“Right. And if you came down somewhere that was not on the route, say the fence is over, or there is a calf in the way, or the ice — what happens then? Does the book stop?”

“Then I do not.”

Sef laughed, because it was the best answer he had heard all year, and put it away in the part of his mind where he keeps the things he is going to look up.

* * *

The long route was drawn in 1581 by a man called Emery Vane, who had a good map and no imagination, and it is still in use because it is the shortest and the dullest of the three. It goes where it goes because a clerk in a warm room decided that it should, and it does not care what has happened since.

It is not a path on the ground. It is a path in the air, and a courier flies it in order, and between two stops she is over somebody’s field and does not come down.

A stop called Harrowmere lasted from 1581 until 1712, when the plague took it. A stop called the Bell Ordinal is now a motorway junction, and the junction is on a slip road, and the slip road is not in the book.

Between Harrowmere and the Bell Ordinal there is a point above the North Sea where the book says turn, and she turns, because the book says turn, and there is nothing under her but water, and nobody to see that she turned.

The Standing Order is one page. Rule eleven is the only one anybody has ever asked about. A courier of the long route shall not alight upon any ground not set down in her book.

The commentary runs to a page and a half. It is not in order for a courier to be landing where she has not been told, and seeing what is there. There are no exceptions. The word person does not appear anywhere in the Order, and rule eleven has been printed unchanged since 1581.

Nine hundred and three letters, two hundred and fourteen stops, and about nine hundred times round the loop. That is four hundred years, which is longer than anybody has ever been anything.

* * *

Nether Fenn is six miles off the route, which is how it comes to be a stop at all. A man was carried off a boat there in 1790 and did not walk again, and the clerk who wrote him in put the town on the list.

The infirmary at Nether Fenn is a workhouse with forty beds and a matron who had no idea why a dragon was coming down into her yard.

The letter was addressed to a ward. It had been in pigeonhole ninety-one at Ashcombe for four hundred years, because in 1604 a ward was a house by the water, and the sorting office cannot tell a ward from a woman called Ward.

Aurelle held the window out in the yard with her chin on her foreleg, which is how she reads. A registrar named Ottery, thirty-one and going grey, came out to see what had landed on him, read the first line, and sat down.

“It is for the ward,” he said.

“It is from the spring of 1604. There is a girl in bed four. It says what to do, and the day and the hour, and the day is the next Tuesday.”

“Do we have a girl of nine in bed four?”

“You have a girl of nine in bed four,” said Aurelle. She had looked.

They operated on the Tuesday at eight. The point of the stop is not the operation. It is that at eight o’clock on the Sunday somebody in the world knew what would happen on the Tuesday. Aurelle went north with one letter gone from the bag and nine hundred and two in it.

* * *

Crack Haven light stands on nine feet of rock and the keeper is Sutcliffe, seventy-nine, who came there as an assistant in 1958 and has not been off the rock since except to the surgery twice.

Four hundred and twenty steps. The oil comes up from the harbour once a month. The weight that turns the lens must be wound every two hours, and a man does it on the rock all night, and he has done it for nine years.

Aurelle had nine letters in the bag addressed to the Crack Haven Light Company at Harbour Street, and there has been no Harbour Street on the route since 1871. Sutcliffe had written one every year, asking them to look at his bill.

The letter she came with was from the light company. It acknowledged nine years of dues paid direct by the keeper. It renewed his appointment for five years. At the foot it said: The light is yours so long as you want it and we will not put it out.

The date at the foot is nine years wrong. It is dated the day he paid the first of them, and postmarked the day before. A clerk copied the year off the line above, which is how the bill was made in the first place. It was four pounds a year, and Sutcliffe had paid it out of a pension of fifty-two pounds, every year, without saying.

He read it on the fourth step, which is where he reads, and read it twice, and said thank you to a dragon four hundred feet below the gallery.

* * *

Wetherlam is not a village. It is a house, a byre, a water trough and eleven letters a year for the woman who lives in it, because the post has never come down that lane and the Ellerys have done the delivery themselves since the lane was made.

The bypass goes through the garden. They have been four times. The fifth is Thursday.

Aurelle came over the ridge at half past three and could not land, and the reason is the whole of the book. The fourth live stop is written as Wetherlam at the ford, and the ford is a culvert now, under a foot of concrete on the line of the new road, and a courier may not alight on a highway.

So she went round eleven times, and the woman came out of the door every time, in a coat, with a list in her hand.

She was eighty-one. She was the ninth Ellery. On the flyleaf at the back of the delivery book was a list of four hundred letters that had come to Wetherlam and had not been the one, in her grandmother’s hand, her mother’s, and hers.

“One of these is the only one that matters,” she said. “And it is not a letter. It is a piece of paper about the road. I have been eighty years waiting for it, and they have been eighty years taking the garden.”

Aurelle had it in the bag. She knew it by the shape of it, and because the bag was heavy on that side.

“And you cannot give it to me,” the woman said, looking up at her.

“No,” said Aurelle. “I cannot come down. There is no ground.”

The old woman sat down on the wall by the trough with the list of four hundred letters across her knees, and said she was sorry, and that it was not the dragon’s doing.

Aurelle went on to the last stop, three miles south, with the bag heavy on one side and nowhere to set it down.

* * *

The dead-letter box at Ashcombe is a stone coffin under the counter and it is not ever emptied, and every letter that has come in over the roofs addressed to Ashcombe itself is in the bag instead, because the sky post cannot put a letter in its own letterbox.

Bram had the oldest one out of it on the counter.

“Aldith Renn,” she said. “Postmistress nine years, from 1588. A great one, and a fool, and both of those are in the book.”

“It has no name on it,” said Sef. “It says whoever holds the post.”

“It is Sef’s name on the manifest,” said Bram. “Fourth down. He copied it out himself in November, because I cannot read that hand and he says he can. Look at it. His own name, in a hand he cannot read, twice a week, for two years.”

Sef went the colour of the paper. “I would have said.”

“You would have said,” said Bram, “if you had known what you were saying.”

The letter said that the post of Ashcombe had one custom: the post walked the whole route once a year on its own two feet and looked at every one of the stops. Aldith Renn’s predecessor had done it twice. She had never done it once. She was leaving it to whoever held the post next, and said in the same breath that it was not compulsory.

“How long is the route?” Sef said.

“Two hundred and fourteen stops,” said Aurelle. “Eleven hundred and forty miles, with the water part.”

“Then I shall want better boots,” said Sef, “and to start in April, and somebody to tell my mother I am not going to the sea.”

* * *

Bram put her hand on the lid of the coffin and said the route would be struck off at the quarter day, the case of nine hundred and three letters sold by weight to a paper mill, and nobody in Ashcombe would ever have to look at the sky book again.

Sef said the route was not Bram’s to strike off. Bram said it was in the schedule of a company wound up in 1601, and that the only reason it had never been struck off was that nobody had ever been up a ladder able to read the column.

Then she went out, and left the two of them, and did not go far.

“Aurelle,” said Sef. “Where do you sleep?”

“The byre. The book has had the byre at Ashcombe since 1581.”

“That is not what I asked. You have got four hundred years and a list, and not one place on it that you have to be late for. If I were you I should not know what to do with a Tuesday.”

She shifted her weight and the whole post house took it.

“Can you carry a person?”

Aurelle did not answer at once. The wind came over the ridge and took a corner of the sacking off the roof, and she put a claw on it and held it there.

“A man wrote that rule in 1581 so that nobody could ever use a courier to move a person out of one place into another,” she said. “It says nothing at all about letters, and in four hundred years I have never once found out what I would do about it.”

“Because you were frightened.”

“Because I was frightened,” she said. “Write that in the book. You keep the book. In a hundred years somebody will want to know whether a courier can, and I should like there to be a note that says we did not find out.”

* * *

The bypass men were in the lane at eight with a laser level and a roll of orange tape, and Aurelle came down the road from the north and put herself on the new tarmac, in the lane, at Wetherlam, eleven yards from the door, and lay along the centre line like a goods engine waiting at a signal.

The woman came out with her list in her hand and did not drop it, and a boy on a bike stopped and did not come back.

The letter had four hundred years of damp through the middle of it. It came from the office of the Crown’s works at York, and refused leave for a turnpike across Wetherlam land, and in one flat sentence held that the lane there is ancient and cannot be stopped up, and it was signed and sealed, and it is still law, and it is the reason the road goes where the road goes.

The old woman read it standing in her own doorway. Then she read it again, and then she sat down on the step with the list of four hundred letters across her knees, and said, out loud, that it was dated 1588, and that her grandmother had ruled a line at the top of the list in 1804, and that the line said: this will come.

Aurelle stayed where she lay, on ground that was not in the book, for as long as it took. Then she got her feet under her, took a run at the ditch in a way that was not in any manual, and went north over the Wetherlam ridge with nine hundred letters and three stops in the bag, and she was the lightest she had been in a century.
