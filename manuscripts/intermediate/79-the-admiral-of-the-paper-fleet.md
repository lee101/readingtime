# The Admiral of the Paper Fleet

![Cover art: The Admiral of the Paper Fleet](../art/intermediate/79-the-admiral-of-the-paper-fleet.webp)

The fleet was built in the winter behind the fish market, eleven ships to a side, at full scale, out of pasteboard, cane and calico, and the town of Callet had been building it every year since 1798.

Not models. Ships. Commodore Ruth Aylwin could put her hand on the bulwark of the French flagship and it would take her hand, and the seams had four coats of flour paste in them, and a boy had to be careful with his elbows.

The nine Aylwins had kept the tide, and the tide was what made the whole thing work. The battle was fought at the same state of water every year, the two hours either side of the flood at half past ten, so that the channel was the channel the original had been, and the sea came over the same sandbank at the same speed.

Ruth Aylwin was thirty-four, and the ninth of her name to hold the chair, and she was tired in a way she had not been at twenty-nine.

* * *

On the day, the town put on its Sunday clothes and walked down to the harbour in a column, and the ninth Aylwin walked at the head of it in a coat with a great deal of gold on it, because the coat had come with the chair and could not be altered without a vote.

Seth was fourteen and had been a powder-monkey for a year, which meant carrying six tubs of sawdust and red paper from the shed to the battery and back, four trips, and then standing well away and being useful about it.

The eleven French ships came out of the eastern creek on the flood, exactly when they always had, and Callet went at them in full dress, and for two hours the channel was a noise of canvas going up and paste coming apart and the crowd on the quay making a sound Ruth had never got used to and had stopped trying to.

At twenty past twelve the French flagship took the Cardigan in the middle of her length, and the Cardigan folded, and the town lost.

The crews were in the water before the fuses went, every year, because the fuses were slow and the boys were fast, and the rescue launch came round the point full of men in wet football shirts. Nobody had ever been hurt. That was not luck. It was in the articles.

Ruth stood in the chair on the quay and said, to two thousand people and the sea, “We are defeated,” and she meant it, because meaning it was nine parts of the office, and her grandfather had told her so once, in a car park, when she was eleven.

* * *

The Invitation Book lived in the harbour office in a drawer that had to be unlocked, and Ruth went into the appendix for the tide table in the January of a year with nothing much in it.

The appendix was ruled off from the main text by a hand nobody in the office had used since 1801. Ruth read the first line of it standing up, with her coat still on.

Sixth of September, seventeen hundred and ninety-eight. The channel was taken at ten o’clock. The town is lost and knows it. The French were better than us, and it is better that this is written down by a man who was standing on the sandbank than that it should be found out later by a stranger.

The copies had all been made by men who could not be bothered with the appendix. Two hundred years of careful, honest copying, and every one of them stopped at the line that told them where the honest part ended.

Ruth sat down on the floor of the harbour office with the book in her lap and read it four times, and then she went and found Abel Ward, who had been the harbour clerk for forty-one years and had a head like a boiled egg.

“Abel. Who won?”

“The French,” said Ward.

“Why?”

“Because the French won,” said Ward, and put his pen down. “Madam, we have never won. Not once. Not in my time, not in my father’s, and my father’s father was in the boats.”

* * *

“The town has never won,” Ruth said, “but the town has never stopped either. Why does a town do that for two hundred years?”

Ward looked at her for a while. “Because it wins it, madam.”

“That is not a sentence.”

“It is the only sentence there is.” He turned the desk lamp down a notch, the way he had turned it down every evening since 1971. “A town that loses a battle it fought for two hundred years has a history, madam. It has a date. It has a song that people are too embarrassed to sing. It has a boat in the museum with a piece of the original timbers in it, and the timbers are real, and there are four of them left. A town that has never fought one is a town with a beach.”

He got the Invitation Book out of the drawer and put it on the desk and squared it. “We have sent a letter to the Dessaints every year since 1801. That is your family’s office, madam, the Aylwins have written it since the first one. And the answer has come back every year since 1801, and every year it says no, and the answer is in the book, in the column, in Abel Ward’s hand and before that in his father’s and before that in his father’s.”

“Declined. With thanks. Regret, etcetera.”

“Declined, with thanks, regret, etcetera,” said Ward. “Two hundred and twenty-six of them. And here is the thing I have never been able to say out loud in this office. Nobody has ever once asked them a question that was a question. You lot have been writing to them for two hundred years as if the answer were a formality you had already decided on, and they have been answering it the way you answer a formality.”

“They are French. They are the other side.”

“They are the other side of a defeat that was agreed two hundred years ago by everybody at this table, and nobody has said so in writing, and so a woman in a dentist’s chair on the far side of the headland has spent two hundred years being politely told by the wrong people that she is the enemy of a town with a beach.”

* * *

Ruth found Seth in the chart room at four in the afternoon, doing what he did with the afternoons, which was the ships. He had built eleven of them that winter out of matchboxes and had the French ones ranged up the chart against the tide so he could count the minutes.

“Seth.”

“Eleven to eleven, same as always.”

“Yes. Abel Ward has been telling me things.”

Seth put a matchbox down. “Ma’am.”

“The letter to the Dessaints. Have you ever read one?”

“No, ma’am.”

“Read one.”

He read it, twice, with his tongue out. Then he read the answer in the column, which was the same answer, and then he read the letter again, slowly, the way you read a thing when you have started to hear it.

“It does not say shall we fight again,” Seth said.

“No.”

“It says shall we agree again. It has said that since before either of us.”

Ruth sat down on the edge of the chart table. “The Aylwins were never taught to read it properly. There has never been a reason to. We have only ever had to inherit a fight.”

Seth did not say anything else about it. Neither did she. It was the only sentence either of them ever said about the invitation, and they were both careful with it, because they could both see that it was the whole of the thing.

* * *

He wrote it in the office, on the good paper, at eleven at night, with the harbour lamp and a bottle of ink.

Dear Dessint. We are having the batel on the 14th. It is writen in our own book that we have not won it in two hundred yers, so we should like to agry to do so again. Come if you can. You can win. We will not mind. Seth.

He read it four times, and it did not get better, and he sealed it with the office stamp because he could not think what else to do with a stamp.

Sainte-Claire was eleven miles along the coast road and the bus did not run on Sundays, so he walked, and it took two hours and ten minutes, and he got there at ten past one in the afternoon in his good coat with the ink on his cuff.

The Dessaint house was a terrace with a brass plate on the door and a surgery on the ground floor, and there were three people in the waiting room and a fish tank with one enormous unbothered fish in it. He stood by the fish tank for a while. A woman of about thirty came out of the surgery in a cardigan with her sleeves pushed up and said that if he had an appointment he should say so, because she was running behind.

“I have not got an appointment,” Seth said. “I have got a letter. It’s for you, from the town.”

She read it standing up. It took her a while. She got to the part about winning and stopped, and looked at him.

“You have spelled my name wrong.”

“Yes,” said Seth. “I’m sorry.”

“No,” she said. “I mean that I have read that letter two hundred and twenty-six times in a column in a book, and nobody has ever put my name in writing at the top of one. There is an accent on the E. People are going to be talking about the spelling for years.”

* * *

She came on the fourteenth, and the town came out for her in a column, and Ruth Aylwin met her at the head of the quay in a coat with a great deal of gold on it and shook her hand and could not think of anything to say except “Welcome to Callet, and I am extremely sorry.”

Élodie Dessaint, thirty-one, dentist, said that she was, and that she had brought a flask, and that her great-great-grandfather had been a very rude letter-writer.

The tide came at half past ten. The French came out of the eastern creek on the flood and took the channel in nineteen minutes, which was faster than anybody had ever managed it, because the captain of the French flagship had spent two hundred years declining to come and had therefore spent two hundred years thinking about it.

The crews were in the water well before the fuses. The rescue launch came round the point. The Cardigan went down in three pieces.

Ruth Aylwin stood in the chair on the quay and said, “We are defeated,” and Élodie Dessaint stood on the quay beside her eating a meat pie she had brought from a shop on the far side of the headland, and said, “Yes. You are. It is frightfully well done.”

The town threw a party in the square until two in the morning, in the middle of the burnt ribs of its own fleet, and Seth danced in the flour paste because nobody thought to tell him to change, and Ruth Aylwin sat on an upturned dinghy and laughed until she had to be given water.

The following February, the letter went out from Callet.

Ruth Aylwin wrote it herself, and her eighth predecessor had written the first one, and it was the same letter it had always been, word for word, the whole of it, exactly as it had been sent every year since 1801.

It came back the way it always came back, in the column, in the same hand, with the same two sentences, declined with thanks and regret, etcetera.

Except that the word Aylwin was not in it. The word Aylwin has never been in it. It had never once been in it.

It was signed Élodie Dessaint, and under her name, in a smaller hand, she had written: we agree again, and next year you may have the tide.
