# The Cat Who Collected Good Stories
![Cover art: The Cat Who Collected Good Stories](../art/intermediate/13-the-cat-who-collected-good-stories.webp)

Puss wore boots made of red leather. He kept them polished with a cloth sewn from moonbeams.

He also carried a small leather book.

The book belonged to the miller’s daughter, Nell. She had lent it to him because he said he could bring back a story worth keeping.

Puss had already collected three.

He had collected a knight who rescued a village from a dragon. The dragon had been old, tired, and unwilling to leave its warm cave. The knight had offered it a basket of honey and a new roof. The village called it a rescue. Puss called it a good story.

He had collected a baker who found a gold coin in her chimney. The coin had belonged to a boy who had lost it while playing a game. The baker had returned it and received a better coin from the mayor. Puss called it a good story.

He had collected a princess who forgave a prince after he broke her favorite crown. The crown had been made of glass. She repaired it with gold thread. Puss called it a good story.

Every night, he added a line to the book.

*Good story: someone brave does a difficult thing.*

Nell read the entries and smiled. “You are making a collection.”

“Of the best,” Puss said. “A person should know what courage looks like.”

“Do you?”

Puss lifted one boot. “I know the stories better than most.”

One morning, the king sent a message. The Duke of Silverbells had disappeared. The road to his castle was unsafe. A hero was needed.

The messenger looked at Puss’s boots. “They are fine boots.”

Puss drew himself taller. “They have walked through three kingdoms.”

“Can you fight?”

“I have collected fighting stories.”

The messenger frowned. “That is not the same.”

Puss went to the mill. Nell was sifting flour into a wooden bowl. Her sleeves were rolled above her elbows. Her hair was tied with a piece of twine.

“I need your book,” Puss said.

Nell held it behind her back. “You promised to bring it back.”

“I will bring it back with one more story.”

“What kind?”

Puss placed one paw over his heart. “The finest.”

Nell looked at the road outside. “The Duke’s castle is beyond the bridge. The bridge keeper says someone has been taking tolls from travelers. The people call the person the Robber of Silverbells.”

“Does the Robber have a sword?”

“I do not know.”

“Does the Robber wear a helmet?”

“No one can see beneath the hood.”

Puss’s tail curled. A blank space waited in the book.

“Then the story is not ready.”

* * *

Puss found the Robber beneath the bridge.

The person wore a gray cloak. A sack of coins hung from one hand. A small lantern shone from the belt. The figure was young, with mud on the shoes and a trembling chin.

Puss drew his tiny sword.

“Stop!” he cried. “The hero Puss of the Red Boots is here!”

The Robber froze. “Puss? You are a cat.”

“Cats can be heroes.”

The figure raised the sack. “Please. These are the bridge keeper’s savings. The mill is broken. My father is sick.”

Puss lowered the sword by one inch. “Then why steal?”

“Because no one would lend me money. The bridge keeper says I must pay the toll. If I pay it, I have nothing to eat.”

Puss looked at the coins. He had collected stories of people who risked everything for a little goodness. This one was small, frightened, and real.

“Hold out your hand,” Puss said.

The Robber obeyed.

Puss bit the coin in the middle of the palm. The metal squealed. A crack ran through it, and the two halves fell as dust.

The Robber cried out. “That was my father’s last silver!”

Puss’s tail stiffened. He had wanted a quick line for his book. Instead, the person before him began to cry.

“I cannot feed him,” the Robber said.

Puss looked toward the mill. Smoke rose from its chimney. A wheel beside the river turned slowly, grinding nothing.

“The bridge toll is too high,” he said.

“Everyone says that.”

Puss picked up the empty sack. He did not know how to fix the mill. He knew only that a story should not ask one person to carry the whole weight.

“Come with me,” he said.

They walked back to the bridge. The keeper was a thin man with a red nose. He counted his coins while the Robber watched.

“The toll is eight coins,” the keeper said. “The rule protects the bridge.”

“The bridge has no planks on the west side,” the Robber said.

The keeper frowned. “That is a separate matter.”

Puss climbed onto the tollbox. His boots made small red marks in the dust.

“Show me the accounts,” he said.

The keeper handed him a ledger. Puss could not read every number, but he could see the same sum copied over and over. Each traveler had paid. Each payment had been counted. The money was gone.

“The mill family paid nine,” Puss said. “You wrote eight.”

The keeper looked at the page. “A mistake.”

“The Robber took the money after that.”

“That does not—”

Puss placed one paw on the ledger. “A mistake is not a reason to build a wall around a hungry person.”

The keeper’s face reddened. He looked at the Robber’s muddy shoes. Then he looked at the broken west side of the bridge.

“I cannot lower the toll,” he said. “The stones need repair.”

Puss glanced toward the mill. Nell was at the doorway, holding the book.

* * *

Nell came down to the river.

She had a sack of flour on her shoulder. Puss hurried to help, but she shook her head.

“I brought this for the bridge,” she said.

The Robber stared. “Why?”

“Because the west planks will not hold a cart. The mill cannot send grain unless the road is safe.” She set down the sack. “And because your father needs something warmer than worry.”

The keeper looked at the flour. “This does not pay the toll.”

“No,” Nell said. “It helps one family and repairs one bridge. The rest is a question.”

Puss watched her open the flour sack. The Robber helped her carry it. Together they cleared stones from the west side. Children from the village brought straw. The miller brought an axe. The bridge keeper brought his strongest rope.

The work was slow. Hands slipped. Dust filled mouths. One child fell into the shallow water and came up laughing. Puss climbed through the gaps, pulling loose knots from rope with his teeth.

By sunset, the planks were fixed. The toll remained. The Robber still owed money. But the bridge would no longer collapse beneath a cart.

The keeper examined the boards. “This will hold for a month.”

“Next month we repair it again,” Nell said.

Puss opened his book. He held his paw above the blank page. The old sentence appeared in his mind: *Good story: someone brave does a difficult thing.*

He wrote instead: *Good story: a person does not have to be fearless. They can be afraid and still hand over the flour.*

Nell read over his shoulder. “That is long.”

“Important stories are longer.”

The Robber stood nearby. “I still took the money.”

“Yes,” Puss said. “You did. That part should stay in the book.”

They returned the remaining stolen coins. The keeper counted them, then added the value of the destroyed silver to the Robber’s debt and wrote Puss’s name in the margin. Puss offered to help repay it. The Robber promised to repay the keeper through work at the mill. The miller offered him a room above the grain room until the first wage came.

No one called him a hero. No one called him a villain. He was a tired person who had made a bad choice and was now making a harder one.

* * *

At the court, the Duke’s messenger waited.

Puss entered with mud on his boots and dust in his fur. The court whispered. They had expected a sword fight. They had expected a dragon.

“Where is the Duke?” the messenger asked.

Puss looked at his book. The old stories were safe inside it. The new story had no castle in it. It had flour dust, broken boards, and a bridge keeper counting coins.

“The Duke is not at the castle,” Nell said. “He left the castle after his son died. He has been walking beside the river.”

Puss blinked. The messenger had told him the Duke was waiting. The message had been written by someone else.

“Where is he now?” Puss asked.

The messenger lowered his eyes. “I did not know. I was told to bring a hero.”

Puss closed the book. A story could send people in the wrong direction if it was too tidy.

They found the Duke at the foot of a willow. He was drawing stones in the mud. His son had liked to skip them across the water.

The Duke looked at Puss. “Are you the hero?”

“No,” Puss said. “I am a cat who collects good stories.”

“Then you may go.”

Puss sat beside him. “May I ask why you left the castle?”

The Duke pointed at the stones. “Because everyone there told me what the death meant. A brave prince. A shining example. A story with a lesson.” He placed one stone on top of another. “My son was not brave. He was afraid of deep water. He liked singing badly. He once hid a pear under my pillow and forgot where he put it.”

Puss looked at the muddy stones.

“Do you want a story about him?”

“I want a quiet place where a quiet person can be missed.”

Puss did not write.

Nell sat down in the grass. The Robber brought two cups of water. The Duke drank, then placed another stone in the river.

Puss opened the book later, at home. He turned past all the shining deeds. He found the page with the Robber and the bridge. He added a final line.

*A trustworthy story does not make the brave person perfect. It shows what the person can be trusted to do.*

Nell closed the book with him. “Will you collect more stories?”

Puss looked at his red boots. The leather no longer shone as brightly. Dust lived in the seams.

“Yes,” he said. “But I will ask the people first. And I will leave the parts they do not want to tell.”

Outside, the mill wheel began to turn. It made a small, steady sound: one weight, one turn, one honest push at a time.
