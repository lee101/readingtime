# The Man Who Could Lift the Town's Roof

![Cover art: The Man Who Could Lift the Town's Roof](../art/intermediate/140-the-man-who-could-lift-the-towns-roof.webp)

Halversgate has eleven hundred roofs and one man who can take a roof off a house with his two hands. His name is Bo Arden and he is forty-four and he is a stevedore on the quay at Halversgate, and the lifting is not strength. He can do it because of a thing that came with him and that nobody in the town has ever been able to explain, and the price of it is his own back, metered in roofs, and a roof he lifts is a roof gone out of his spine for good.

Dr. Tuppence Ashby has the count written down in a column. The column is in the surgery on Coble Row, on the second page of the ledger, and it has a heading that says ARDEN, B. and it has been ruled with a nib so that a line cannot be added without it showing, because a man who can put things down is a man who can be asked for a number.

The number is nine. At nine the spine finishes. Bo Arden has had eight.

* * *

The eight are on the page and they are all there is to read.

Eleven years ago, the bakehouse on Fennel Street, which was a roof and two chimneys and came down in one piece while six men stood in the road. Then the boatyard shed at Gallant's Slip, which was felt one autumn morning in forty minutes. Then three in one winter: the roof off the almshouse chapel, the roof off the net loft at Coble Row, and a greenhouse roof at the top of Vellum Lane that Dr. Ashby has described in the column as not being a real roof and having been counted anyway, because Bo Arden had lifted it and Bo Arden does not argue with the column about what he has done with his own back.

The last one was four years ago and it was a wall, and it is in the column as a wall, and Dr. Ashby wrote underneath it that this should not have been counted and that it was counted.

Below the eight there is a ninth line ruled off and empty, and the ruling is Dr. Ashby's, and the ruling has been there since the day the first one went in.

“You will ask me what happens at nine,” said Dr. Ashby, in the surgery, to anybody, for four years.

“And what do you tell them.”

“I tell them the truth, which is that it finishes the spine at about the third lumbar and that after that I am a man who cannot lift anything at all, including a kettle, and that the pain starts in about six weeks and does not go away and is worse in the wet and is not pain anybody can be given anything for.” She put her hand flat on the page. “And I tell them it is not like pain. It is like a room in your own house being walled off, one after another, from the bottom.”

* * *

Bo Arden lived in two rooms over the rope store on the north quay and he had walked with a bad limp since the winter after the third of those roofs, and the quay knew which way to go round him.

He did not want to be a story about himself and he was a story about himself. Two boys on Fennel Street had made a broadsheet called the Roofman out of a printing press they had bought for eleven shillings, and there was a drawing in it of a man bent under a beam with his knees going, and under it, in letters a quarter of an inch high, it said HE CAN TAKE IT OFF IN A MINUTE, which was true, and did not say what it took off with it, because the boys were seventeen and did not know.

The Vestry sent him a letter in the February.

They wanted a number. Not a figure of speech. They wanted to know, in writing, how many roofs Bo Arden could lift in the whole of his life, so that a schedule could be drawn up for the town, so that the harbour board could know what it had, so that the thing could be written down before it happened instead of after it.

There were four roofs in Halversgate that the survey had marked as wanting doing, and eleven more that would want doing inside ten years.

Bo Arden wrote back on the fourth of the March. He wrote four lines. He wrote that he did not know the number, that he was not going to be measured for it, and that the Vestry was writing to a man and not to a wall.

* * *

The storm came in on the ninth night of the November from the south-west, and it had been going since the afternoon, and it did not stop for a day and a half.

At about two in the morning on the second day, the front wall of the terrace at Coble Row came in under the wind and took the roof off the top house, and the roof went up rather than down, which is what a wind does to a roof that is not nailed, and it came over the gardens and was coming down across the street into the back ends of eleven houses on the far side with about four hours of dark left in it.

Somebody in the dark shouted for Bo Arden.

He was at the harbour office at two in the morning because the harbour office keeps the tide tables and Bo Arden reads the tide tables, and he went up to Coble Row in a lull with a lantern, and the street was full of glass and slate and people shouting in the wrong places.

There is a thing he does before he lifts anything and he does it every time and there is no hurrying him.

He walks under it. He goes in at the front door of the house the roof is on and he walks the length of the rooms and out of the back, and he looks up the whole way, and he comes out and he stands in the street and he looks at where the roof is going to land. He does that because a roof that is going to land in a street is not a roof, it is eleven thousand pounds of slate travelling.

He did it that night in about nine minutes.

Then he said to them that they were to take the eleven houses' chimneys off, because a chimney stops a roof and a chimney also kills the man under it, and the men did it, and he stood in the middle of Coble Row and watched them and did not help, because the chimney work was theirs and he was there for the roof.

Then he took it.

He had it off the house and over the street and set down flat on the mud below the low wall, and the sound it made when it went down was the loudest thing that had happened in Halversgate for a hundred years, and the window in the hut above the rope store came down out of its frame.

He was on his knees in the road afterwards and could not get up, and four men got him up, and he walked to Dr. Ashby's on his hands on the front of a cart and then on his feet down Coble Row to the surgery door with somebody's arm over his shoulder.

Dr. Ashby came out into the street.

She did not go in. She looked at him standing in her own street in a wet coat with the wind still up, and then she went in and turned to the second page and put the ninth line in, and the date, and what was under it, which was eleven houses and nobody.

* * *

Then the whole town wanted him to do it again.

Not the Vestry. Halversgate. There were forty-one roofs on the harbour board's list inside a week, and a builder's yard on Corrie Street put up a board offering him work by the day, and the board said anything, and the board was still up in the March. Men from the yard came down to the rope store on their lunch hour to ask him how he did it, which side he put his hands, whether it hurt, and Bo Arden answered them the same three sentences every time, and the three sentences never once mentioned the ninth.

Dr. Ashby came down on the Thursday with her bag.

She said it was finished, and she said it in the surgery, with the second page flat open, and she said that from that day the column was closed and no line would be ruled under the ninth one, and that if a man asked her in the street she would tell him the same thing.

“You will be asked in the street for forty years,” she said. “Because there is nothing else to ask about. Ask a man who has taken nine roofs off and you will be told about the ninth, and there is no tenth. That is the whole of what I have got and I have been giving it to this town for four years and nobody has ever once come back for it.”

“What would you have me do with it,” said Bo Arden.

“I would have you sit down,” said Dr. Ashby.

* * *

The survey came back in the February and the Vestry withdrew it. Nobody said it was withdrawn for a reason. The four roofs were left where they were, and the eleven more were scheduled in the ordinary way, by masons, over four years, at eight times the price, which is the price of a roof in Halversgate and has been for a long time.

There was one thing left over and the harbour board gave it to him without a vote, because it could not be used for anything else.

The roof from Coble Row was lying on the mud below the low wall where he had put it, and eleven houses had no roof, and eleven houses that had no roof in the February had roofs in the March, because eleven households went down to the mud on a Sunday in shirtsleeves and carried slates up a ladder a piece at a time for two days and did it themselves, badly, and a man called Huldah Roon told them which way to lay them.

Bo Arden watched it from the low wall. He could not get down the ladder and nobody asked him to.

* * *

He was fifty-two before he could lift a kettle with one hand, and he has never lifted a roof since, and the ninth line in the column on the second page of the ledger on Coble Row has a rule under it and nothing after it, and in this town nobody has ever asked a man to be brave again.

A roof is the largest thing a town owns and it is the easiest thing in it to talk about, and Halversgate has spent four years learning that the man who says nine is not a man with a number, he is a man with a thing left.
