# The Ballad Singer Who Was Not Allowed to Sing

![Cover art: The Ballad Singer Who Was Not Allowed to Sing](../art/intermediate/83-the-ballad-singer-who-was-not-allowed-to-sing.webp)

Ivo sang for the copper on Tuesday, and the copper was thin that week.

Vell paid its singers badly and on time, which Ivo had decided long ago was the finest arrangement a city could offer anybody. He stood on the low stage of the Green Kettle with his licence buttoned inside his shirt, scratching him all evening, and sang a song of four hundred lines about a barge that went down in the Marrow. By the fourth verse the room knew it well enough to come in on the chorus.

Eleven coins, a twist of paper notes, and a glass button landed in the hat. Under the stage lamp they came to ninety-one pence. His rent was due Friday.

Then the girl in the third row put two fingers flat on her ears and kept smiling at him, and he understood, the way you understand a dropped plate, that something in the room had gone wrong. The chorus had been half a beat behind him the whole song.

Sana was on the low stage already, case open, working the strings down to nothing while the last of the crowd decided it was time to walk home.

“You’re flat,” she said.

“The barge is flat. Barges are flat things.”

“You have been a semitone under the last three lines since Eppers Green and you know it.”

He had not known it. He pocketed the hat money and stood beside her while she got the fiddle quiet. Outside, the fog was sitting in the gutters like milk going sour.

“Play the Marrow song tomorrow,” she said. “Take it a shade under. They will call it style.”

“Tomorrow I want the Accordion Rooms.”

“Then sing something they have.”

He had a song, and that was the whole difficulty with it. It had lived in his chest eleven days, walking about at night in his own street, sitting on his back teeth waiting to be let out. Four verses and a chorus about a girl called Meri who sold candied plums outside the fish market. It was good, and it was going to be enormous, and he could never legally sing it, because Vell had not added a song to the roll since 1740.

* * *

The licensing office was in the low grey building on Fenner Street, and it smelled of ink and of the linseed they used on the floorboards.

Ivo went in on Wednesday with a speech he had rehearsed on the way. The clerk was Mr Bhatt, who had held the post for thirty years and sat in the same chair at a desk squared up so exactly to the wall that it was not a desk but a formality.

“You want to enter a song,” Mr Bhatt said, before Ivo had his hat off.

“I want to enter a song.”

“Title.”

Ivo gave him the title. Mr Bhatt drew a ledger toward him, uncapped his pen, wrote it in the visitor column, did not ask what the song was about, and went straight down the page to the fee line. “Four shillings,” he said.

“Four shillings is a week.”

“It is what the law says it is.” Mr Bhatt held the pen out, nib toward Ivo, and tapped a printed card beside it. “Read that. The card is the face of the law. The preamble is behind the roll if a person wants to know what the law is for.”

Ivo took the card and read the prohibitions. No song of riot. No song of famine. No song of a war not yet paid for. No song naming a magistrate. Then the fee, then the penalty, which was a hundred and forty pounds or the loss of the licence, whichever the magistrates preferred.

“For a song about plums,” he said.

“For any song.”

“And you decide which is which.”

“The office decides.” Mr Bhatt put the pen down and pulled a second book across his knees, thick as a paving stone. “Read it out. Then decide.”

Behind the roll was a broadsheet folded in three, gone brown along the creases. The seal on it was the old one, with the fish and the two hands, and the ink had faded to the colour of weak tea.

“Anno 1740. To the end that this city be not given a song it cannot pay for. A song of riot, of famine, of war not yet paid for, is a bill presented to the honest poor. The city therefore require the seal before the voice.”

“That is all of it,” said Mr Bhatt. “One clause. The rest of the book is titles.”

“It is a good law,” Ivo said, and heard how thin his own voice sounded in the room.

“It is a short one.” Mr Bhatt turned the ledger round so Ivo could see what he had written. “Four shillings. Today or not today.”

Ivo put his hand into his coat, counted what was in there, which was ninety-one pence, and did not take it out.

* * *

He sang the new song on Thursday night in the back room of the Accordion Rooms, for six people, badly, with the door shut and his back to the wall.

He had got as far as the first line of the second verse when the landlord came in carrying a lamp.

“Who is that one?”

“Mine. It is new. It is not for the room, I am only trying it.”

“It is not on the roll.”

“It is not.”

“Then you can finish it somewhere else, and I will have my back room back.”

In the alley Ivo stood with his back to the wet brick and thought, quite clearly, that this was what a person was owed. The rule said a person could not sing it. It did not say a person could not have one.

Sana was there, because she had been in the alley some time and had heard the door shut on him.

“He is right, though,” she said.

“Say that again and I will put your case in the canal.”

“He is right about the card. I have been licensed eleven years. You do not sing off the roll, not even for six friends, not even when they beg. That is not the room, Ivo, that is the licence, and the licence is on your chest, and the magistrates do not ask the room. They ask the chest.”

“I know what the licence is. I know what a plaque is.”

“You do not know what a hundred and forty pounds is.” She pulled the case strap over her shoulder. “My mother sang for thirty years to feed three of us and never sang off the roll once, not even the night the fever came. The money was in the room and the fine was in the city.”

On the Friday he went back to the office, meaning to find the fee, and found the buying ledger instead.

* * *

The buying ledger sat on the third shelf at the back of the office, chained to the desk leg. Ivo read it with his heart going like a bird in a bag, because the door was open and Mr Bhatt was at the window with his back to him.

It was a ledger of purchases. Titles, sellers, sums, dates, running from the year the office opened, in one hand for two hundred years and then a different hand and then a third.

“The Ferryman’s Round, three shillings, paid, T. Nabb, yeoman of the Low Weir.”

“Widdershins, bought at auction, one pound four, the estate of J. Harrow.”

That last one was his grandmother’s, and he knew it before he finished the line, the way you know a street in the dark. She had sung Widdershins at the Candleworks for forty years and died with a chest cold in a room over a chandler’s, having told him at nine that the song was hers and would be hers after her.

He found her entry. “Widdershins, one pound four, from the estate, 1911.” The column after it was headed performance, and beside his grandmother’s name the ink thinned out, and the clerk had written a single word, “assign”, and under that a list of surnames running down a full column, eleven of them, then a rule, then “no further claims.”

The office had not stolen the song. It had bought it, honestly, in the right currency, at the price of a bad spring and a funeral, and the buying had never stopped, and the roll had not grown by a line in all that time, because a song on the roll was not an old song any more. It was a thing the city held.

* * *

He took the song to the yard behind the Bellhouse on the second Sunday at four, because the yard is where the Accordion Rooms crowd goes to smoke and argue, and forty people saw him do it.

Sana played the first eight bars. Then Ivo sang the whole song, four verses and a chorus, to the smell of wet stone and other people’s smoke, at the top of his voice, unlicensed, in the open.

He had the third verse into it before the singing went out of him.

It was not nerves. He had seen the word “assign” in a clerk’s hand, and “no further claims” under it, and something in him had come loose, and for the length of one chorus he wanted the whole yard to know what the city was. He had the fourth verse coming, and that one would finish him.

Sana put her palm flat on the strings and cut the sound dead, and said, loudly, in the voice she used for playing over a bad crowd, “That was vanity, Ivo, and not a hair of it was courage.”

Half the yard laughed. Somebody said, “oh, well.”

“You wanted to be right,” Sana said. “You wanted to be seen being right about a clerk, and your vanity picked a song and got it a coat and a name, and that is the whole of it. Say so and we can go home.”

He had it in his chest again, closer than it had ever been, and every word of the fourth verse was sitting on the backs of his teeth.

“I will finish it,” he said. “Let me finish it. Then they can all go and tell the magistrates where they heard it.”

She did not argue with him. She stepped in, which was the mistake she had been waiting for, and got her back to him and said, low, so that only he had it, “And where did you think it would end? Say the sentence, Ivo, because I have not heard you say it in eleven days.”

“I will be famous.”

“You will be a man who sings a love song about plums in an alley for thirty pounds, with a fine, and no plums.”

“Then it is the city against me.”

“It is nobody against you. That is the whole of the trick, and you have had the city made into an enemy for eleven days because it was the only honest thing left to do with the other fact, which is that your song is good, it is very good, and somebody is going to have to pay you for it, and the somebody is a room full of people, and a room full of people is a customer.”

He had no answer to that. He stood in the wet yard with his hands open and let it stand.

“You are not being brave,” Sana said. “You are refusing to sell. Every balladist in this city sells. Your grandmother sold and did not know it and died believing the song was hers, and I am not the one who tells her grandson he is above it.”

* * *

On the Monday he came into the office at nine and put four shillings on the desk in coins and a note and the change, and Mr Bhatt counted it and did not ask where it had come from.

He had not sung in nine days. He had nine days of hat money in front of him in a room with the curtain shut, and he had counted it four times.

“Title,” Mr Bhatt said.

Ivo gave it. The clerk wrote it in the visitor column, turned the ledger, and found the heading he wanted, the sale column, the price column, the date column, all ruled and all clean. The page had been ruled in 1740 for precisely this and had been waiting.

“Sold to the licensing office for four shillings, with performance,” Mr Bhatt said, writing. “The office holds the song. It goes on the roll at the price you have paid. It is yours until you sell it again.” He blew the ink, set the stamp down with a soft wet thump, and the room smelled of iron. “You will receive a call on the second Thursday of every month. Not a request. A call.”

“If the roll is the city’s, then everyone on it is mine.”

“It is not the city’s,” said Mr Bhatt. “It is on the roll.”

He lifted the big book onto the desk, turned to the back, laid a clean sheet against the ruled line, and set the pen into Ivo’s hand instead of taking it back.

Ivo wrote his title, and his name, and the date. It was eight words. His hand shook so badly that the clerk had to put two fingers flat on the corner to hold the paper still.

The last page of the roll of licensed songs is long and clean, bound in the same book as the four hundred that came before it, and the entry at the bottom sits in the correct column, dated the twenty-third of the month, and the ink is still drying.
