# The One Good Mission

![Cover art: The One Good Mission](../art/intermediate/138-the-one-good-mission.webp)

The Register has a room above the saddler's on Coombe Street. It opens three days a week, and the notice on the stair says anybody may come in, and there is no form to fill in.

The rule is that there is no money in it and no credit either. Nobody is paid and nobody owes. A volunteer hands the thing over and the thing is gone, and it is not written down against anybody, because a thing written down against a person is a debt, and the woman who runs it will not have debts in her room.

Siri Nettle was sixteen. Her one useful ability was arrival. She could think of a place she had stood in and be standing in it. The price was an address. Every time she arrived, one address went out of her head for good, she never chose which one, and she found out which only later, when somebody said a street name and she did not know the street. She had kept the list in a notebook since she was eleven, and there were forty-one lines in it.

Verity Sallow ran the room. She was forty-two, with grey hair cut square at the jaw, and she wore a grey coat with the fastenings done up to the top one, and she had run the Register for eleven years on a wage the committee paid her late and a budget nobody had raised since the second year.

Siri came in on a Thursday in the January with a pen.

“Put the pen down,” said Verity. “What can you do.”

“It is not much.”

“Everybody who comes in says that. Say it properly.”

“If I know where a thing is, I can be there. Not soon. Straight there.”

Verity wrote a number on the roster sheet and nothing else at all, because the rule is that what a person can do is never written down, only how many hours they are on.

“What does it take,” she said.

“An address.”

Verity looked at her for a while.

“You will not go out alone for a month,” she said. “And you will do one job. Not two. One job, one shift, and at the end of it you go home.”

* * *

The Saturday list was the biggest thing in the building. Verity called it the grocery run, and a grocery run in Halversgate is not a basket. It is a list of people, and on a Saturday it had eleven names on it in alphabetical order, and the order was the whole of the skill.

Kellan Prior at number four had been on that list since he was sixty, and he got flour and tea and nothing that had a name printed on the packet. Oddie Fennell at number nine had two children under four. Huldah Roon at the top of the stair in Pell Court took her shop up in a carrier bag because it was easier than a basket and had been doing so for nineteen years.

There were five rules for the run and Verity said them out loud every week whether she had said them that week or not. One, you go in the order of the list. Two, the milk goes in last, because the milk is the thing that spoils. Three, you never leave money, because money is credit and there is no credit in this room. Four, you knock twice and you wait, and if nobody comes you leave it at the door and you write nothing. Five, you do not carry anybody else's list into yours, because one person with two lists stops being one person.

“There is no sixth rule,” said Verity. “People keep asking me for a sixth rule.”

* * *

The first week Siri did not use it once.

Verity took her round on foot, because that is the point of a first month, and it was two hours in the rain and Siri's coat was soaked through before they reached Pell Court. Verity stopped at the bottom of the lane and said, “You have an hour and eleven houses. Say something about that.”

Siri thought of the step outside the shop on Fennel Street, where she had stood on a Tuesday buying a paper, and she was on the step.

The shop was shut. The shutters were down and the bread had gone to the bakery at the back.

Verity was still standing in the rain at the bottom of Pell Street.

“That took you eleven minutes,” she said, when she caught up. “Write eleven minutes on that line and write nothing else on it.”

* * *

By the fourth Saturday Siri was doing the run in fifty minutes.

She went in the order of the list. She thought of number four, and she was at number four. She thought of the shop on Fennel Street, and she was at the shop with the bread. She thought of the top of the stair in Pell Court, and she was on the stair with the carrier bag under her arm and the milk in her other hand, because the milk went in last and she had put it in last, in her own head, on the way.

Eleven arrivals. Eleven addresses.

On the fourth Saturday she stood on the step outside number four and could not have told anybody how to get to number four from anywhere else. She knew the house. She knew the door and the cat in the window and the man in it. The street it stood in was gone. She wrote nothing on the line, because the rule is that the list is people and not places, and Verity had said so on the first day.

“There is a blank in your notebook,” said Verity on the Thursday. “You have not filled it.”

“I did not choose them.”

“Nobody chooses. That is what I am telling you. Fill them in anyway, so that you know what you have spent, because the way people lose their houses in this town is not by fire. It is by arriving somewhere eleven times.”

* * *

The committee met on the first Wednesday of the February in the room at the bottom of the stair, and the stair shook whenever anybody stood on the top step, which was most of the time.

Bo Tranter chaired it. He was sixty-eight, a builder, and he carried a notebook of his own that was full and was not a list of people. He wanted a number per job. He said so for forty minutes, and Verity sat with her hands flat on the table and did not interrupt him, which Siri was told afterwards was the most impressive thing she had ever seen a person do.

“The Register costs what a hall costs,” said Bo Tranter, “and it turns out groceries. Every other body in Halversgate that costs money can tell me what it produced. Mrs. Vell has forty names on her waiting list and I can go and stand in her room and look at them. What does this room produce.”

“Eleven names on a Saturday,” said Verity.

“That is not production. That is shopping.”

“No,” said Verity. “It is not.”

* * *

Then Oddie Fennell spoke, because Oddie Fennell was on the Saturday list and had been since her children were born.

She was thirty-one, with her hair up under a scarf, and she had a baby on one hip and a shopping bag in her other hand, and she said, “I did not put in for the Saturday. I put in for Kellan Prior's flour, because he cannot lift the eight. He gave it to me in October and I gave it back to him in November, and he cannot thank me for it, because thanking me would be saying where his flour came from.”

“Who says there is no other room,” said Bo Tranter.

“The room you have just told me is a shop,” said Oddie Fennell.

Nobody said anything for a while.

* * *

They took the papers away and came back on the second Wednesday with the audit, and the audit had been done by two women from the Vestry who were asked to look at hours and who spent nine days in the room with Verity's own notebooks.

She had kept them. That was the thing. Eleven years of them in the same hand on the same blue paper, and the audit found two thousand one hundred and four names, and beside every name there was a thing that had been asked for, and how long it had taken, and whether anybody had to be chased.

The committee wanted a number per job and Verity gave them one. It was not money, because there is no money in it. It was hours. Eleven thousand hours in eleven years, of which she had done two thousand one hundred, and the rest had been done by ninety-one people who had never been paid and had mostly never been thanked.

“There,” said Bo Tranter. “Two thousand one hundred hours. That is one person. Halversgate has thirty-one.”

“It is one person,” said Verity. “That is the number. You have been asking me what it produces and I have been giving you what it costs. It is the same question and you have had it for nine days.”

* * *

The vote was on the fourth Wednesday and it went five to two, and the two were Bo Tranter and a man from the Vestry who thought that two thousand one hundred hours ought to be on a form.

The Register kept its three days, and the rent of the room above the saddler's was found by eleven people putting in a pound each, which Verity did not want and then kept, because she had spent nine days learning that a thing given without a form is the only thing anybody in this town has ever been able to give away.

Siri went back to the Saturday list the week after the vote. Eleven names. Eleven arrivals. She thought of number four and she was at number four. She thought of the shop on Fennel Street. She thought of the top of the stair in Pell Court and she was on the stair with the milk in her hand.

That was the eleventh Saturday and the eleventh address since the January, and when she came down the stair afterwards Verity was standing on the landing with the roster sheet on a clipboard.

“You will want to fill the line in,” said Verity, “so that you know what you have spent.”

Siri opened the notebook. Forty-one lines, forty names, and then a gap.

“You will have to walk me home,” she said. “I do not know which one it was.”

“It is Pell Street,” said Verity. “You have been to it every Saturday since November.”

Siri stood up and went out into Coombe Street beside her with the notebook shut in her hand, and the last line of it was blank.

What the Register does is not rescue anybody. It is that eleven names in alphabetical order is a thing a tired person can be trusted with, and Halversgate has never once been able to say that about itself.
