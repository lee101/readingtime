# The Flame With No Lease

![Cover art: The Flame With No Lease](../art/intermediate/906-the-flame-with-no-lease.webp)

The glass yard and the lamp store keep two books of the same glass and neither of them is the register, and on the fourteenth of December somebody compared them.

The yard’s day book for the second of November, 1902, has one line: Lamp 182, third quay. Chimney out, cracked in the wind, returned to store, 4-8-2. The store’s ledger for the same day has the same line and the same number and one more word, which is in, meaning the glass came back through the counter and onto a shelf and was expected to stay there for as long as the town existed, which is what a shelf in a lamp store is for.

On the fourteenth of December the store ran its yearly reconciliation, which it has done every December since 1841 and which it does in December because the shelves are counted anyway at the turn of the year, and the count came out with one chimney on the account that was not on the shelf it was put on.

And that is a different thing from a chimney that is lost. A store can lose glass. What a store cannot do is lose it and be right, because the number is stamped into the base of the glass when it is blown, and the number 4-8-2 is stamped into the base of every chimney that comes off that bench, and the yard’s day book for the second of November, 1902, is the day the number was last written down by anybody as being on a shelf in this town.

The store clerk wrote four lines on a slip and sent it up, and the slip said: Lamp 182, glass received 1902, glass not on the shelf, lamp not let since 1889, and a glass cannot be on a lamp that is not let, so where is the glass.

* * *

Hettie Rowntree read the slip and then went and got the fifth volume, which is the fourth ward, and leaf ninety-one of it, and read the line, and read it again because it is one line and there is nothing on the leaf above it and nothing on the leaf below it.

Lamp One Hundred and Eighty-Two, at the corner of the third quay by the fish stage. Unlet. The eleventh day of August, eighteen hundred and eighty-nine.

It is a good line. It is in the hand of a clerk who did his work, and it is the whole of the register’s interest in the busiest corner on the longest quay, and it has been the whole of it for fifteen years.

The office does not send a clerk out on a slip of that kind. It sends the slip down to the harbourmaster of the quay the slip is about, with a note asking for an answer in writing by the end of the week, and the note is stamped, and the stamp is the office’s, and the office has never in its life sent a stamped note that produced an answer from a man who is not a clerk.

It sent one on the fifteenth and the answer came back the same afternoon, in Sil Marrow’s own hand, and it is four lines, and it is the only writing about the corner that has been made anywhere in fifteen years.

Corner is lit. Lamp is on the standard. The man has it. Nothing else to report.

* * *

They went out at dusk on the sixteenth because the thing about a lamp is a lamp, and you cannot read a lamp.

The third quay is the working end. The stage is timber, tarred, forty feet of it, and the fish comes up on it at four in the morning and the boats lie alongside it in a swell that comes through the harbour mouth once a minute or so. There are two cranes, a scale, and forty-one boats on the moorings at that time of year, and the harbour wall runs behind the stage with the wind on it, and the lamp standards along the wall are set at a lean because the wall was built in 1861 out of what was to hand.

The corner standard is the last one before the turn, and it is the only one on that stretch with a light in it.

It was a hard little orange point in a large dark, and the halo stood in the wet air off the water, and the glass in it was cracked.

Not crazed. Cracked, from the shoulder down eleven inches, with the crack branching twice, and the branches had been polished smooth with something — a piece of glass, ground flat on a stone, worked back and forth over it by a person with time and no other use for it — and the chimney was in the standard and the base of the chimney was a quarter of an inch under the burner where a hand cannot go.

Hettie had the boy fetch a chair from the chandlery and she went up on it in the wet with her skirt gathered in one hand, and she had the taper from the boy’s box, and she brought the flame of it down under the burner until the number came out of the base in the yellow, and the number came out and she read it, and then she read it again because the number is the whole of this business.

Four, eight, two.

She wrote it in the day book on the spot with the time against it, which is eighteen minutes past five, and the day book does not require a lamp number for a line like that and she put Lamp 182 in anyway.

Hettie got down off the chair with her skirt wet to the knee and did not say anything for a minute.

“Four, eight, two,” she said then.

“That is the store’s glass,” said Sil Marrow. “That is the glass the store took off my counter in November of 1902 and that I carried up myself and put in with a new washer.”

He was on the stage with a ledger of his own under his arm and his bad left shoulder held a little higher than the right, and he had not come down to meet them, and he answered before he was asked, which is a thing he does, and the answer has a time in it and a state of sea in it whether or not anybody asked for either.

“Slack water, quarter past five, and it is a flat sea off the point with a west-north-westerly coming on after midnight,” he said. “I took that chimney off the store shelf on the ninth of December, 1902, being six days after it came in, and I have been taking the bottle to that crack once a winter since, and the store has carried a chimney on its books for two years that has been standing in a fire the whole of the time. The glass is cracked and the standard is old and the stage is tarred timber, and if that chimney goes in the wind it goes into the stage, and there are forty-one boats tied to it, and the fourth ward returns it every year as dark.”

“Your return is right,” said Hettie. “It has been dark since eighteen eighty-nine.”

“It has,” said Sil Marrow. “You may put that in a book.”

* * *

Coll Rayner was on the stage with a line across his knee and a bodkin in his hand, mending a trawl net in the last of the light, and he was sixty-eight and had been going out at four in the morning since he was eleven, and he did not stop mending it.

“He is the holder,” said Sil Marrow. “He has been the holder six years this spring.”

“There is no such thing,” said Hettie. “There has not been a lease on that lamp since eighteen eighty-nine, and a lamp with no lease has no flame, and a flame with no lease is an accident, and an accident is put out inside the quarter hour by whoever gets there, and the register is left with a line that says unlet and the date.”

“That is the whole of the law and I have had it in my head for six years,” said Sil Marrow, “and the law does not have a corner in it. The law has a register and a register has a lamp that is dark, and the corner was dark, and the men come in at four in the morning off the point and there is a mile of wall with nothing on it after the chandlery, and this one had a light in it, and I let him have it, and I let him have it every winter since, and he has never once asked me for a paper.”

Nobody said anything for a moment. A boat came alongside the stage with the swell going through the harbour mouth, and the man in it made the line fast, and did not look up.

“Then you are the letting party,” said Hettie.

“I am the harbourmaster of the third quay,” said Sil Marrow. “Slack water, quarter past five, and a flat sea, and I have not been instructed to do anything.”

* * *

They went back up to the office and Hettie laid out everything the office could do to that lamp on the desk, in the order of the standing instruction of the fourth of January, eighteen sixty-seven, which is four lines and governs everything, and which she could recite.

She laid out four things and then a fifth.

A lease could not be made. A lease is signed in the clerk’s presence and the clerk watches the face, and a lease is entered with the date on which it was signed, and no lease may be entered in this register bearing a date earlier than the day on which it was signed, which is the first of the four and has never been broken and is not going to be broken in December by a clerk who is cold.

A transfer could not be made, because there was no holder to transfer from, and the clause about transfers is explicit that a transfer is made out of a lease and not out of a practice.

A refusal could not be entered, because a refusal is the answer to a lease and there has been no question. The rule that makes a refusal expensive is the rule that makes a refusal possible at all, and where there is no lease there is nothing to refuse and nothing to lose and nothing to write.

And the fifth thing was the one the accident is put out with.

“When an accident is extinguished,” she said, “the register is left with a line that says unlet and the date. That is the fifth rule of this office and it is the reason the day book exists. The quarter hour is for the man with the bucket. The line is for the clerk.”

“Then write the line,” said Pyle, from the end of the table, where he had been standing since he came in with his hat still on.

“The last clause of eighteen sixty-seven,” said Hettie. “A lamp is to be entered as unlet once, and once only, on the day the flame is out, and no second entry is to be made for that lamp while the earlier entry stands.”

She put her finger on leaf ninety-one.

“This lamp has one. The eleventh of August, eighteen eighty-nine. It stands. It is a good entry and it is true and I would not take it out for the whole quay. And so the line the fifth rule requires me to write on Thursday is a second unlet line for a lamp that already has one, and there is no provision in this office for a second, and I am not going to invent one in December and call it a rule of the house.”

Pyle looked at the leaf for a while.

“So it is a fire hazard with a register entry that says the corner is dark, and there is no form for putting it out.”

“There is a form for putting it out,” said Hettie. “There is a bucket and there is a quarter hour and there is a man. There is no form for the fact of it.”

* * *

She told Coll Rayner on the Saturday, at the stage, in the last of the light, and she told it in the order the office keeps it, and she did not do it kindly and she did not do it in a way that would let him be angry instead of accurate.

He listened with the net in his lap and the bodkin still in his hand.

“A flame with no lease is an accident,” she said. “That is not a thing I have found out. It is the fifth rule and it is what the word means. The register has that corner dark since eighteen eighty-nine, and a lamp that is dark has no glass and no burner and no lease, and so the fire in it belongs to nobody, and a thing that belongs to nobody is put out inside the quarter hour by whoever gets there. That is you, if you get there. That is the constable. That is the boy on the round with a bucket.”

“And who gets me another light?”

“The store has four hundred and eleven lamps’ worth of spares in it and not one of them will do it, because the corner is a standard with a hole where a glass goes and there is no lease on the standard to say what the glass is to be, and the yard will not blow a chimney for a lamp that the register says is not there.”

Coll Rayner put the bodkin into the net and pulled it out again.

“Six years,” he said. “I have had a light in that corner every night for six winters and you are telling me it is a boy with a bucket and a form that does not exist.”

“I am telling you that on Friday the twenty-third of December it is to be out by six o’clock, and that you have till then to be at the store for a hand lamp at your own cost, because the store will sell you one and the store does sell them, and because a hand lamp is a thing on a nail and a hand lamp is not on a standard and is not in this book.”

“What is the westerly doing?”

“Westerly, force seven by midnight, and the glass in that standard is cracked through eleven inches and it has been in there two years.”

“Then it will go in the wind and not in a bucket,” said Coll Rayner, “and when it goes it goes into my stage, and the stage is tarred, and there are forty-one boats on it, and I have a boat of my own called the Four Bells with nine weeks of the winter in her. You may write that down or you may not.”

Hettie wrote it down. She wrote it in the day book, in the ordinary column, in the ordinary hand, with a date and an hour, and she read it back to him before she shut it, which is the office way and is also the only way she knows to be exact.

* * *

The week went the way weeks go when the office is waiting on a man to do something it cannot write down.

On the Monday the store sold Coll Rayner a hand lamp, eleven inches of glass, a thumb latch, and a quarter of a gallon of oil, and entered it against no lamp at all, in a book that is not the register, in the column headed sundries, and the boy from the copying desk copied the number out for him because that was one of the things the boy was for.

On the Saturday the fourth ward’s return came up for the year and showed the third quay corner as dark, in a hand that is the same hand that has shown it dark every year since 1889, and the harbourmaster’s own return showed the same, and the store’s return showed the corner with a cracked chimney on it, which is a thing the store had never had cause to show before.

On the Sunday Hettie went through the ward books for six years, month by month, and the corner is in them every month of every year, and the entry is a ruled line with a tick against it, and the tick means no lease, and the tick means dark, and the ward book has been perfectly right for seventy-two consecutive months while a lamp burned at the corner of the third quay through every one of them.

On the Tuesday the fourth ward clerk came in and asked whether he should amend the returns, and Hettie said that a return is made once a year on the state of the lamp at the hour of the return, and that the lamp was dark at the hour of the return in each of those years, and that the six hours between the hour of the return and the hour of the lighting are not in the return and were never asked to be.

On the Wednesday, in the afternoon, Sil Marrow came to the office, which he has not done in eleven years, and stood in the middle of the floor with his ledger under his arm and did not sit down.

“Six years,” he said. “Slack water, and a flat sea, and the whole of the time I have been the only man in Ninehook who knows what is in that corner. If it had been a lease I should have had a paper. If it had been an entry I should have had a line. It was nothing at all, and I have let it six years, and nobody has ever come and said stop.”

“You have been harbourmaster of the third quay for nineteen years,” said Hettie, “and a lease of any lamp in this town is made in this office, and the office does not let a lamp and the harbour does not, and you have let a lamp for six years out of a store shelf.”

“Yes,” said Sil Marrow.

“Then I cannot tell you to stop, because there is nothing to stop. There is no lease and no holder and no paper, and the office records and does not adjudicate, and a thing that is not in the book cannot be instructed out of a man by a clerk with a pen. You will stop on Friday because the office is going to put the fire out, and not because I have told you to, and I am obliged to tell you the difference, because in a year’s time the difference is the whole of what is known about the corner.”

* * *

On the Wednesday morning she was out at the corner before the boats, because a man who has been told that his corner is going dark comes and stands in it, and there is no instrument for that either.

“I could have had a lease,” said Coll Rayner. He was not asking. He had the hand lamp in a bag and the bag at his feet and the corner behind him, with the crack in it and the light going on over his shoulder while he said it. “I have been in and out of that office since I was twenty. There is a door and a clerk and a pen and it takes four minutes. A man in my trade does not need to be asked twice for a thing that costs him nothing and keeps him out of the dark.”

“No,” said Hettie. “You do not, and that is the whole of what has gone wrong with this corner, and I am not going to dress it up as a mystery or as a rogue or as a bad man. There were two documents. One of them said the corner was dark and it was signed and dated and it has been right the whole time. The other one would have said the corner was lit, and nobody ever came and asked for it, and a document that nobody asks for is not a thing that goes wrong. It is a thing that does not exist.”

“And the flame.”

“The flame is the easiest part of it to explain and the hardest part of it to say,” said Hettie. “A lease is a piece of paper that says a flame is owed to a place at an hour. That is all it is. It is an IOU with a street on it. The lamp does not care who holds it and the flame does not care who is fond of it; the flame reads the paper and does what the paper says, and a paper that says a light is owed here at six o’clock on a Tuesday in December is the reason a light is here on a Tuesday in December six hundred Tuesdays later. You have had six winters of that, and none of it was a flame you were keeping. It was a flame with nothing behind it, and it lasted because nothing was ever asked of it that it could refuse.”

Wren Alsop was at the far end of the stage with his back to them, going along the wall counting under his breath the way he does, and got to one hundred and eighty-one, and stopped, and said out loud, “That one is not on my list,” and neither of them answered him, and he went on to one hundred and eighty-three.

* * *

On the Thursday Coll Rayner came to the back door of the office on the quay, at two in the afternoon, with his cap in his hand, and there is a slate by that door and a chalk on a string, and over the lintel there is a sentence cut into the stone in 1861 and it says that the holder is named and the lamp is read, and under the sentence there are forty years of chalk marks, one for each attendance, and none of them explained.

Hettie read him the entry for Lamp One Hundred and Eighty-Two, which is one line, and it took eleven seconds, and at the end of it she said, “That is the whole of it. There is nothing else and there never has been.”

He said, “And the ring.”

“The ring is on the standard.”

“I have never touched it,” he said. “I did not know it was a thing you touched.”

So they went out to the corner at half past five with the tide making and a hard westerly starting to get into the harbour mouth, and Hettie made him stand in front of the standard and she stood behind him and she read the entry again from the beginning, in the open, with the wind taking the words, and then she told him to take the ring.

He put his hand where a hand goes, in the hollow that a hundred and forty years of hands had worn into a brass ring on that standard as on every standard in Ninehook, and it fitted his hand the way it fits everybody’s, and he stood there with it for a minute and a half, in the wind, waiting for something.

The flame did not go out. It did not brighten. It did not do anything at all. It went on burning at the size it was burning at, in a chimney that had been cracked and polished and let alone for six years, in a corner where forty-one boats lay against a tarred stage, and the ring in his hand was a piece of brass on a piece of iron and it was doing precisely what a ring does when there is nothing behind it.

“It is not mine,” said Coll Rayner.

“No,” said Hettie. “It never was. A ring is not a lamp and a lamp is not a ring. The ring is only the handle by which a person takes hold of a thing that is on paper somewhere, and there is nothing on paper, and so you have been taking hold of a handle for six years and keeping a fire in a chimney that the store wrote off in 1902.”

“That is the answer, is it,” he said. “After all that.”

“That is the answer,” said Hettie. “It took the whole of the register to get to, and I read it to you at the back door and again at the standard, and the ring did what it does.”

* * *

On the Friday the westerly came in at four bells with a sea on it, and the store’s cracked chimney went out of the standard at twenty-two minutes past six, and it did not go into the stage, because the stage was eleven feet to the left of the standard and a man had put two coils of hawser on that part of the stage in the afternoon for a reason he could not afterwards explain to anybody.

Constable Iven Pyle was there in nine minutes with a bucket and a hand lamp of his own that is not police issue, and the boy from the copying desk was there with a second bucket because he was on the round and counting, and the fourth ward clerk was there with a form, and the form was put away again unfilled inside four minutes, and the office rule on an accident is the quarter hour, and the flame was out at twenty-eight minutes past six, and the quarter hour had been kept from the time the boy called it, which he did, out loud, at the standard, in the ordinary way he counts things.

Afterwards they stood about in the dark corner for a while, and Sil Marrow checked the tide and said it was making, and did not look at the standard, and the boy said the number of lamps in the fourth ward out loud to get to the end of them, which took a while.

Hettie went back up to the office and ruled the line in the day book for the twenty-third of December, in the column for the hour, and wrote the hour, and in the column beside it she wrote that the fire was out at twenty-eight minutes past six, and that the quarter hour was kept, and that the entry required by the fifth rule was not made, and why, in one line, in the ordinary hand.

Then she put the day book on the shelf where the day book goes.

Volume Five was not opened. Leaf ninety-one was not touched. Lamp One Hundred and Eighty-Two stands in the register of the fourth ward as it has stood since the eleventh of August, eighteen eighty-nine, one line, unlet, and that line is the truest thing in the book about the corner, and it has been true for six of those years as well as for the other nine, and the office has no instrument for the six years and has never claimed one, and the register is not silent because nothing happened.

The thing that happened is on a slip in the store and in a day book on the quay and in a boy’s mouth at the foot of a standard, and it is in all three, and it is in none of the four hundred and eleven.

On the Monday after, the twenty-sixth, a lease was made on Lamp One Hundred and Eighty-Two for the term of the holder’s life, and it was signed at the desk in the presence of a clerk who watched the signatory’s face, and it was entered with the date on which it was signed, and the specification for the glass is a glass to be blown at the point and stamped four, eight, three, and the yard’s next firing is on the nineteenth of January. The margin note against the signature says that the signatory looked at the burner the whole time and did not once look at the paper, and that he asked whether the standard would be painted, and was told that painting a standard is not a thing the register has a column for.

It is a good entry, and it is dated, and it is the only entry this office has ever made about that corner that is entirely in order, and there is nothing in it about the six years.

* * *

On the first night of the new year the corner is dark, and the wind is still westerly, and Coll Rayner goes out at four in the morning with a hand lamp of eleven inches of glass hanging off two fingers of his left hand, which is a thing that cannot be entered and is not entered anywhere.

The boy on the round goes by at six with a taper clipped to the end of a pole carried flat, and the light from the hand lamp is a small hard point in a large dark with its halo caught in the wet air, and the ring on the standard is cold, and he looks at it as he passes, because he cannot stop looking, and he does not write it down.
