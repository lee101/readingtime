# The Map with Twelve Noons

![Cover art: The Map with Twelve Noons](../art/intermediate/22-the-map-with-twelve-noons.webp)

Perrin Vaal could tell you three things about any station on the Kestrel Line: how far it sat from the depot, which side the milk tankers loaded, and the exact minute the Sun stood highest above its tower.

The third one was the whole job. Twelve towers, twelve noons, one brass book.

The charter that built the line said the bell must be rung when the Sun is highest over each tower, so that every year the record would show where the shadows fell. Old Keeper Bram had done it for thirty years with a shadow board: a white plank, a pin driven through the middle, a line scratched true north, and a notch cut so that it met the pin at one moment only.

Perrin was fourteen and the junior keeper, and she had been trusted with the board at two towers. This spring she had the eleventh, and a driver named Oswin Brace, and a courier’s crate lashed behind the coal bunker.

“Two minutes early,” she said. “I marked the board.”

Oswin leaned over her shoulder. The noon mark on the wall still said four minutes later than her notch.

“Then which is the tower’s noon, yours or the wall’s?”

“That is the question.” Perrin wiped her hands on her trousers. Behind them the bell-room window showed a pale sky over a yard full of sleeping coal. “The wall was cut by my grandfather. If the wall is wrong, then the brass book is wrong, and the charter says the Sun decides.”

They caught the first tower, Alder Bridge, at eleven thirty-four. Perrin set a white rod upright in a barrel of sand and put a coin on the tip, and every five minutes she called the time and marked where the shadow fell.

“Forty-nine, long. Fifty-four, shorter. Fifty-nine, shorter still. Oh-four, and that is the one.”

“The shadow moved four inches between fifty-four and oh-four, and not one inch after it,” she said. “That is the Sun standing over the middle.”

“The board on the tower wall says twelve-oh-five, and the board has been wrong by a minute and honest about it for eighty years.” Perrin scratched a line. “The tower stands a little west of the noon line on the old charts, so the Sun crosses the meridian later here than it would at the coast, and the town hall sets its clock by decree. The Sun does not read the decree.”

“Yet the tickets say twelve o’clock.”

“The tickets say twelve o’clock because a hundred clocks in this county have to agree on the same minute, or the milk will stand in the yard.” She looked east along the line, where the ground began to climb toward the Vell Adra border. “Twelve o’clock is a promise people make to each other. Noon is a thing the sky does.”

* * *

They reached Vell Adra at four in the afternoon with the last of the light, and the tower keeper met them at the gate with a lantern and a bad face.

“It’s moved,” he said. “The border moved. Yesterday at midnight the survey posts went up on the west side of the yard, and every clock in Vell Adra is now an hour fast of Kestrel time. Eleven here is twelve there. The decree says the province keeps eastern hours from now on.”

Perrin opened the brass book to the twelfth page and read the noon mark, which had been cut eighty years ago: eleven fifty-eight, and a note in her grandfather’s hand that said the board disagreed by one minute and the sky was right.

“We ship tomorrow at eleven,” Oswin said. “Noon here is one o’clock by the yard clocks. Which one goes in the book?”

“Both, and where the Sun is.” Perrin walked out to the tower. The shadow board was still bolted to the plinth, the notch still cut, and the Sun was still climbing toward that notch exactly as it had been yesterday, and the year before. “The border moved the clocks. It did not move the Sun, and it cannot move this board.”

The keeper laughed once, without much in it. “You’ll find the town cares a great deal. My wife has been timing the bread by the yard clock for thirty years and now the loaves are an hour out.”

“So she starts by the window.” Perrin laid her hand flat on the warm stone. “Same Sun. Same bread.”

* * *

They slept an hour in the depot and came back to the tower at one in the morning, because the harvest decree was signed for two. At one forty the yard was full of lamps and shouting. Farmers wanted the early hour so they could be in the long fields by dawn.

The hands crept to two. The tower keeper put his hand on the rope, waiting for the stroke.

It never came.

Instead, somewhere behind the wall of the belfry, a mechanism knocked twice and gave up, and the hands on the face walked straight from one-fifty-nine to three-oh-one. A chime sounded, bright and wrong.

Three.

The crowd cheered, because the fields were waiting. Perrin did not cheer. She looked at the clock, then at the sky, then at the clock again. The leap had cost no time at all. The hand had simply stepped over two o’clock, and the Sun, which does not read dials, was sitting exactly where it had been a second before and would be a second after. Nothing had been lost from the morning. A name had been lost.

“The Sun moved the same,” she said.

“Miss Vaal.” The keeper’s voice was thin. “The engine wants the board.”

“The engine can wait. Get me a lamp.”

On the board she wrote what she had actually seen: the hand stepping, the Sun standing still, and a day that would carry twenty-three hours on the town clock and the same full turn of the Sun as any other day. Every one of those twenty-three hours had a name. One of them, two o’clock, did not.

“The boy’s draught is timed for two o’clock in this town,” the tower keeper said.

“There is no two o’clock in this town today,” the boy said. “My mother will be sitting up until three.”

“The Sun says two and forty and your mother’s clock says three, and each is right about its own job. You will be at the clinic at three, and the label will say three, and I will be standing beside you saying three, and if anybody asks, three is what happened.” She turned the page and wrote under the hour: NO CLOCK IN THIS TOWN READ 2:00 THIS MORNING. The hour happened. Its name did not.

The engine took the noon mark an hour later than her grandfather’s board said. Perrin cut a new notch anyway, in the old place, and rang the bell when the shadow was shortest, and the bell sounded wrong against every clock in the yard, and it did not matter.

* * *

On the way home the province east of the border turned its clocks back an hour, so that two became one again and the hour from one to two happened twice.

They were on the eleven o’clock westbound, standing at the depot platform, when Oswin’s assistant came running along the line with a lantern and a shout.

“Water stop at Corbel! The milk stop! It’s booked for half past one, and the driver says it’s booked for half past one, and the dairy says the second one means the late delivery, and the late delivery is what we promised the creamery!”

Perrin took the lantern. “Which one was it the first time?”

The boy blinked. “How would anybody know?”

“Because we mark it.” She fetched the brass book and the chalk stub from her coat. On the platform she drew two small clocks, side by side, and on the face of the first she wrote 1:30 and on the face of the second she wrote 1:30, and above the first she wrote FIRST TURN OF THE HAND and above the second she wrote SECOND TURN. Then she copied the line into the book twice, and the creamery’s book, and the dairy’s book, and the milk driver’s.

“You will say first turn and second turn now,” she said. “Not half past one. The hour repeated, so the hour gets two names, and now nobody can arrive at the wrong one.”

The assistant looked at the two little clocks for a long moment.

“That is more chalk than the answer needs.”

“The answer needs less. People need more.” She closed the book. “That is why the book exists.”

* * *

They reached the eleventh tower at ten the next morning, and Perrin went up alone to cut the mark.

She set the rod in the sand. At eleven forty the shadow was long. At fifty-five it was shorter. At twelve-oh-three the coin sat under the rod, and she could not have told it from the notch at a foot away.

She cut a new notch, and a new number, and rang the bell.

Across the fields to the east, ninety miles off, the twelfth tower’s bell had already rung. Every degree east puts its noon four minutes earlier, so Vell Adra’s Sun stood over its meridian ten minutes before this one did, and by the time Perrin’s bell was in the air the shadow there had been at its shortest since morning. Twelve towers, and the Sun had crossed them all in one turn of the world, at twelve different minutes, and not one of those minutes was anybody’s decision.

Bram found her at the board with the ledger under his arm. “You will need more than one column.”

“I have three.” She turned the page for him. Zone, clock, Sun. “Any time on this line can be written three ways, and a timetable that says only one of them is not a timetable. It is a rumour.”

He looked a long while. Then he put his thumb on the last line, Vell Adra, and the note under it about the morning that had no hour in it, and the two clocks drawn side by side on the platform at Corbel.

“Twelve noons,” he said.

“Twelve noons,” Perrin agreed, “and two that happened twice, one whose hour was never named, and a border that walked across a yard in the night.” She closed the ledger. “The bells are the easy part. It is the writing that keeps a railway from arriving in the wrong minute.”
