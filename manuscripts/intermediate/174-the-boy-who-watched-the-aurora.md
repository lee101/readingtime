# The Boy Who Watched the Aurora

![Cover art: The Boy Who Watched the Aurora](../art/intermediate/174-the-boy-who-watched-the-aurora.webp)

The logbook said the lights go green before they go red, and that green is the fast one, so you can tell which half of the night you are standing in by the colour alone. Håkon Sandvik was fourteen and he kept the logbook for his brother Sander's vans, and that sentence was in his grandfather's handwriting on the inside cover, and he had read it two hundred and eleven times.

On the fourteenth of February the van had four people in it, and the forecast said three out of five, which is a number about the whole of the Earth and not about Kvaløya. Getting in at Prestvannet, Håkon said three out of five had been the forecast on nine of the last twelve nights and had been worth nothing on six of them.

“Two minutes down the page,” said Marion Huse, who was a nurse and had taken the night off.

“Sit down,” said Sander, and started the engine, and they went out along the road that climbs the west side of the island toward Stordalen with the sea on their left and the dark coming down behind them.

* * *

At twenty past eight they were parked on the gravel shelf above the old farm at Stordalen and the sky had a low red band sitting on the northern ridge, soft at the edges, no rays in it at all, a colour more than a light. It ran fifteen degrees along the ridge and it did not move.

“That is the start,” said Sander. “That is the first one. Give it ten minutes.”

Håkon wrote nothing in the van's columns. He wrote in the column for seconds, which was his own, three rules wide, and which he had started in the September.

The band had been there at eight o'clock, low in the north, and it had been there when they turned onto the Kvaløya road. It had not brightened and had not developed the vertical streaks that mean the fast thing is happening, and now it was going grey along the bottom, which is what a red band does when the thin air that makes it red runs out.

“Green before red,” said Sander, to the van in general.

“That is in the logbook,” said Håkon.

“It is. And there is the first one.”

* * *

He turned the notebook round so his brother could see it, open at the back past the columns for weather.

Sixty-one displays were written in that log with a start time and a finish time and a colour. In thirty-four the red came first, in nineteen the green came first, and in eight he had written both at the same minute and left it, because he had not trusted himself.

“There is no green first thirty-four times out of sixty-one,” said Håkon. “It is not a rule. It is one night.”

Sander looked at the page with the heater going.

“Night is when it started.”

“Night is when you wrote it down. That is all anybody wrote down. You wrote down green when you got there.”

The band over the ridge went from red to a colour with no name in it, and the heater ticked.

* * *

So Håkon did the other thing, the one he had been doing for two years because it needed nobody to agree with him. He set the finder on the ridge line so two marks lay in the frame together, a lattice mast on the old farm and a notch in the hill above it, and started the stopwatch when a bright piece of the band came in on the mast and stopped it when the same piece came in on the notch.

He did it eleven times. The slowest was fifty-one seconds, the fastest thirty-four.

He wrote thirty-four in the column and closed the notebook and said nothing, because a band moving that slowly has an hour left in it and not three, and there was nothing to be done about that tonight.

* * *

“No,” said Håkon.

Sander turned round in the driver's seat.

“We go back. We go to the harbour and I take some bearings off the ridge and we come up here again. Forty minutes on the road, two litres of diesel, and the four of them have had a red band.”

“That is four of them,” said Petter Aud, from the back.

“And three thousand four hundred,” said Håkon. “I am not saying it for the diesel. I am saying it because I am wrong as often as I am right and tonight I am wrong.”

Sander Sandvik was twenty-seven and had run the van for six winters and had never once lost a night on the strength of a number out of a notebook. He looked at his brother, then at the four of them, then at the ridge.

“Do it,” he said.

* * *

They were back on the gravel shelf at half past nine, and the log for the fourteenth says start 21:44, finish 00:07, both colours, and the word breaking, and the seconds in his own column were 9, 11, 8, then a run of numbers between eleven and fourteen for two and a half hours, then 30 and 34 and 38, and by midnight there was nothing in the frame at all.

Torstein Vold was sixty-two and had come up in February for eleven years and had never once seen the whole sky do it. He put his hand flat on the roof.

“You could have gone home,” Marion Huse said to Håkon on the drive down at one in the morning. He said that was the plan. She said, “Good,” and asked nothing else.

The van's page for that day says one night, four seats, three thousand four hundred kroner, seventy-six kilometres, and six point eight litres of diesel.

* * *

The bearings were the part he had been after since the September, and he did them properly, which meant a whole degree and not to the nearest five, three readings of each, averaged.

Three things were in the frame at the harbour. The notch below the summit of Tromsdalstinden. The foot of the green, where it came down onto the ridge of Kvaløya opposite. The foot of the red, which sat lower and further left.

He wrote the numbers, drove the thirty-eight kilometres up, took the same three off the gravel shelf, and did the whole thing in the head of the van with the notebook flat on his knee and the map light off.

The notch swung one hundred and seventy-six degrees, from three hundred and eleven at the harbour to a hundred and twenty-seven at Stordalen, which is what happens to a peak twenty-five kilometres away when you drive thirty-eight kilometres past it.

The foot of the green swung five degrees, from eight to thirteen.

That was all of it, and it took him nine minutes with no light on his hands. A thing twenty-five kilometres away swinging one hundred and seventy-six degrees means the far thing has to swing about five, because both must account for the same change of direction. Five is thirty-five point two times smaller, so the far thing is that much further away.

Twenty-five times thirty-five point two. He wrote eight hundred and eighty on the back page and put a ring round it, because eight hundred and eighty is the distance from Tromsø to Kirkenes and a bit more, and his aunt lives past the fjord on that road.

Then the height, off the elevation. The foot of the green sat thirteen degrees up.

Eight hundred and eighty times the tangent of thirteen degrees.

Two hundred and three.

He looked at that for a while. Two hundred and three kilometres is higher than any aircraft goes, and he could not think of anything that lived up there, and then he could think of two things, which were weather aeroplanes and this, and he knew one of them was not.

* * *

Sander did not believe him for three days, and not because the arithmetic was wrong. The logbook said green was the fast one, so the slow red should be further still, and Håkon had the red swinging two and a half degrees where the green swung five, which put it one thousand seven hundred and sixty kilometres away and two hundred and thirty up. The two came out nearly on top of each other, and that was a fact about his night and not about the sky.

So he went out again on the sixteenth, and again on the seventeenth, and on each night the notch swung one hundred and seventy-six degrees again, and the green swung five, then six point one, then six point eight.

Six point one gave twenty-five times one hundred and seventy-six over six point one, which is seven hundred and twenty-one kilometres, and the foot at twelve point two degrees, which is one hundred and fifty-six.

Six point eight gave six hundred and forty-seven kilometres, and the foot at nine point six degrees, which is one hundred and nine.

Then he wrote the three in a row and looked at them, and the difference was forty-seven both times, and he could do a fourth, and the fourth was sixty-two, and the fifth was fifteen.

* * *

Olav Sandvik was eighty-one and lived two streets down and came to the workshop on the twentieth with his coat on. Håkon had the notebook open on the bench with the three numbers in a row.

“Green is the fast one,” said his grandfather.

“No. Look at the page where you started counting. Thirty-four, nineteen, eight. You did not write green first thirty-four times out of sixty-one.”

His grandfather read the column with a finger on the row, the way people read a page they have already read.

“Then who says it is a rule.”

Håkon turned the notebook to the back page and put the stopwatch down beside it, and talked his grandfather through the thirty-five point two, and then told him the thing he had known before he drove out, which was that the foot of the green had not been in the same place on the two nights and he could not prove it was the same piece of sky.

“So you have one number that is too big and a reason.”

“I have one number that is too big and a reason and no way to take the reason out. On the fourteenth I had the mast and the notch and eleven seconds and I was wrong, and tonight the same two marks and I am wrong in a different way, and both times I wrote it down.”

* * *

So they took the sentence off the inside cover. That was the last time Håkon saw his grandfather in the workshop. The ink came off with a cloth and one word came off harder than the rest, and the cover read The aurora log, Tromsø, 1978 to, and under it, in fresh ink, two columns and no sentence.

On the twenty-first the green swung seven point four, which put the foot at five hundred and ninety-five kilometres and eight point nine degrees, and that gave ninety-three. He had predicted sixty-two on a piece of paper on the seventeenth and wrote both numbers side by side and put no ring on either, because forty-seven had held three times and then it did not hold, and a thing that holds three times is not a law. It is a coincidence with good handwriting.

What was worth writing down was the direction. The number was falling, and the true one was somewhere under it, and the only way to get at it was the night somebody stands at the harbour with a stopwatch while somebody else stands at Stordalen with a stopwatch, in the same minute, watching the same piece of green, so that the moving is in both numbers instead of one.

There was one other person in Tromsø awake and looking up that night, and he had been driving.

Håkon closed the logbook and put it on the bench above the heater where it had always lived. He did not have a number that was right. He had ninety-three, falling, with a difference that had been forty-seven twice and then was not forty-seven, and a rule in his grandfather's hand that had been true thirty-four times out of sixty-one, and a way of being wrong that he could write down.
