# The River of Names at Low Tide

![Cover art: The River of Names at Low Tide](../art/intermediate/28-the-river-of-names-at-low-tide.webp)

Vale clipped her brass tide compass to her yellow oilskin coat. At sixteen, she was the estuary’s youngest tidal cartographer, though she had no place on the Survey Bench. Surveyor Oren Kest, a broad man in a red cap, kept it. He checked and stamped her maps, calling her “half a cartographer” when market crews crowded his table.

She poled past Low Wharf toward register stones that guided boats through the estuary’s shifting channels. Nine stood along the old civic boundary. Ten waited beneath the mud at North Sluice.

Today was the lowest tide of the season.

The water drew back from the market steps, exposing brown slopes, blue mussels, and old wheel ruts. A knot of herons lifted from the mud. Vale checked the compass twice. The needle rested on the silver line marked LOW.

“No higher than this,” she murmured.

The first register stone showed a blank face. So did the second. On the third, wet sand surrounded letters that had not been there the day before.

VALE SAYER

Vale’s skiff struck a pale root. She leaned forward, nearly spilling her chart across the water.

That was her name. Not a dead name or an old family name. Her name.

She touched the letters. Cold water filled the grooves. Her compass needle spun, then stopped at a mark used for danger.

Vale recorded the time in her field book, made a rubbing with paper and charcoal, sealed it in her map tube, and rowed for the survey office.

Surveyor Kest was already in a meeting with Reeve Annet Pell. Vale laid the rubbing on the table.

“The register stone is sounding my name.”

Pell had silver pins in her short black hair and a seal ring on one hand. She leaned close. “Every low tide, it speaks.”

“Not mine. It should be blank.”

“That is not the same thing.”

Vale opened her survey grid. Stone positions formed a line across the channel. The line bent around the buried register at North Sluice. When she marked the bend, every known name vanished. The stone remained.

“It is not a list of citizens,” Vale said. “It is a correction.”

Kest studied the paper. “Then North Sluice needs a survey.”

“Before tonight’s turning tide.”

The reeve fastened her cloak. “You have six hours.”

At the eastern point, Vale found more marks.

SERA QUIN

MILO DANN

ISA VEY

Names surfaced on the three stones nearest the old ropeworks. At the western point, two more appeared on separate blocks: OREN KEST and ANNET PELL. Vale photographed the inscriptions with her box camera, using its timer while the stones sat beneath the cloudy sky. When she unfolded the survey sheet, a final name had been added in pale wet lines.

EDA RILL

Vale’s grandmother had chosen Eda Rill as her name after the flood. She told Vale she kept Rill in memory of a stranger who drowned that night. She never explained why her house was called Rill Cottage, though the name was worn above the door.

Vale rowed to Rill Cottage. The windows were shut, the chimney cold, and a mourning ribbon hung from the gate. Her grandmother had died three weeks before.

On the workbench, Vale found a waxed packet marked DO NOT MAP. Inside were a survey key, a council notice, and a letter.

Vale read it at the table, keeping one hand on the compass.

Eda wrote that she had served the eastern gate during the Night of High Water, seventy-one years ago. Afterward, the Crown Salt Office removed seven gatekeepers’ names so it could deny that its floodgates had been opened by command. The deleted became “missing volunteers,” and their families lost wages, pensions, and proof of courage.

Eda was not one of the seven. She carried their warning from the sluice house. Reeve Pell’s grandfather, the office chief, made her sign a false delivery record. If she refused, he said he would register her as a gatekeeper and erase her too. The office then erased her as an unreliable witness, though she had been their warning bearer.

“I signed so three names would remain somewhere else,” the letter said. “I did not know he would take the ledger itself. My hidden copy survived because a rope keeper hid it inside a buoy.”

The letter warned Vale not to claim the old honor for the family if Eda’s name rose from the mud. A rescued truth and a stolen name were not the same.

Vale folded the letter.

Across the room, a small blue cup stood on the shelf. Eda had floated keys, seeds, and notes on it during floods. Beneath it, Vale found a dry roll of paper.

The map was old but clear. It showed North Sluice, the register stones, and a buried buoy station east of the ropeworks. Seven lines led from the gatehouses to the station. Beside one line, Eda had written DELIVERED BY HAND.

Vale compared the map with her new survey. The buoy station stood where the name-stones made their sharpest turn. A low-tide reading from Eda’s notebook gave the depth needed to find the door. Vale marked a route.

A knock shook the cottage. Reeve Pell entered with Kest and River Marshal Iven Shore. Shore wore a blue coat fastened with silver shell buttons. He studied the letter without touching it.

“The council will seal the stones before sunset,” Pell said. “Public fear is not irrational.”

“Eda’s name is not public property,” Vale replied.

Kest pointed to the old map. “The buoy could hold evidence. It also sits in the fastest current. One wrong meter could take the skiff apart.”

Vale checked her compass. “Then we measure twice.”

They launched two flat-bottomed survey boats. Vale and Shore took the eastern skiff. Kest and two river officers worked from the western punt, dragging a marked rope across the channel. At each rope number, Vale lowered her marked survey rod, called the depth, and recorded it in Vale’s even columns.

The first line led to an iron ring buried in mud. The second ended beneath a fallen beam. The third pulled tight around a shape rising from the water.

It was the buoy: a black cylinder with a copper roof and seven iron hooks.

The tide began to turn earlier than the tables predicted. A thin foam line showed the flood racing along the western shallows. The buoy tilted, caught the survey rope, and dragged the punt toward the main channel.

“Cut the rope!” Vale called.

Kest reached for his knife, but the rope had wound around the buoy’s lower hook. The punt swung broadside. A river officer dropped to his knees and caught the mooring post.

Vale poled her skiff alongside. She did not pull with brute strength. She studied the rope’s angle and read its movement against her compass. “Slack on two. Hold on one. Turn together.”

They loosened the line, turned the punt, and freed the post. The buoy rolled upright with a dull clang. Vale caught its copper handle while Shore held the skiff steady.

Inside the buoy was the missing ledger, protected by a sleeve of oiled canvas. Seven gatekeeper signatures covered the last page. Beside them stood an order from Reeve Pell’s grandfather to erase the names after the flood.

Vale felt anger tighten under her ribs. Anger could wait. The rising water could not.

On the outer stones, new names had begun to fade. As the tide covered them, their letters softened. Some would vanish before dawn. Vale photographed each one and copied every mark. She noticed something else. One of the seven old gate marks near the ropeworks carried neat double lines. The other six had none.

“Do those marks mean something?” Kest asked.

“Eda’s buoy keeper used paired reeds to record a voluntary handoff,” Vale said. “One gatekeeper shed his post with witnesses. Six were forcibly erased and had no pair.”

“Shed names are the same as stolen ones when the public sees them,” Pell said.

“No. They are not.”

At that moment, the buried register shifted beneath North Sluice. A crack raced through the old lock wall. Water began to turn toward the lower market, where three streets sat below the ordinary high-water mark.

Vale looked at the survey lines. The name-stones formed both a warning and a map. Seven missing points had broken the old chain of measurements. A stable route existed only if the survey restored every gate position to the public channel.

“Shore, can the market wall hold?”

“Until midnight if we close the food-market sluice.”

“Close it.”

“The sluice key is inside North Lock. No one can reach it now.”

Vale pointed toward the buoy station. “The buoy crane reaches.”

They secured the ledger inside Vale’s dry map tube. Shore and Vale used the copper frame as a stepping platform while the river officers worked the crane rope. The lock door was slick, and the current pushed hard. Vale tied two short safety lines to her belt, one for Shore and one for the nearest piling. At the crane wheel, she found the sluice handle jammed by a broken tooth.

Kest examined it. “It needs a lever.”

Vale’s survey rod was strong enough. She braced it between the wheel teeth. Everyone leaned together. The handle moved, and the food-market sluice dropped. The diverted current swung toward the empty saltmarsh. Water struck the old stone bends without entering the lower streets.

The turning tide had not stopped, but the danger passed.

Vale returned the ledger to Reeve Pell at sunset with Eda’s buoy evidence, the double cut, and seven family accounts from the Night of High Water. Six matched the ledger. The seventh matched an old signature Dala Frost’s granddaughter identified.

She explained that Dala had left his gate post with a woman he loved and requested a new name and civic record. His descendants knew the story. He had asked that his old post name never be used again, yet a historian had published it without consent two years ago.

The council nearly split over him.

Vale stood before the full assembly the next morning in her dry blue coat. Her grandmother’s folded letter lay beside the ledger. She did not hide the family connection.

“Eda Rill told me the river remembered what powerful people preferred to forget,” Vale said. “She was partly right. The stones speak the names the registry tries to remove or bend. They do not carry consent. That part belongs to people.”

Pell asked what Vale wanted the council to inscribe.

“Not one verdict for every name. Restore the six forcibly erased gatekeepers. Record Dala Frost as a protected shed identity, and restore Eda Rill as an erased warning bearer. Open the letters of people who have consented to speak. Close the private records of those who have not.”

A dockworker objected that the second rule protected offenders.

“So will witnesses and descendants,” Vale said. “The old system punished them with the erased.”

The assembly approved the distinctions, but a vote to display every recovered name narrowly failed. Families of shed identities would receive private notice, and the public record would distinguish verified erasures from sealed choices.

Before sunset, Vale returned to North Sluice. The exposed stones stood above the mud, their letters sharp in the cold air. She did not carve any of them. Instead, workers reset the seven missing survey markers according to Eda’s recovered coordinates.

The chain of measurements closed.

When water returned to the market steps, the river turned exactly where the charts promised. Vale’s compass needle rested on its ordinary mark.

Her grandmother’s name was restored on the civic wall as a warning bearer, not a gatekeeper. Vale did not add the Sayer line to the old gate roll. She kept her own name, Vale. A chosen name deserved the same respect as a name taken by force.

That evening, Vale hung Eda’s brass survey key above her desk and placed the new map beneath it. The estuary looked unchanged, but every safe channel now carried a truth that had nearly been lost.

Vale understood a boundary could be a lock, a wall, or a lie repeated until it became solid. The work of a cartographer was to measure such things accurately.

The river rose around the old stones. The names remained.
