# The Sidekick Who Kept the Records

![Cover art: The Sidekick Who Kept the Records](../art/intermediate/139-the-sidekick-who-kept-the-records.webp)

Every person in Halversgate who can do something impossible has, somewhere, an archivist. The office is two rooms above the print works on Vellum Lane, and it is open on Wednesday and Saturday, and anybody may come in and read anything, provided they do not take it out.

The rule is one sentence long and it is painted on the door frame: if it is not written down it did not happen. That is not a motto. In that office it is a description of what a town is made of, and the archivist before Wren Dell said it, and Wren's own teacher said it, and nobody in the office has ever found the sentence to be too strong.

Wren Dell was seventeen. Her one useful ability was recall. Anything she had ever read she could bring back exactly, in the order she had read it, whenever she wanted it. Nothing else stuck. A thing she saw and did not read went out of her head inside the hour, and the price of the recall was metered: for every hour the cabinet stood open she lost one hour of her own day, and it went whether or not she found anything, and it could not be put back by writing it down afterwards.

She had kept her own life on paper since she was nine. It was in a shoebox on the top shelf of the cabinet and it was the only file in the office that was not about somebody else.

* * *

There were four subjects.

The first was Kellan Roome, called the Lampman, who could take a fire in his two hands and put it out by closing them. The second was Siri Fenwick, who could put her palm flat on a road and know what had stood there before it was a road. The third was Tuppence Loe, who kept the signal lamp in the water tower on the hill and could hold the light in it for as long as somebody was looking at it. The fourth was Pell Nabb, who could carry twice his own weight for eleven minutes.

Wren knew what each of them cost and had written all four down, because the office does not keep abilities. It keeps hours.

Kellan Roome's column said that every fire he put out took a room out of the house he was born in, and he was born in a house on Gaunt Street with eleven rooms, and there were nine left.

Siri Fenwick's column said that every road she listened to she could never find again, in her own town, on her own foot, for the rest of her life.

Tuppence Loe's column said that every hour her light held went out of one eye of hers, left before right, and she had a card in the front of the file with the count on it in her own hand, and the count was on the left of the two lines.

Pell Nabb's column said eleven minutes, and after the eleven minutes he could not lift his own boots off, ever again, and he was twenty-nine and could not lift his own boots off.

Wren had four files on the shelf in front of the desk and nine years of them in each, and the shelf was not the office. It was her name on the list, because somebody had to be second in the room when the fire was in the fireman's hands.

* * *

A Saturday in the office was eleven entries, and most of them were nothing.

Kellan Roome's file for the second Saturday in the March said: called seven forty, Tannery Lane, out by seven fifty-one, nobody hurt, two rooms gone at the Gaunt Street end, left the lamp on the step afterwards because Mrs. Ibbotson likes it. Two hundred and forty words. It took her forty minutes to file it and she got two of those hours back in the evening, which was not a thing anybody noticed but which she had written on the sheet and did.

Siri Fenwick's file for the same week said: four hours, Mill Lane, the ford, a set of stepping stones under the silt, a boundary wall about six feet back and running east. She had been on the ford a hundred times and could not get to it now. That was the price and it was already paid and it took ninety seconds to write down.

Tuppence Loe's file said: light held three hours and forty, watched by nine from the towpath and a car on the top road, none of them knew they were watching. Tuppence had done it three times that week. It was the first thing in Halversgate that anybody could pay for and nobody had ever thought of buying.

Pell Nabb's file said: no call.

The rule about no call was the hardest one, because the office also keeps what did not happen, and a man who cannot lift his boots cannot carry anything, and a fireman who cannot carry anything is a man standing in a doorway telling people how they go out.

* * *

She went back through the nine years in the January because the Vestry had written to ask for a list of the costs, in order, with dates, for a report.

Nine years is eleven files a month for Kellan Roome. Wren had done it before, for the insurance book, and she had done it in the order of the years and never in the order of the man.

On the eleventh of the fourth month of the year before last there was an entry that stopped her.

CALLED NINE TEN, ROPE WORKS, OUT NINE EIGHTEEN. PUT OUT BY VOLUNTEER OF THE BRIGADE, ROTA THREE. THE BRIGADE FUND PAID ONE SHILLING TO ROTA THREE.

Rota three was Pell Nabb. Rota three was one shilling, and one shilling in that winter was a week of bread and coal, and the brigades had been stood down in the autumn and the fund had eleven calls in it all year.

Wren checked the original sheet in the pocket at the back of the file, which is where the sheet that was actually written on the night lives, and the original said PUT OUT BY VOLUNTEER OF THE BRIGADE, ROTA THREE, in Pell Nabb's own hand, and Pell Nabb's hand had nothing wrong with it.

Kellan Roome had put that fire out with his own hands at nine ten, standing in the doorway of the rope works, and had told the office that a man called Pell Nabb had done it, and had paid the man for it out of a fund with nothing in it.

The second was eleven months later, at the tannery, and it was the same sentence with rota five on it, and rota five was a young man called Oswin Trove who had left the town in the spring and could not have put a fire out anywhere.

* * *

She went and found Pell Nabb on a Saturday, at the lock-up yard behind the rope works, where he does the brigade accounts on a bench in the open because the office has no window.

He is twenty-nine, with a red cap and a bad left shoulder, and he lifted two sacks of coal off the bench one at a time while she stood there, which he cannot do, and he put them down and looked at her.

“You're the file,” he said.

“Two entries,” said Wren. “Both in your name. Both paid.”

“Fourteen in my name over the nine years,” said Pell Nabb. “You have found the two that are interesting.”

He did not say anything else about it for a while, and then he said, “The fund had nothing in it. He paid them out of his own wage and told me to say I had done the work, because I had a bad shoulder and a name on a list and the Vestry will not put a man on a list twice unless there is a call against his name.”

Wren wrote that down. It went in, in the same hand, on a new line, under the original.

“And the second one,” she said. “Oswin Trove had left the town.”

“Oswin Trove never had anything to do with it,” said Pell Nabb. “Kellan used him because Oswin had signed a sheet and Kellan knew the sheet was in the file and the sheet was in the office for nine years. Kellan Roome is the only man in Halversgate who knows what is in a file.”

* * *

The town had one reading a year, in the Assembly Room, and it was the reading of one file, chosen by the archivist, and the room held two hundred and nobody spoke.

She had read Kellan Roome's file before. She had read it four times and had been asked about the lamp he leaves on steps. In Halversgate there is a printed sheet about him called the Lampman, made by two boys who print it themselves and sell it at the quay, and it has been printed since Wren was nine, and it has a drawing in it of a man in a yellow coat holding a fire in one hand, and the fire is drawn as if it were a bird.

One of the two boys who print it calls her the sidekick. He said it out loud at the back of the Assembly Room last year, in front of two hundred people, and meant it kindly, and she has never corrected him, and the word is not wrong, because the whole of what she has ever done has been to write down what somebody else did.

Wren had always known the file was not the whole of him. She had nine years of it and it was a man described from the outside, in hours, in four lines a week, and he had never once come in and asked to correct anything, and she had decided at twelve that this was because he did not want to be looked at.

On the twenty-first of the February she read the file in the Assembly Room, from the top of the nine years, page by page, with the room quiet.

She read the nine ten call at the rope works as it stands, with rota three on it.

Then she read the entry she had added on the eighth of the January, in her own hand, under the original, which said that the fire was put out by Kellan Roome at nine ten with his own hands, that no volunteer attended, that the brigades had been stood down, that the one shilling was paid out of the wage of Kellan Roome, and that rota three was Pell Nabb, who was at home that night with a bad shoulder, and that the account had been kept and was in the file.

She read the tannery the same way. She read the original and then the new line, and she said both, in that order, both times, and she did not say which was the lie, because the file does not say either. The file says what was written, and then it says what else was written, and the archivist's rule is that both of them happened, because both of them are on paper.

It took an hour and forty minutes and she lost the hour and forty minutes out of the day she was standing in.

Kellan Roome was at the back on the aisle. He was sixty-one and grey and he wore the yellow coat, and he did not stand up and he did not interrupt her once, and she did not look at him until the reading was finished.

Afterwards she went and put the file back.

* * *

She had been putting files back for nine years and she could put one back in eleven seconds with her eyes shut.

She put it in on the shelf, and then she stood with her hand on the drawer, and then she took it out again and put it back with the front cover open, the way it had been kept for nine years with the first page showing, and she left the drawer where it was.

She had two hours of that afternoon left and she did not know it. She knew the walk home and she knew the key and she knew that she had eaten, because it was on the sheet, and she stood in the yard behind the print works for a while trying to put the middle of the day together and could not do it, and then she went and washed her hands in the yard tap because her hands were grey from the paper, and the drawer upstairs was still open with the first page of Kellan Roome showing, and the light from the window went across it until the light went.
