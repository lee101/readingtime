# The Boy Who Won the Winter Games

![Cover art: The Boy Who Won the Winter Games](../art/intermediate/87-the-boy-who-won-the-winter-games.webp)

The wreath belonged to the champion, and the champion was decided before the wreath was cut, and the year they changed the bracken it came down out of a different dale entirely, so by the time it reached Craigmoor it was two sizes too big and there was no sending back for another.

Ewan had not wanted to enter. His gran had entered him on the eighth of December, in pencil, in the back of the ledger, and told him on the Tuesday, which gave him four days to get used to the idea.

There were nine events. The Bog, the Saw, the Candle, the Roost, the Ladder, the Kettle, the Sled, the Nine, and the Bell, which was last, and which everybody knew was the one that decided it.

* * *

Kieran McGrath won the first one before breakfast and the crowd did not seem to notice, because they had seen it before.

He was sixteen and he was the town’s champion, and the town had arranged this for a very long time. His sled went into the smithy on the Monday and came out on the Tuesday with new irons and nobody charged his family a penny, and the paper wrote four lines about the irons, and the crowd clapped the mending. His boots were done by a cobbler in the Square whose name was also McGrath, and the crowd clapped that too. When he lopped the Candle at eleven o’clock, the applause went on eleven seconds, and Ewan counted it, and thought about those eleven seconds for a long time afterwards.

Nobody had told Ewan to run fast. That was the part he could not get over. He ran the Bog in third and the Saw in second, and at the bottom of the Ladder steps a man he did not know put a hand on his shoulder and said, well, that is the boy, and meant something entirely different by it.

By the kettle his gran was the only person in the stand who knew his number.

* * *

The Bell is up Fish Ladder to the mill, two hundred steps cut into the rock, and the winner is the boy whose rope pulls the clapper first. The miller times it and the crowd counts it out loud, and by the time the two of them come back down the whole of Craigmoor has said the same number at the same time.

Ewan went first, because he was third in the Sled and it fell that way. Kieran went third.

Ewan did not know he had won until the miller said it, because the bell is behind the mill wall and you hear it through nine inches of oak.

“A quarter,” the miller said. “A quarter of a second.”

The Square went quiet in the way a place does when everybody turns to see who it is about.

Then it did not go quiet. It went the other way. The woman at the fish counter gave Ewan her wrong change and did not apologise. A man in the front row said, out loud, in Ewan’s hearing, that you wait and see, and he said it kindly, and that was the part Ewan could not put down. The paper came out on the Friday with CHAMPION in a different typeface and no photograph, because the photograph had been arranged on Wednesday for a man of sixteen.

Kieran found him behind the hall with the wreath in his hands.

“That was a good race,” he said, and he said it well, in the voice of a man reading out a result.

“Thanks.”

“You ran the Ladder better than I have ever run it.”

“Thanks.”

Kieran looked at the wreath, and then at the ground, and Ewan saw him reach a decision about something and take it, and the taking of it was the only real thing that happened to him that day.

“Come here, though.” He held out his hand. “Shake on it, so there is no arguing about it later.”

* * *

The ledger lived in Gran’s kitchen in a tin, and it had outlasted two of the houses it had been carried between. Mrs Fenwick had kept it for nineteen years and her husband had kept it for thirty-one before her, and the rule of the house was that whoever kept it was called the keeper and the keeper wrote true.

It was ruled in four columns. YEAR. CHAMPION. MARGIN. KEEPER. The first page of the MacGrath run went in 1888, and before that there were three different families in the CHAMPION column within nine years, and before that the column was empty for a long stretch and the whole thing began in the middle of a page in handwriting so small Ewan had to put the book on the sill.

“It says one family since 1888,” he said. “That is your family.”

“It is Kieran’s family.”

“It is your family now, though. You keep it.”

Gran turned her tea cup round on its saucer, which she did instead of saying a thing she did not agree with. “Then what are the Games for, Ewan? Because if one family has had the thing since your gran’s mother was a girl, then the town is not having a competition. The town is having a custom, and customs are for something.”

“I do not know,” Ewan said. “Ask him.” He meant the man in the front row with the scarf, eighty-nine or so, who had not moved out of the front row since Ewan could remember being in it.

* * *

The front-row man was called Tam, and he had been nine years old in 1887.

“The Games are a winter,” he said.

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the only one I have got that is any good.” He turned the cuff of his coat over. “January of 1887 was the coldest anyone living remembers. The peat road was under nine feet of drift. There was no coal in this parish and none coming, and the men of Craigmoor decided in the second week that if they did not get the road open the carts would not run in the spring, and the whole town would be a place people had been to.”

“How long did it take?”

“Nine days. Nine days, and no money in it, and the miller kept the tally on his wall, and half the men were down with the cold after the fourth day and went back out on the fifth.” He turned his cuff back. “And then the spring came and it was the best spring anybody had, and Craigmoor had done a thing, and a town that has done a thing wants a reason to do it again, and the Games are the reason. Nine events, one winter, nine days. They are not for the winner. They are for the nine days.”

* * *

The ice-swim is the first thing on the programme, in the dark, at seven in the morning, and forty-one people enter it every year, and the water is a channel cut for the peat carts that the town dug out in 1887 and never filled in.

Ewan watched Sheila McGrath swim it twice a day for a week and then not come at all, and on the last morning he came down to the ladder to ask her about it, because she was the only person at the Games who did not seem to be waiting for anything.

She was sixty-four. She had swum it every year since she was thirty-three, which was thirty-one years, and come fourth every year since 1911, and she had the good-tempered look of a woman who has been beaten on a timing sheet so often that the beating stopped being an event.

“Fourth,” Ewan said. “All the time.”

“Fourth is a place you can get to on a bad day,” Sheila said. “First is a place you have to be lucky on. And they do not give a cup for fourth, and I am not interested in a cup, and if you want the honest reason, my father dug this channel in 1887 and the men used to come in here at the end of the day because it was the one part of the work that was warm.”

She put her cap on. “Your gran keeps a ledger.”

“She does.”

“Then she has my name down four hundred times, and every time it is the same four lines, and one of them is mine.” She went down the ladder backwards, one-handed, without looking. “That is not a complaint. It is a set of facts.”

* * *

The hall on the Sunday after was the fullest it had been in eleven years, and the reason it was full was that people wanted somebody to say out loud what the story was going to be. Kieran McGrath said it instead, standing at the bottom of the steps with his hands at his sides, not being gracious, not being dramatic.

“If the champion is whoever is best, and the best is whoever wins, and the winner is whoever the town needs, then the Games do not measure the best,” Kieran said. “They measure the town’s need. And the town’s need is a boy.”

Nobody in the hall said anything for a while. In the third row somebody’s pipe went out.

“I have not won a thing since I was nine years old,” Kieran said. “I have been handed it. You cannot hand a thing to a person and then ask the person whether they are good at it, and that is what we have been doing to him, and he is twelve.”

Ewan looked at his gran in the fourth row, holding the ledger against her chest with both arms. She looked back at him, and then stood up and came down the aisle with the whole crowd watching her do it, which was the most alarming thing that had happened to him all winter.

Ewan went up the steps, took the wreath off the miller’s table and put it down again, and picked the cup up. It was a heavy ugly thing with a dent in the rim from 1934.

He gave it to Sheila McGrath, who was the fourth, and who was sixty-four, and who had swum the channel thirty-one times.

“A cup is for a winner,” she said.

“You have swum it thirty-one years.”

“That is a fact, not a win.”

“It is the win here,” Ewan said. “You have been the best person in the water every single winter and this town gave it to a boy who is good at running. Take it and let them all look at it.”

She took it. The hall made a noise, and it was not a cheering noise, and the Games went on on the Tuesday exactly as the programme said, all nine of them, in the same order, with the same bell.

On the Monday, Gran opened the ledger on the sill and turned to the front.

The first page of the MacGrath run had gone, and the keeper had ruled a new one, and the first page did not begin with a family. It began with a column headed ENTERED, and it ran down the page and round onto the next, in the same small flat handwriting, every name that had come to the nine events that winter, ninety-one of them, and the fourth, and the last, and the boys who had walked away from the Ladder halfway up, and the champion’s name in the middle of it in the same size writing as the rest.

It took Gran the whole of Tuesday and Wednesday. It is four generations now, and it is the reason Craigmoor is still a town.
