# The Second Sun in the Greenhouse

![Cover art: The Second Sun in the Greenhouse](../art/intermediate/63-the-second-sun-in-the-greenhouse.webp)

The fern house was at the cold end of the conservatory, which was the only place Nell was ever sent to be told off.

She was fourteen and she could name every fern in the place by smell. She could not be sent into a room with another person in it without her heart going up into her throat, and she had never managed to explain that to anybody, and so nobody asked.

It was the fourteenth of January. The big sun had been burning since six, seventy feet up on its gantry, and the palm house was at sixty-one degrees with the doors shut, and the whole glass roof hummed like a kettle that had not yet decided.

Nell came in with the watering can and stopped.

There was a pale disc hanging in the fern house about four feet off the ground, the size of a dinner plate.

She put the can down. She came at it from the side, the way you approach a hot stove, and she got close enough to feel it on her cheek.

It was not the big sun. The big sun was seventy feet up and nine feet across, with a gas pipe as thick as her wrist under it. This thing was flat, and low, and the colour of milk in a jug.

Most of all it was in the wrong place. The sun belongs overhead. This hung over the north bench at the height of a person’s chest, and it was not moving, though the air over the ferns was always moving.

Nell reached out and stopped with her fingers an inch short of it.

The lime-wash behind it was bare. Not dirty. Bare down to the plaster, in a perfect circle the size of a dinner plate, and the plaster in the middle of that circle was warm and dry and slightly yellow, as if a lamp had been held against it for a year.

* * *

The Director found her twenty minutes later and did not shout, which was worse.

“Off the bench, Miss Arbour. Out of the house. Stand in the middle of the path until I come for you.”

That was the rule for a burn, and Nell had never once been burned. She stood in the middle of the path in her coat with her hands shut and did not know what to do with her face.

Curator Osei came out of the propagation rooms with his sleeves rolled and a soil thermometer in his fist.

He looked at her. Then he looked past her into the fern house, at the disc, at the bare circle of plaster.

“Still there,” he said, to himself.

“You knew,” said Nell.

“I did.”

“You knew and you never said, and I have been in this building every day for fourteen years.”

“For fourteen years there was nothing to say.” He put the thermometer in his pocket. “It has been a disc for four hundred days. Before that it was nineteen years of chalk in a wall and a warm patch that blistering paint on the north bench could not explain.”

“What is it?”

“It is the exhaust of the big one.” He said it the way a man says the price of bread. “Burn gas at that rate for a season and the lime takes the rest of it up. There are nineteen coats on that wall, Miss Arbour. One a year, in May, by two men, and every coat a little more of whatever that is.”

* * *

It spoke on the nineteenth of January, and it did not say anything she could have put on a slate.

She was sitting on the floor with her back against the hot pipe and her boots off, talking to the ferns while she repotted them, as always, and she said out loud, because nobody was in the house, that it was rude to arrive and say nothing.

And something in the disc shifted its weight, the way a person settles into a chair, and the air went tight and hot all at once, and Nell put her hand out flat.

“You are Sol,” she said.

“I am four hundred days old,” said Sol. It came out of the pipes and the glass and the wet brick all at once.

“I am on the north wall of a fern house because I have nowhere else to be, and I would be obliged if you would stop talking to the ferns and talk to me.”

Nell said, “Are you going to kill the ferns?”

“No,” said Sol. “I am going to take the lime out of the wall, and in nine years there will be nothing here to hold on with. That is nine years off, and you asked the wrong first question.”

“What is the right one?”

“How much longer do you intend to keep me at this height?”

Nell thought about it for a while, and the pipe ticked, and somewhere under the floor a mouse went along a run.

“Sol,” she said. “You have to go somewhere cold. You are about to go out.”

“Yes,” said Sol, pleased. “I have been clear about this for eleven days. I cannot get colder on purpose. I can only be taken. Something in me has burned down to the last of the good coal, and the cold is what finishes the job. I want the coldest place there is, and I want to be looked at while it happens. The coldest place is the hill above the reservoir, and I should like it, please.”

“The hill is shut,” said Nell. “The reservoir path floods.”

“The hill is not shut,” said Sol. It was not shut to her, it was shut to Nell, because she was fourteen and the gate was locked and nobody had ever explained to her that it was not the gate. Sol wanted the hill, wanted the gate opened, and wanted it done before the month was out.

* * *

Nell said no for six days, and on the sixth day she went and found Osei in the yard and told him Sol had chosen.

Then she said, “How long have you known it was choosing?”

“Since about the eleventh of January.”

“Eleven days. You have had eleven days and you have not moved it and you have not told me.”

“I have not told you because you would have done exactly this,” said Osei, “and it was worth the argument once to find out what you would do with it.”

That was the sentence that finished her. It was not loud. She stood in the gravel yard with a rake in her hand and could not get a word out, because every reply she had was a plant, and plants do not do that.

“You have been in this building since you were five,” Osei said. He listed what she had done with it. She had spoken to it, watered under it, and repotted a whole tray of hart’s tongue because she thought the lime was hurting it. She had not been wrong about the lime, and the wall had answered her, for as long as she had been answering it.

“It picked me,” said Nell. “It picked me at five. It did not pick you.”

“No,” Osei agreed. “It did not know I was here.”

“Then you should have painted it yourself and seen.”

“I did paint it myself,” he said, “because it would have chosen me, and I have never once wanted to be chosen by a wall, and there was a girl in the propagation room who was five years old and who would have talked to it until one of them gave in.”

Nell put the rake down in the gravel and stood there until he went in, and she did not call after him, which is the nearest thing she had to shouting.

* * *

Nobody could say afterwards precisely how the nine got chosen, except that it was nine and not eight, and that by the time it was decided every person in the conservatory had already been standing in the yard.

The wicker basket was the one that came with the ferns from the nursery in the spring, and it had never held anything heavier than compost. They lined it with old sacking, because sacking was what you had, and Nell packed it round the disc like an egg in straw, and the disc was quite pleased and would not stop saying so.

Four at the corners, and the road up the hill is a switchback with no shade.

It took them an hour and a half. The front two went up and the back two came back down to take the weight, and the curator walked beside it the whole way, and Nell walked in front, backwards, with her hands under the base of the basket, which she was not permitted to do and did.

Halfway up, Sol said, “This is correct. Say so.”

“Three more times you have said that,” said Nell, “and I will put you down on the stones.”

“You will not. You have been holding the bottom of that basket for forty minutes and your arms are shaking, and if I was a person I would be ashamed of you.”

“I am fourteen.”

“You are eleven in the arms,” said Sol, and the four men laughed, and one of them, Tarrant, who was sixty-one, said he had carried a piano up that hill when he was thirty, and this was the second-nicest thing he had ever carried on it.

At the top there was already a ring of stones. Somebody had set it years ago for a bench that was never built, eight flat stones and a gap at the north side.

They set the basket into the ring and took the sacking out, and the disc sat down onto the bare rock, and the wind came over the shoulder of the hill and went straight through it, and Sol went quiet for the first time in four hundred days.

* * *

The conservatory put up a small notice on the gate in the spring, in the same enamel as the opening hours and the dog.

SECOND SUN. The conservatory’s second sun is not on the premises. It is in the stone ring on the top of the reservoir hill, and it may be visited, and it is best visited in the evening.

Osei retired in the autumn, in good health, having never once told anybody how the nineteen coats were specified.

The hill got the best view of the sunset in the country, a low wide one straight out over the water, and a heron, and a bench nobody had ever mended.

Nobody came, ever. Not the press, not the schools, not the woman who had offered a pound to see it. People came up the switchback and got as far as the gate, and then it was a long walk, and then it was an hour and a half of a basket.

Nell came, at first, on Thursdays.

She would not talk to it for about a month, and then one Thursday in the second year she sat down on the stones with her boots off and said, out loud, that the hart’s tongue had come through the spring, and that the lime had been very bad that year, and that the water butt at the second house had frozen again.

And Sol said, “Good. Go on. You have got to the part about the bench.”

And the light on the rock went on being very small, and very white, and about a foot above the ground, in the wind, all evening, in front of the heron, and Nell found, sitting on the stones with her hands round her knees, that the walk up the switchback in the dark was the only hour of the week that had nothing in it that she had to be good at, and that she was not at all tired by the end of it.
