# The Hollow Crown

![Cover art: The Hollow Crown](../art/intermediate/06-the-hollow-crown.webp)

Captain Iri Tal was thirty-three, broad-shouldered and spare. The Ember Vault’s ceremonial half-mask covered an old burn along her chin, and her dark hair was braided beneath it. She could not enter the Hall of Reflections wearing it: silver answered silver, and two sovereign eyes in one chamber could unmake a lesser one.

So when Princess Sava Orin came to request a mask that showed no face, Iri removed hers.

The princess was twenty-five, imperious without shouting. A wine-red coat covered ivory silk, gold thread traced her dark hair, and plain boots showed beneath her split skirt. She had come by the servants’ stair.

“A white ground,” Sava said. “No features. Not an oval, not a shadow. Nothing.”

“The masons call that a vacancy.”

“It is the only shape the law cannot crown.”

Iri knew the forms. Each New Year, a sovereign mask was remade from the ruler’s living imprint. The old face was stored beneath the palace, where it continued to rule. Dukes entered rooms whose doors opened for a dead queen; clerks heard a murdered chancellor approve sums in their inkstands. The empire called it metaphor.

It was not. Reflection obeyed the removed face, and the removed face obeyed the mirror.

“Why now?” Iri asked.

Sava’s gaze moved across the emberglass cases. In their depths, generations of royal faces floated without bodies: stern, amused, bloodless, crowned. “Because someone has begun collecting me.”

“We have your summer mask.”

“The face beneath it left three nights ago.”

Iri’s pulse beat once against her collar. “That is impossible.”

“My body was found in the Hall. My face was not.” Sava touched the black pearl at her throat. “If I go where I intend, the reflections will not know what to call me.”

Before Iri could ask what she intended, someone struck the vault door three times.

Chancellor Varek stood beyond it in an emerald frock coat and silver-trimmed boots polished to mirrors. Though fifty-three and lame, he entered without a cane. The door showed no Varek at all.

He smiled with his stolen mouth. “Your grace. You should not be below.”

“My captain is guarding me.”

“I am always delighted to see diligence rewarded.” His empty reflection moved a moment late, though Iri saw it plainly. “Princess, surrender the wax impression.”

Sava struck him with the hilt of her knife. He collapsed without blood, his face remaining upon the stone, and his body changing into a sheet of silvered paper.

The door slammed. On its inner surface, Varek smiled again.

“Do not follow me,” Sava said. “The old faces need a witness. I will give them one. At the Mirror Bell, wear black. Bring no silver.”

“Wait.”

But she was already lifting a lamp to the black mirror behind the masons’ dais. Its surface rippled. She stepped through with one hand raised, and vanished without disturbing dust.

* * *

For three hours, Iri searched. Sava’s rooms were unguarded. Beneath her mirror lay a note in the princess’s angular hand: DO NOT LET HIM HEAR MY NAME.

The note’s final strokes continued across the marble sill in black grease, leading to a cabinet of ceremonial masks. Behind the imperial cast, Sava had cut away a display plinth’s lining. On the hidden shelf lay a ledger of annual impressions.

Every year for forty-two years, one name had been struck from the official succession rolls and written here in a different ink: Varek Calim, removed for regicide. Beside his name was a note in the dead queen’s hand: He still approves my bells.

Beneath the ledger waited a fresh rectangle in the dust. Something had been taken.

Iri knew where.

The Hall lay beyond the black mirror inside the palace’s oldest wing. No official map admitted it existed. A masons’ sealed stair led downward.

Iri went without armor, dressed in plain black wool and felted boots, her half-mask left in its case. Clothing was a less reliable claim than a face.

At the sealed door, two guards stared at her, but not directly. Their eyes fixed upon the place where her jaw should have been.

“Captain Tal,” one said, though Iri had not spoken.

She showed her seal. The lock clicked.

“Order her back,” the other whispered.

A black key turned itself. The door opened upon a corridor of polished floor. Iri crossed.

Every mirror in the passage held a different court. In one, the Emperor dined with his dead mother. In another, the Duke of Aven led soldiers across a frozen bridge. In a third, Varek, bright and whole, stood before a mirror throne.

At the end waited the Hall.

It was a circular room beneath a lake of black water. Empty masks ringed the walls, their features floating above no neck. At the center, beneath a bell of unpolished iron, Sava lay upon a bier of common wood.

She was dressed in black, as she had promised. Her face was perfectly preserved. Her eyes were open, but the figures watching from the mirrors had her expression, not her gaze.

Iri stepped onto the first of many stone tiles.

Reflection stretched behind her, wearing a silver half-mask and the uniform of the Ember Guard. She crossed into shadow. Her reflection remained, then smiled when she did not.

Sava’s voice came from every empty mask. “You came in silver.”

“I left it.”

“That is not the same as surrendering it.”

Her body lifted from the bier without breath. Sava’s reflection walked out of the glass, feet meeting the floor without weight. The two Savas faced one another: the living woman in dark wool, the sovereign face she had worn all year.

Varek appeared between them, more solid than before. His stolen face belonged to Prince Merek, the heir who had died nine years ago.

“What did you do to her?”

“Nothing that will not be corrected. The princess wished to dissolve the Afterrule. She asked for a vacancy, believing it a harmless door. I improved the design.”

Across the walls, expressions shifted. Surprise, anger, shame.

“The court sees me in polished brass, silvered glass, the still water in an assassin’s cup. Every removed face keeps its old authority. The Emperor’s mother locks the nursery. His grandfather’s Marshal commands the north gate. Why should power end because its holder died?” He spread his hands. “I am the most senior surviving face. Give me the vacancy, and every other mask will be empty by morning.”

Iri looked at Sava’s body. It had begun to fade at the edges.

Sava’s reflection said, “He needs a bearer because his own name was cut from the rolls. He has ruled for nine years as a rumor reflected in polished things, but rumors cannot touch a person.”

“Then her face is the bearer.”

Varek turned. In the black water, a door of white light appeared.

Iri put herself between him and Sava. The movement placed her before a wall of mirrors. A hundred Captains Tals looked back, each stamped with command. Varek reached into their reflections.

Pain struck inside her skull. She saw the succession rolls burn, her face hung above black water, and Varek’s voice follow her everywhere. Men would obey the seal on her belt because the law would rather call it command than haunting.

Iri closed her eyes. The pain did not lessen, but it no longer belonged entirely to her.

“Captain,” Sava said. “You have watched me for eight years.”

“Your highness.”

“Did you think I chose blankness because I feared ruling?”

“I thought you feared becoming what Varek is.”

Sava’s reflection approached the lake. Varek moved to intercept her, but his stolen face could pass through her only as light. She ducked and ran toward Iri.

“He owns the mirrors,” Iri said.

“No. He owns the recognition.” Sava reached the edge of the black tiles. “The court saw Prince Merek die, so it obeys that face. Mine died in the Hall three days ago. The public still calls me princess, but the palace’s reflections no longer acknowledge my living face. Bright glass erases me.”

“If you stand in shadow?”

“They cannot follow. But neither can I be seen.”

Iri looked at the white porcelain mask beneath the unpolished bell. Sava had meant to enter wearing vacancy, then lead the removed faces into common darkness. Varek had reversed the mirror bell and dragged her reflection back.

“Help me stand,” Sava said.

Iri guided the real Sava across the black tiles. Her body had almost no weight, but her hand was warm. Together they stepped beneath the bell. Above them, the face of Sava moved through the glass toward her body.

Varek seized the last occupied mirror.

Prince Merek’s image flooded the Hall. Doors thundered. Guards obeyed false commands. The Emperor’s dead mother cried from every silver plate: Sava had murdered her son and must be bound.

Instead she laid down her captain’s sword and took the empty mask from beneath the unpolished bell.

“You are not a claimant,” Sava said.

“No.”

“It will choose you.”

“Let it.”

Iri put on the hollow crown.

For an instant there was no sound. The mask had no inside or outside, no sovereign face. It opened upon every reflection in the room.

The removed faces rushed toward it. Dead emperors, forgotten consorts, disgraced ministers: all tried to become the vacancy. Varek’s stolen face struck the porcelain first, expecting the authority of recognition to carry him through.

But the living could refuse recognition. The court had mourned Prince Merek, not chosen him again.

Sava’s living body looked into her reflection. The woman in the glass did not command. She asked, “Do I return?”

Sava placed her hand over her heart. “Without your command. Come home.”

The reflection considered, then touched the glass. The face passed into Sava like breath returning to the body that had held it. She gasped, bent over, then stood in her own skin. Her reflection matched her at last.

The empty mask trapped Varek’s stolen face between its blank forehead and the lake of black water. Varek howled in Prince Merek’s voice.

Iri seized the unpolished bell chain. The chain had been cast in iron, the one material the palace could not remember. She pulled the bell down over the mask.

The iron struck porcelain.

Every surviving reflection broke at once. Glass rained across the black tiles, though not a shard touched skin. In the far mirrors, commands dissolved. Dead rulers cried once and fell silent.

* * *

They reached the surface together. Dawn whitened the palace windows, and without reflected orders the court woke confused, frightened, and free.

Emperor Orin heard the accusation in the Hall. The succession rolls were amended before noon: no face would be removed from a future mask. Each old face was returned to the sea-glass grave without authority, and the vacant mask was melted in the public forge. Sava read the decree in the same wine-red coat she had worn when she asked Iri to imagine a crown with no face.

The court noticed Iri then. Not merely as a captain standing behind the princess, but as the woman who had entered the Hall unbound and returned carrying Sava’s hand.

“Six years ago,” Sava told her privately, “you kissed me behind the south riding wall and said duty was a poor reason not to speak.”

“It remains a poor one.”

“I had gone because the palace knew my face better than it knew me. I should have told you.” She touched the collar of Iri’s plain black coat. “I told myself secrecy would keep you safe. Instead it let Varek take three days from you.”

“Take the nights back.”

Sava’s smile was slow, bright, and entirely her own reflection.

In the doorway, court officials gathered, uncertain whether the captain’s plain clothes were an insult or a declaration. Sava faced them without releasing Iri’s hand.

“Captain Tal will attend me,” she said, “not behind me.”

And when the crowd parted, Iri Tal walked out beside the princess, into a morning that could not be ordered by any face but their own.
