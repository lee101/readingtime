# The Girl Who Found the Comet

![Cover art: The Girl Who Found the Comet](../art/intermediate/175-the-girl-who-found-the-comet.webp)

Wren Mahon was fifteen and she had the 200 millimetre Newtonian on loan from the Tekapo astronomical society, on the condition that she wrote down everything she saw and did not use a phone at the eyepiece. She had argued about the phone for nine months, lost that argument on the first night, and had not brought it up since.

The society's requirement for naming anything was on the inside of the folder lid, and she had it by heart. Two people. Three nights. Positions better than one arcminute. Wren read it the way her uncle Dave read the weather, which was fast and out loud.

She found it on the eleventh of August at twenty past eleven at night, low in the west under the Scorpion's sting, a soft grey patch wider than it was tall with no stars in it at all. It was not on the list of twenty-two objects in the folder, and the list had the photographs marked, so she checked the stamp against the red pencil before she checked anything else.

She centred it and wrote the time, 23:21, and then she came back a quarter of an hour later and looked for it in the same place.

It was not. Four arcminutes along in twenty minutes, and the stars around it had not moved, and that is five point seven degrees in a day. A star cannot do that, because a star's own sideways motion is a small fraction of an arcminute a year. A thing that moves like that is close, and a thing four arcminutes across and that close would be bigger than the orbit of the Moon.

Dave came out onto the concrete at midnight and looked at it through the finder for a while.

“Asteroid,” he said.

“An asteroid that is not on the list.”

“There is a page in that folder with nine on it and none of them are under the Scorpion.”

She wrote comet in the last column and left the question mark, because an asteroid does not have a tail and she had not seen a tail yet, and she was fifteen.

* * *

The second of September she came out at nine with the atlas on the board and a protractor off Dave's desk, because there was one thing she could settle without any more nights.

The tail had come out past the end of her patch on its own, and it was thin and perfectly straight, and it lay along the line that joined the comet to a spot in the sky with no star anywhere near it. She did not know what the spot was. She had looked for forty minutes with the atlas open on her knees, and the atlas was nineteen years old and its constellations were drawn in a way she had never been taught to read.

So she did not need to know what the spot was. She needed to know whether the line from her comet to that spot was the line from her comet to the Sun.

She put the protractor's arm along the tail, read one hundred and seventy-nine degrees, and wrote it down. Then she worked out where the Sun had been on the second of September from the date page in the atlas, which gives it to a degree for anybody prepared to sit up, and she read one hundred and seventy-nine degrees from her comet to that place.

The same number, twice, with the atlas not involved in the second one at all.

“The tail points away from the Sun,” she said to Dave, in the doorway, at nine o'clock on a Wednesday. “Not away from the comet. Away from the Sun.”

He said that was so, the way he said the price of wool, and then he said the thing that mattered, which was that she had just thrown away her own objection. If the tail is a pointing away from the Sun, then every position she measures for the rest of the season can be fixed against a Sun she knows the exact place of. She did not need the sky chart. She needed the direction of the tail.

* * *

That was how she got an arc.

The fourth of September she took a position and it was 22:04 and it was good to about half an arcminute. The fifth she took one at 22:11. The sixth, the same. Then the seventh the cloud came in and stayed in until the nineteenth, and by the nineteenth the comet was four degrees further along and a good deal fainter and setting twenty minutes earlier into the ridge behind the wool shed where she worked, and Dave was in there from six in the morning and did not want the drive up the hill again.

Five and a half degrees, from the second to the nineteenth.

“The rule says three nights,” she said.

“The rule says three nights and better than an arcminute, and you have got five and a half degrees and one pair of hands, and if the rule is there for a reason then it is there because of what happens to somebody with five and a half degrees and one pair of hands.”

He was right and she wrote it down, and then she did the thing she had been putting off, which was the fit.

* * *

The fit is not a drawing.

You have three points in the sky, and a path through them, and the path is a piece of a closed curve, and the closed curve has a size and a shape, and the size and the shape are what people mean when they ask when it comes back. Two numbers do it, the half of the long axis of the curve in millions of kilometres and how much the curve is opened out, and from those two you get the rest.

She did it in the garage over two nights in the last week of September, on Dave's kitchen table, with the observation book and the calculator from the wool shed office that she had permission to borrow and had returned on the Monday.

The number that comes off the end, once you have the size of the orbit, is the time. The size is in a ratio to the size of the Earth's own path, and the time comes from that ratio the way the three comes from the square on the end of it, so a bigger orbit means a longer time and it means a much longer one than it looks like it should.

Her fit gave her one hundred and eighty.

She wrote it on the back of the roster and then worked out the time, because that is the part people actually want, and it came out at two thousand four hundred and fifteen years.

She sat with that for a bit. It is a length of time with no picture in it at all. It is longer than the cathedral at Cologne and longer than the oldest thing anybody has built, and she could not make it mean anything, and she wrote it in the last column of the observation book and left it there and told nobody for two weeks.

* * *

And then she did the part that was going to cost her, which was getting a second pair of hands.

The rule wanted two people and she had one, and the one she had was a man of forty-six who reads the sky the way he reads a pen register, quickly, and who had never once said the wrong thing about a star and had said the wrong thing about a line on a page in the first ten minutes. So she gave him the atlas, the protractor, the red light and the observing book, and she stood in the doorway and let him do the eleventh of September on his own, from a position she had already written down.

He was two and three quarter arcminutes out.

It was not carelessness. It was the fourth star from the left, which is the one the plate and the atlas disagree about by a little, and he read it where the plate said, and he was two and three quarters out on a straight line and did not know it.

“If you do that in the third position,” Wren said, “the whole thing moves.”

She took the book back and did the eleventh herself, correctly, and then did Dave's position over from his own written numbers with the plate on the table beside them, and got him to two and a quarter and then to one and a half, and they used his, because a second observer's number is worth more than a better number and the rules say so and she was not going to be the person who breaks that.

So the fifth position went into the fit at 22:48 on the second of September, eleven degrees further along the track, and everything about the comet changed.

* * *

The size came out at two hundred and forty, and from two hundred and forty the time came out at three thousand seven hundred and eighteen years.

She checked it three times, because the first two gave her the same number and she expected the third not to. Two hundred and forty, then the two, then the three, and the three was three thousand seven hundred and eighteen.

One thousand three hundred and three years of difference, out of eleven degrees more sky, and all of it inside the three-halves power of a number she cannot get much better than.

That is the part that stayed. Not the comet. The way a very small mistake at the front of the curve comes out very large at the back of it, so that five and a half degrees of arc gives a period wrong by a factor nobody would print, and eleven degrees gives one wrong by a factor you might get away with, and the difference between those two is a girl with a protractor and a plate and a bad line.

“I had it at two thousand four hundred and fifteen on Wednesday,” she said.

“I heard you telling the shop.”

“You heard me telling the shop on the twenty-eighth of September.”

“It is the same comet.”

“It is the same comet and it is not the same orbit, and I do not know yet which one of us is right and neither does anybody else, and one of those two numbers is going into the book and I cannot tell you which, and I have looked at the difference for twenty minutes.”

* * *

The last page of the observing book that season is the arc, and she drew it, because a person has to see what she did.

Five and a half degrees of it. Eleven degrees of it. Two hundred and forty where it belonged, out of one hundred and eighty, with a line on the sheet through both points that showed how far the fit swung for the length of track she had added, and how much further it would swing for the next ten degrees, and the next twenty, and the direction all the swings were going in.

Three thousand seven hundred and eighteen was the number on the front of the book that season, in her handwriting, with the date under it and the two observations it came from written small beside it.

She did not put a ring round it the way her uncle rings his prices. It was the best number she had, and it would still be moving when somebody stood there after her, and the only line she could write that would still be true was the arrow, going up, and the instruction that came with it, which was that nobody fits this thing properly until it has been watched for years and there is no hurry, because it is not going anywhere.

The comet was still there that night, thin tail pointing out from under the Scorpion, one and a half degrees long by her estimate and probably two, and she took one more position to add to the page, and it was good to about half an arcminute, which was twice as good as the rule asked for and a good deal worse than three thousand seven hundred and eighteen needed.
