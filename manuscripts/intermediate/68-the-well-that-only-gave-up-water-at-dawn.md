# The Well That Only Gave Up Water at Dawn

![Cover art: The Well That Only Gave Up Water at Dawn](../art/intermediate/68-the-well-that-only-gave-up-water-at-dawn.webp)

Harrow Barrow had one well, and the well had four minutes.

Nell had been awake since two, sitting on the cold edge of the wellhead with a notebook on her knees. Clear night, late October. She had a bucket, a stopwatch, and the cold.

The well was dry. Not low. Dry. She had leaned down at three with the lamp and looked right to the bottom, past the cracked lining and the rotted pail, into black air.

The well was not empty. It was waiting. It had a shape and it was holding itself still.

Four. Nothing. Five, and the eastern sky going grey. Down the lane a gate banged, a dog started, and stopped.

At ten past five the sky went white along the horizon, in about the time it takes to boil a kettle.

The well gave up its water. It came up the shaft in one long breath, not a trickle and not a seep, a lift. It filled the bottom in a minute, then stopped, quite simply, the way a person stops talking. On the fourth minute she had the bucket in, and the last of it went over the rim in a sheet.

She sat with a full bucket in the freezing dark and thought about dew.

* * *

She had been told dew was soft and stupid, and had gone and looked anyway.

Her grandfather’s copy of The Farmer’s Account had a chapter she had to fold sideways to read, Harvest Water, or How to Have Water When You Have None.

She set the bread tin on the wellhead metal side up, weighted with the iron chock. Then her grandmother’s biggest roasting pan, dented like a fist, upside down beside it. Then bed. She slept forty minutes.

At half past five she came out. Frost in the grass, white on the inside of the fence, and the sky not properly light. The thermometer on the shed wall said two degrees. In the last hour it had gone up to nine.

Nell lifted the bread tin. There was water on the underside. Not a puddle. A skin of it, held there by its own skin, shivering faintly, and where the dent was there were three separate runs going downhill into the middle.

She turned the roasting pan over. The same. A whole grey membrane lying against the metal like a mirror laid face down.

Condensation. The air had been carrying water all night, the wellhead was cold iron, and the air poured its water into the iron and down the shaft, because a shaft is a chimney, and a chimney goes up. Every day, for as long as it had been doing it.

Nell sat down hard in the wet grass and laughed until her ribs hurt.

* * *

Tam Brace had been at the well since six with a hired man and a length of blue pipe.

He was seventeen, angry about something, and he had a plan: a pump, drawn on a feed sack in pencil, and underneath, WHAT THE TOWN NEEDS.

“You’ve seen the north field pump,” he said. “Runs all winter. That’ll run all winter.”

“That’ll pump the last of it out in a year. Anybody knows that.”

“Not if you cement the mouth first. Seal it, smooth it over. The committee’s got the same idea about the top field.”

“The top field reservoir. On the spring?”

“On the spring, yes.”

They looked at each other in the shed doorway, where the schedule was painted on the wall in her grandmother’s capitals.

“What spring?” Nell said.

“Won’t say. Everybody knows there’s a spring. There’s a well.” He turned the feed sack round. “Does your gran ever say no to anybody?”

“Then tell her to say no to the committee for me.” He was already walking down the lane. “You always get that face on, and then nothing happens for a week.”

* * *

She found her grandmother in the shed, comparing the two tin lids on a plank, the one from the well and the one from the butt.

The wall was not a schedule. Nell had known that for a fortnight, since she counted the columns, and had not let herself say so out loud, because saying it would mean the next thing.

There were seven columns. Not seven hours. Seven pages. Two hundred and six nights, running back to a Tuesday in March: NIGHT FROST 6 BUCKET BY FOUR. Then, thirty years on, in her grandmother’s square capitals, under the same night: SOFT, 4, AND A THIN ONE ON THE TIN. By then the columns ran into the hundreds.

Nobody schedules water by the frost.

“Grandma,” Nell said. “The well isn’t a well.”

“I should think not,” said her grandmother, and did not look up.

* * *

Nell put her hands flat on the wet tin lids, so her grandmother would have to notice her.

“I’ve worked it out. It’s a still, and has been one the whole time. It fills from the air, and it’s only ours when we get a cold night and a warm morning, and that is what the wall is. It isn’t buckets. It’s weather. You’ve been keeping weather.”

Her grandmother put the pencil down.

“Forty years I have told people the well gives water at dawn,” she said. “Not one of them ever looked at the wall to see what was on it.”

“It was buckets in 11 and 15. Then frost. After 31 a thermometer. After 58 I stopped writing buckets, because a bucket is a thing you decide to have.” She turned the pencil over. “Your great-grandmother began it out of fear. In the winter we ran out I was nine, and there were eleven of us, and no water on this hill for six weeks. And I have kept it because I am the last one alive who can read her handwriting.”

Nell looked at the page for the week of the fever. Eight lines, a gap of three weeks, then ONLY BUTT, and underneath, in the same hand, NELL.

“I was proud,” Nell said. “I wanted to write it up for the paper. And then I read the wall. All those years everybody hauled water in the dark because we thought there was a spring under this hill. And you knew. And you never said.”

“No. The day I said it, somebody would have come up the lane with a cement truck and a lid, and we would have had a covered cistern with a plaque on it and no water at all. We were never short of water. We were short of a cold night.”

Nell put her hand flat on the wall, over the numbers.

“Grandma. Why only four minutes?”

Her grandmother looked at her, with the look of a woman who had decided long ago exactly how a thing would sound. Then she stopped.

“Did you sleep at all?” she said.

“Grandma — ”

“You’ve been out in it since two. Your hands are the colour of a fish.” She took Nell’s wrist and shut her fingers round it, the way you would hold a door. “Nobody in this family has ever asked you one thing about yourself, Nell. Not once, in fifty years. Get the question right, love.”

Nell said, “Why has nobody ever asked me anything?”

“Because there was always water to carry,” said her grandmother.

* * *

Tam’s plan and the reservoir plan turned out to be one plan. On the sixth of November the man from the committee came up the lane with a roll of drawings, delighted to explain all of it to an eleven-year-old.

The reservoir was to go on the shoulder above the well. It would take the spring, they said, and put the water in a covered tank, and the committee were calling it the Harrow Barrow Water Scheme. And there was a plan, drawn in a firm line, of a lid.

Not a cement lid. A stone one, sloping, set into a collar, the way they do it over a spring, so the water sits in the dark and nothing falls in. It would be the best-looking water in the county.

It would sit eleven feet above the mouth of the only well in Harrow Barrow.

Nell told her grandmother that night, in the shed, holding the lamp so it threw them both across forty years of numbers.

Her grandmother listened without sitting down, then looked at the clean space under that morning’s entry where the next one was going to go.

“You are telling me,” she said, “that they are going to put a lid on the well.”

“A stone one. Sloping. For the water’s own good, they said.”

“Aye. Well. They will.” She turned her hand over and looked at the ink on her fingers. “Forty years I have waited for somebody to walk into this shed and ask me a question about the water. Any question.”

“You are not angry,” Nell said.

“No,” said her grandmother. “I am going to have a very good week.”

* * *

The reservoir took eleven months and cost more than anybody had thought, because the spring ran clearer than the committee’s man believed, and the tank wanted a bigger tank.

Nell kept the notebook. She wrote the date, the overnight low, the sunrise, the minute the water came, and the state of the tin, dry days included.

The man from the committee never sealed the well. There was a form, and Nell filled it in, and it said CONDITION OF SURROUNDING STRUCTURE: SOUND, UNENCLOSED, NOT AT RISK, and he stamped it and went away. There was nothing about it to seal.

The first winter was bitter. The second was worse. In the third winter the tank cracked.

Nobody found out why for nine months. In the third February the wall went, because the water behind it had been eating the mortar since the day it was poured. A clear slow spring does that. The tank drained into the field over eleven hours and the standpipe ran dry for six days.

The well gave up its water at ten past five on all six days. The village came up the lane with their buckets in the dark, and the water came up in one long breath and stood there four minutes.

Because it was never a well. It was a cold iron lid on a cold night and a warm morning, and it had managed that for a hundred and fifty years without help, and the reservoir had built a roof over somebody else’s rain.

Nell owns a thermometer now. It hangs on the shed wall where the columns were, and there is a fourth column at the front of the notebook, one page for every morning since November.

Her grandmother is eighty-one and still gets up in the dark. She sits on the wellhead with the notebook on her knees, and does not read the wall any more. She reads the tin.

“There,” she says, one morning in January. “Look at the top of it. Not beads. A skin. It’ll be there by half five, and you’ll have a wet sleeve before I have my coat off. Cold night, warm morning, that’s the whole of it.”
