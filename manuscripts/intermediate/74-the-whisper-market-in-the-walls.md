# The Whisper Market in the Walls

![Cover art: The Whisper Market in the Walls](../art/intermediate/74-the-whisper-market-in-the-walls.webp)

Rain on the skylight, the cold of the plaster against her cheek, and Ariadne lying flat on her side with her ear in the wall above her bed.

It was nine on a Thursday in February, and the wall was a degree warmer than it had any right to be, and always had been since she was nine.

“Ariadne.” Her mother’s knuckles on the door. “Are you arguing with the wall again?”

“It is not arguing with me. It has not won yet.”

“That,” said her mother, “is not the reply you think it is.”

Ariadne put her ear back. That was how the market came, for anybody with the ear. First a hum under the plaster, low, further off than a room ought to hold. Then small sounds with edges: a stall dragged sideways, coins landing on wood, somebody laughing too hard three stalls away. Once, a dog.

She wanted her mother’s ring back from Ottie Brack, pawned in the autumn, and she had said nothing about it for six weeks. That was a thing she had not said out loud, and the market would take it.

“I want the ring out of Ottie Brack’s shop,” she said into the plaster.

The hum stopped. The market did not go quiet. It went orderly, the way a room full of people goes orderly when somebody at the back says a name. Then a voice under the wall said, “Say the price.”

She said, “I let the dog out.”

The wall was warm against her mouth and the room was cold. She heard the rest of the sentence happen on the other side of the plaster, in her voice first, then her mother’s, then a voice she had no name for. Something cold clicked, like a latch, and the market shut like a hand.

On her bed, folded in the sleeve of her mother’s good cardigan, was the ring.

* * *

On the eleventh of December Barley had got out into the hard frost and been brought home an hour later by a neighbour who found him under the Tolly cart, stupid with cold. Nell blamed Ottie Brack’s boy in front of eleven people, in the rain, and apologised to him on his own step. The wall had taken the words, and the price got paid in a currency she would not have chosen.

“You have been quiet,” said her mother at the table. “Quieter.”

“I am listening to the weather.”

“You are listening to the wall. I can hear it from the bottom of the stairs, like a tap with a slow drip.” Nell turned her cup a quarter turn. “Your grandmother did that too. She said the house told her what it wanted.”

“She never gave it a name.”

“No. Names make it real, and then you have to say thank you to it.”

That night she tried the sentence she had carried since she was eleven. She got three words into it, and the plaster went cold, and the market declined to deal.

* * *

The bell went at half past six, and the plasterer in the hall had his coat off and lime in the creases of both ears.

“Which of you is the wall?” he said.

“That is my mother’s line,” said Ariadne.

“Your mother has been saying it since Tuesday, so it is hers now. I am Osk. Put the kettle on, and hold the light.”

He worked the chimney breast for two hours and explained nothing, which Ariadne found harder to bear than if he had talked the whole time. Behind it the lath was grey as a fish and soft under his thumb, and the smell was wet earth and old sugar.

“This is not a crack. This is a house that has stopped agreeing with itself. There is a settlement up there and it is moving toward the front door.” He knocked the breast with a knuckle and it gave a fat hollow note, like a barrel. “That is a house with nothing in the wall.”

On the Sunday she went to the lock-up, where he was opening her mother’s ring with a saw.

“You are using it,” he said, before she had the door shut. “I heard you through the plaster on Tuesday. I know what a house sounds like when somebody is feeding it out of the wall.”

“You are not going to teach me.”

“I will tell you what the trade is, and then you can decide. If you decide you want it, I will unteach you for six years and be glad of it.” He set the ring down. “It is not a shop, Ariadne. It is a floor. Everything a house of that age holds up, it holds up with something said out loud. The plaster is only the part you can see.”

“Then it is not evil.”

“No. It is worse than evil, because evil has a face and this is infrastructure. Ask your mother why the roof on the back bedroom is a different colour.”

“That is a different roof.”

“It is a different roof because the men who put it on were frightened, and frightened men say more of the truth than they mean to.”

* * *

On the Monday he took her to four Cooper Street, because you cannot understand a market standing in a house that has none.

The house was two storeys with a beautiful staircase and the sound of a space where people had stopped living. He put his ear to the newel post, and it answered like a block of wood.

“There is a family on the front of this street who heard it until the spring of that year. They will tell you it was a good house, and they are right, and that is what made it worse.”

She put her ear flat against the banister. Nothing in it at all. The loudest silence she had ever stood next to.

“The house is not cursed. It is a house with no market, and the market is a wages system. It is how the whispers get paid for. When the plasterers go quiet, the houses go quiet, and then the houses come down. A bathroom ceiling first. Then a chimney breast. Then somebody, at two in the morning.”

A woman named Wren Tews had been standing in the doorway the whole time. Ariadne looked at the ceiling of her own front room, pale and dead flat, and pale for months.

“The bathroom ceiling came down in here in the March,” Wren Tews said. “I was in the bath.”

* * *

On the Tuesday Osk put the big ladder in the hall and told her to come up, and she thought he was going to teach her something.

Plasterers keep their accounts where nobody else thinks to look. A lintel. A beam end. The underside of a step, in a soft pencil a carpenter made out of the fat of a candle, rubbed out with a thumb when the account is square.

“You are on here. Fifty-one times.”

“How much do I owe?”

Osk rested both hands on the ladder and looked at the wall until the room got uncomfortable.

“You have not been buying, Ariadne. You have been borrowing. The market does not want paying at the counter. It has never wanted paying at the counter. It wants paying at the point of consequence, which nobody tells you, because at that point the person who owes is standing there and the thing has already happened.” He rubbed a mark with his thumb, and it did not come off. “You have traded for nine years and never once asked what you were spending. I am telling you because I am thirty-one years in, and I have buried two apprentices who did not ask.”

Somewhere below, a clock that had never worked properly ticked six times.

“Ariadne,” said Osk. “The balance on your account is your mother.”

“That is a person.”

“It does not weigh. That is what I am telling you. It has no face, and it will not notice that you love her.” He came down a rung. “It has been patient with you. I say that plainly, because you will go looking for somebody to be angry at and there is nobody. The market is not a usurer, it is a ledger, and the only question left in this house is whether the last whisper you have been carrying gets said by you, or taken from you.”

She got to the top of the ladder on her knees and put her hand where his had been. The lintel was only wood, and cold.

“What is the last whisper?”

“You know what it is. You have had it in your mouth since you were eleven and you have never been in the room it belongs to. It is not about me and it is not about the market.”

* * *

She waited until her mother was in the back kitchen, which had two chairs at the table and no picture on the wall.

“Sit down,” said Nell, “and let an old woman drink her tea, and do not stand in my kitchen holding your hands like a woman at a graveside.”

Ariadne sat. The lintel over the range was the colour of weak tea, and she had four hundred words in her and could not get one of them up a plank.

“You never left.”

“I never left.”

“You said you stayed because of me.”

Her mother put the cup down. The dog got up off the kitchen floor and went out, and the door at the end of the passage stayed shut.

“Where did you hear that.”

“Through this wall.”

“I have never said that in this kitchen.” Nell looked at the corner where the plaster lay in sheets on the floor. “I said it once, in the room where the wall was thickest, and I did not know there was a child on the other side of it.”

She was not shouting. Ariadne found that much worse.

“Not tonight,” said her mother. “Take your candle up and leave me my tea.”

Ariadne went up and lay with her ear against a wall that had nothing in it, and the snow came on the glass.

Number eleven came off its feet at two in the morning on the Thursday. Not a crash, which was what nobody believed afterwards. A long groan out of the middle of the house, a smell of wet plaster dust like a cellar after a flood, then a wall landing in a room it was not in.

Ariadne got down in bare feet. Her mother was in the front room with the dog against her knees, and Osk was shoring in the hall.

“Thirty minutes,” he said. “Not an hour. Then it finds the front door.”

“Do we go up?”

“You go to the kitchen.” He did not look at her. “Not because I am telling you to. It is the only room in this house with a wall in it older than me, and you are the one who has been eating off it since you were nine.”

The house leaned, not much, the amount a tired dog leans.

“Mum. Take Barley and go to Nell’s. Go now.”

“You are not the plasterer.”

“No. That is why I am asking you and not telling you.”

Her mother went, the dog still crying, and the house straightened very slightly, the way a man straightens when somebody gets up off him.

* * *

The back kitchen had been standing in a wet field since the fourteenth century. Ariadne put both hands flat on the plaster over the range. It was warm.

“The sentence is four years long,” said the voice under the wall, in her own voice through deep water, “and you have been holding the front half of it since you were eleven. Say the price.”

“I know you stayed because the house needed a child in it.”

The plaster took it, and the market under the kitchen floor made a sound like a great many people standing up at once, and every lintel above her cracked softly, all together, the way a row of glasses crack.

The front door came off its hinges and dropped into the hall.

Nobody had fed the fire. The house was cold, and struck very hard, and not on fire, because nothing in it had wanted anything for four years, and the old lumber in the walls needed nothing more.

The house settled. Not collapsed. Settled, the way a dog goes down on a mat, into the shape it had always been going to take.

Osk came in over the timber at dawn and looked at the ceiling of the hall.

“Say that again,” he said.

“Say what.”

“I said it does not weigh. You have just told me it weighs exactly one thing, and I am old enough to be told I was wrong.”

* * *

The market did not close. It came back into the walls of number eleven the following week, a hum, then the stalls, then the dog, and Ariadne let it, because a market that stops is a house that starts.

Osk taught her the trade from the plasterer’s side: the ladder and the rule and the chalk, and forty years of other men’s marks. She learned the ladder last, because he kept her off it for six years with his hand on her shoulder.

She was nineteen when Nell died in the chair with the dog on her feet, and the house made a sound that night like a person putting down something heavy.

She became the village plasterer at twenty-two. She had wanted the trade at fourteen, had the tools at fifteen, and was told no until she was twenty. By the time the reeve and the two men from Overbeck came to her, she was the only plasterer within nine miles taught out of the trade instead of out of a book.

Two years later than she would have liked, and entirely right.
