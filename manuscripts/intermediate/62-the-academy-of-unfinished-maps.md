# The Academy of Unfinished Maps

![Cover art: The Academy of Unfinished Maps](../art/intermediate/62-the-academy-of-unfinished-maps.webp)

Ilse Verrow had drawn eleven countries that winter and thrown away ten of them.

The one she kept was pinned to the north wall of the drafting hall with four drawing pins, because the bench lamp made her wrist ache by the third hour. It was a coast and nothing else. A long western edge, hatched in her own impatient hand, three bays, a headland, and a smudge where she had gone back over a line she did not like. There was a road. The road went inland two inches and stopped.

She had stopped it because she was bored, which was the one thing she was genuinely excellent at, and it had got her into this academy over four hundred other hands.

She woke at three with her cheek on the bench and a crick running down the side of her neck.

Then she looked up at the wall.

The plaster stood proud of the lath in ridges, hatched, hatched, hatched in a hand she knew as well as her own. Salt bloomed in the grooves and glittered under the lamp. Three bays. A headland. The smudge. Six small squares with little chimneys drawn into them, and a road that walked inland and gave up.

On the far side of the wall, where her paper had been blank, the plaster was smooth and grey and said nothing at all.

Ilse got up so fast she put her hand into the inkwell.

She put her palm flat against the coast. It was cold, with grit in it. She fetched her sheet, drew a tick in the margin beside the road, and looked back. There was a tick on the wall too, in her own hand.

Somewhere under the plaster, water moved.

She stood in front of it until the lamp guttered, then went out into the rain in her coat and no boots and stood on the steps looking north over the fields for a line of low grey coast in a sky that had never promised one.

There was one. Very far off, very flat, stopping dead where the east should have been.

* * *

By nine the north wall had a fence in front of it and a queue of students behind.

The board sat at the long table with the atlas of the realm open in front of them, which was traditional and which nobody looked at. Master Oduya sat at the far end. He had not finished a map in thirty years, and the room had stopped pretending to find that sad.

Vice-Chancellor Merril read from a sheet.

“The finding of this board is that the thing on the north wall is a student matter.”

“That is not what that means,” Ilse said.

“It is exactly what it means. Every country in this hall that ever came out of a sheet came out whole, with its rivers in the right beds. A coast on a wall with nothing behind it is a piece of work that was not finished. So the student who did not finish it will finish it.”

“Which student?”

“The academy is not stupid,” said Merril, “only slow.”

Laughter went along the table and stopped when Ilse did not join it.

“You will go,” Oduya said. “Twelve days of leave, the hire of a cart, the cost out of your term. Bring back a finished map or bring back a report. Either way you will not be sitting this examination.”

“I am the best cartographer in the third year.”

“You are the best hand in the third year.” Oduya turned one large hand over and looked at the palm of it. “Being the best is a report about a drawing. Yarrowmere is a report about you.”

That landed, and Ilse hated it, which is how she knew it was true.

“Who goes with her?” someone said.

The answer came from the back row, so quietly that Ilse turned round twice before she found him.

Corin Halle had seventeen unfinished sheets rolled in his locker and had never shown one to anybody. He put his hand half up and looked at the floor.

“Corin,” said the Vice-Chancellor.

“I will go.”

“You have the least interest in geography in this academy.”

“I have the least interest in geography,” Corin agreed, “because everywhere you go, they tell you what is already true.” He swallowed. “I would like to go somewhere where nobody has decided anything yet.”

Nobody laughed at that either, which Ilse found irritating.

* * *

They walked out of the west gate on the second morning. The cartman wanted eleven shillings, Corin had four, so they walked, and it took four days instead of two.

They went west over the ridge, down through a ford that took the water to their knees, and then for a whole afternoon along a coast Ilse looked at sideways and did not speak about.

“You have not said anything since the ford,” said Corin.

“I have been counting.”

“Counting what.”

“Bay number three,” said Ilse. “In case I have made it two.”

On the fifth day they came over a low hill and there it was, the three bays, the headland, the smudge, exactly, as she had put it down on tracing paper at midnight with a numb hand.

“The road stops in a quarter mile,” Corin said.

“It stops in two inches.”

“It stops wherever you made it stop,” said Corin.

The tide was going out on the same schedule as the drawing, the water pulling back over flats the colour of wet slate, uncovering a bar of eelgrass and a channel that would fill again in six hours. The air smelled of cold salt and cut grass.

The road did stop, in a cart rut, between two gateposts with no gate between them.

And in the flat land behind it, six hundred yards off, there were six houses with smoke coming off them.

* * *

Nobody came out to meet them. Ilse wrote it in her notebook and underlined it twice.

They walked the lane. The doors were shut, the windows up, and behind the fourth window a curtain moved once and stayed moved. A dog barked four times and stopped, as if it had been told.

At the second house a slate hung by the door on three nails, and the writing on it was the same sentence on both sides in three different hands.

Ilse read it. “It is not the fourth house’s wall.”

“It is not,” said Corin.

“It is the fourth house’s wall,” said a voice, and the door opened, and a woman of about sixty stood in it with her sleeves rolled. “Or it is not. That has been the position for thirty-one years, and the position is not wrong. It is simply not settled.”

“Do you draw it?” said Corin, nodding at her notebook.

“All of it,” said Ilse. “That is the trouble.”

By noon they were sitting at the second house’s table and the whole village had come to look at them, which was forty-one people and a dog.

Here was how Yarrowmere was governed, Ilse wrote that night in her notebook, in the flat hand of a girl who could not sleep.

The argument had begun three generations ago over where the capital was. It had never ended, and it had never needed to, because an argument, unlike a government, keeps working while it is still running. The slate went round. Each house kept its own copy. A question that needed settling was given to the argument, which gave an answer, and the answer held exactly as long as it took for the next person to object.

That week the boundary between the third house and the fifth was in dispute over a wall. On Tuesday the fifth house was winning, so the third house mended the wall, badly, and the fifth house watered the third house’s allotment, resentfully, and the wall stood.

The market went to the front of whichever house the argument had most recently reached, so on Friday it was at the second house, where Fen was selling badgers at the price badgers were actually worth, and not at the price anybody said.

A boy of eleven carried water from the second house to the last, because the second house had argued the last house into a thirst.

“It is not a government,” Corin said, when they were alone. “It is a queue that never reaches the front.”

“It is a government,” said Ilse. “It decides things. Badly, and then again.”

* * *

On the second day in the village, at four in the afternoon with the light going, Ilse did the stupid thing.

She wanted a capital. Six houses and no capital was not a country, it was a village with an opinion about itself, and she had an examination and no intention of going home with nothing. So she walked out to the gateposts, where the road stopped, and said, loudly, to nobody in particular and to forty-one people who were all listening.

“The capital is here. Where the road stops.”

The arguing stopped.

That was the part Ilse had not expected. It stopped all at once, everywhere, like a door closing, and the silence on that lane was so complete that she could hear the tide turning a mile and a half west.

The boy with the pail stood where he was and did not go. The third house’s wall stayed unmended. The badger market stayed at the second house, and Fen could not move her trestle, and began to shout about it, and nobody answered, because answering was the thing they were supposed to do and Ilse had taken it away by deciding first.

An old man came out of the fifth house and looked at the gateposts for a long time.

“Three generations,” he said, “and a girl with a wet sheet.”

“That is not fair,” said Ilse.

“No,” he agreed. “It is what we have.”

She went back to the second house with her hands shaking. Corin did not come, and did not later explain, and that was the whole of his opinion.

* * *

The last house in the lane had a cart outside, no one at the door, and the door open eight inches.

Inside, the walls were covered in charcoal. Not one map. Twenty, maybe thirty, pinned over one another, the paper gone soft with handling. Coast on every one of them. And on every one the east began and was abandoned: two lines here, a river there, a road to nowhere, hills that stopped in a squiggle.

An old woman sat in the middle of the room on a stool with a blanket round her shoulders and charcoal to the elbows in both hands.

“You are the school,” she said. “You are the one who did the coast.”

“I drew it at midnight. I did not know.”

“No,” she said. “You did not.”

Ilse looked at the walls. The nearest of the abandoned eastern halves went back a very long way. The paper had browned, and one had a date in a hand four or five times older than the others.

“I am Sorrel Vane,” the old woman said. “I was your age in the hall with a west coast of my own, and I have been trying to finish the east of this country for fifty-one years.”

“Then tell me where it goes.”

“No.”

“What do you mean, no?”

“You drew a west coast and left off the east because you were bored,” said Sorrel Vane.

Ilse stood in that room with the cold coming up through the flagstones and did not answer for a long time. Behind her, Corin stopped in the doorway.

“Yes,” Ilse said.

“That is the whole of it, then,” said the old woman, and picked up her charcoal, and looked at the wall the way she had for fifty-one years, which was not kindly.

“Is that all you have? Three bays and a road and six houses?”

“Forty-one people,” said Sorrel Vane.

“And no interior.”

“Ask them for it,” the old woman said. “You will get a great deal of coastline and no hills. It is not your fault, and it is not mine.”

* * *

They were four days on the road back, and Ilse did not open her notebook once.

She sat on the cart with her boots against the tailboard and worked on a fresh sheet propped on her knees while the hills went by. She drew the coast first and made it twice as long as she had. She added the headland, and the three bays, and the smudge, because the smudge was now a real fact about the world. Then she added the bar of eelgrass, and the channel that filled, and the tide that went out on a schedule, and the road that stopped, because the road had to stop or nothing else in the country would ever happen.

Behind the road she drew six houses, and chimneys, and a lane, and a slate on three nails, and a wall mended from one side only, forty-one small strokes for forty-one people, and on the wall of the second house a sentence she did not explain.

She did not draw hills. She had never drawn hills in her life, she found, and she was not going to invent them now. Where the east of Yarrowmere should have been, she drew more coast, then more, then a bay, then a headland, then a long line that went off the edge of the sheet and came back on the other side, which is what a coast does when a cartographer runs out of interest and refuses to admit it.

It was a bad map. The examiner who marked it said so twice.

The academy hung it in the entrance hall, where the afternoon light lies along it in a long warm bar. To the right of it, at Ilse’s request, they hung her tracing paper. The east half of that sheet is still blank. Ink will not take on it, and it is the only paper in the building that refuses her.

Under the two of them is a small brass plate.

THE ACADEMY OF UNFINISHED MAPS PRESENTS ITS BEST TEACHING AID, WHICH IS A FINISHED MAP AND A BLANK ONE HUNG SIDE BY SIDE, AND WHICH CANNOT BE SHOWN IN ONE.
