# The Girl Who Traded Her Name for a Season

![Cover art: The Girl Who Traded Her Name for a Season](../art/intermediate/70-the-girl-who-traded-her-name-for-a-season.webp)

The house on Mill Row had a cold tap in the kitchen and a bed in the front room, and Carys slept on a blanket in her clothes most nights because the bed was for whoever was drunk.

She was fifteen. Her name was on the birth certificate and on the school register and in her mother’s mouth about twice a day.

The letter came in February, between the two.

He was standing outside the gate in the rain when she opened it, a small man in a dark suit with his hair oiled flat, holding a soft black case in a gloved hand. He took off his hat. He did not come in.

“My name is Sedgwick,” he said. “I represent the Fair. I have about forty minutes and then I have to be in Cardigan by six.”

He said the rest in the gate, under the porch roof, clearly and without hurrying, giving her time to think between each part, which she understood much later to be the most frightening thing about him.

“Your name, in exchange for one whole season of a good life. House, food, school, a mother, a bed of your own. A season is the first of June to the last day of August. The terms are in writing and notarised, and I have the papers here, and there is no charge to you or to anybody. You will not be able to describe this afterwards. Not to me, not to the Fair, not to a court. That is the first clause.”

“Then how do I prove it happened?”

“You will not be able to prove it happened either,” said Mr Sedgwick. “That is not a threat. It is a mercy, arranged both ways.”

The house behind her was making the noise it made. Carys looked at the black case.

“Clause nine,” she said. “I read that bit twice.”

“You did. The season ends at the end of the last day of August, or at one day’s notice if the borrowed name is given, in writing, to somebody who is not a party to the contract. There is no other way out of it, and not a bad one. That is the whole of the design and I did not draw it.”

“Show me.”

He put the case on the wall, opened it, and turned the first page round. Four sides, printed small. Her name was on it, typed at the top of the block. Her hand was not on it yet.

Mr Sedgwick dipped the pen and offered it, and Carys read the whole page before she signed anything, which he permitted, and which took four minutes, and which she thought about every day for two years.

* * *

The first of June she was standing in a kitchen with a window over a garden.

The house was tall and thin and pale stone, eleven windows, and the garden went down in three terraces to a hedge and a field. Roses on the wall, looked after by somebody. A tap outside ran cold and then, after ten seconds, warm, which was the most upsetting thing that happened that month.

“Morning,” said a woman behind her. “How was your day?”

Carys stood in the doorway and could not answer, because she was eleven again and nobody had asked her that in four years.

On the bed upstairs was a duvet. A proper one, with a cover, and two pillows, and a lamp that worked. She lay down on it in the middle of the afternoon and did not sleep and then slept for four hours.

At the end of June, Mr Sedgwick sat at the kitchen table with a form on a clipboard.

“I am required, twice a season, to satisfy myself that you are content. It is a short question and a signature. If you are not content, the season ends early and there is no balance of any kind owed either way. It is quite painless.”

He turned the form round. It said, in full, HAVE YOU BEEN CONTENT.

“Yes,” said Carys.

He waited until she had finished writing, then read it, signed as witness, and closed the case. It had not been opened.

* * *

July was a month of learning how long a day is.

The mother called her Cary at home and Carys everywhere else, and asked her opinion about things she had no opinion about, and once, in the third week, knocked on the bathroom door to ask whether she was all right.

Toby lived in the house behind. He was sixteen, he mended a bike for four months instead of six weeks, and in the second week of July he was on the middle terrace doing homework on an upturned crate because the sun was out.

“You’re the one in the big house.”

“I live here,” said Carys, and heard how silly it was, and he did not laugh, or if he did he hid it behind the front of his bicycle.

They were friends by the end of that month. He lent her a screwdriver and a song. She helped him take a wheel off and put it back on the wrong way, and he fixed it, and told nobody.

At the end of July, Mr Sedgwick came again with the form.

“Have you been content.”

“Yes,” said Carys, and signed her name, and watched him sign as witness.

“It is the same question every time,” he said, closing the case. “You will get used to it.”

* * *

August she spent on the middle terrace with a paperback and a bottle of water, and she worked out what she had been doing for sixty-one days, which is nothing.

She had a name she had not chosen and had never been asked to choose, a garden she watered, a mother who asked her how her day was, and a boy behind who had known her as Carys since the second week of July and had never once asked where she had come from, which she had assumed was manners.

On the twenty-eighth she wrote him a note.

She wrote it in the kitchen at the bottom of the garden and put it under his cycling helmet, which was still on the fence, and she used the word Cary three times in it, because it was the only name she had to write with. She said she would be at the wicket gate on the first of September, and that if she was not there she would be gone.

It is four lines. It took eleven minutes and it is the only thing she has ever written that she would want read out.

* * *

Mr Sedgwick came at four o’clock on the thirtieth of August, and it rained, and he was alone, and the case was closed.

“The season ends at the end of today,” he said. “I am required to attend in the last hour to take back the name and close the account. I am sorry to do it a day early. Here is the notice.”

He gave her a folded sheet, dated that morning, initialled in ink at the bottom.

“Clause nine has been invoked. A written communication of the borrowed name was given on the twenty-eighth to a person who is not a party to the contract, which is your note to Master Toby, and I must close before sunset. I am not the cause of it. I was in Llandrindod at eleven this morning when it was decided.”

Carys read the notice twice. Everything in it was correct. That was the part she could not get past.

“So say it, then,” she said. “My name. You take it back, I write it on your form, we shake hands.”

“Certainly.”

She sat down at the kitchen table and picked up the pen, and she could not for the life of her remember.

It was not that it was far away. It was not behind anything. There was nothing there. She knew there was a name; she knew it had been given away; she had signed it four months ago, and a fortnight ago, and eight days ago in pencil under a bicycle helmet, and the going had been so smooth she had not felt it happen.

“Mr Sedgwick,” she said. “What is my name.”

He looked at her across the table for a moment, and then he closed his eyes, the way you do when you are being careful.

“I have it,” he said. “It has been in the case since February. I have carried it every day of the season and you have never once asked me for it.”

“Why didn’t you offer it?”

“Because the moment I offered it, the season was over, and the contract does not permit me to do that, and I would have had to put you out of a house in the rain before the work was done.” He opened his eyes. “You did not ask. That is the whole of my answer.”

The garden was very quiet. Down the middle terrace a hose was running. Somebody somewhere in that house had put a light on at four o’clock in the afternoon, for a child, in June.

“You have one hour,” said Mr Sedgwick. “The choice is not mine and I will not advise you. If I take the name back, the season ends at five, the house is not yours, the garden is not yours, and the note you posted is a lie you will have to live in. If I do not, you will be living in a house you are not paying for under a name that is not yours, and that is a fraud, and I will be obliged to report it, and I will.”

Carys put the pen down.

“Take the case,” she said.

Mr Sedgwick did not move.

“That is not one of the two options.”

“It is now,” said Carys. “You said you had to be in Cardigan by six.”

“I have,” said Mr Sedgwick. “That has never once mattered to me.”

* * *

She was in the front room with the case on her knees when the mother came in from the wet garden with her sleeves up.

“Was that the man with the case?”

“Yes.”

She put the trowel down on the sill and stood there, and did not ask anything, because she was part of the arrangement and had been told what to say, and Carys understood that for the rest of her life and never once said so out loud.

“Where am I going?”

“For now, nowhere good,” said Carys. “Can I stay tonight?”

“No,” said the mother, kindly. “I shouldn’t have to. I am sorry, love. It isn’t mine to give.”

That was the whole of the goodbye.

* * *

The two years afterwards were the hardest thing in them.

There was a room over a shop in Cardigan with a bed, a gas fire, a hatch to the street, and a landlord who did not want a fifteen-year-old. The case went under the mattress and stayed there. She washed in a launderette at ten at night and it was empty by eleven. She got the name back into use in nine weeks, and told people she had lost it in a fire, and the lie was so much smaller than the season that she could hold both.

The Fair wrote twice a year, on the first of March and the first of September, to the address of last residence, which was not hers, and had been her mother’s, since February. Printed cards. NOTICE OF A CONTRACT ENTERED INTO BY CARYS, ADDRESSED AS ABOVE, AND OF ITS REVERSAL, PENDING COMPLETION OF TERMS. Her mother had four of them on the sideboard for two years and did not throw any away, and did not answer them, and did not ask.

Carys did not answer them either. She slept in the daytime for the first eight months, and then not. She could not cook and could not be cooked for, and she could not say the word content. On the first of September of the second year a man came to the shop and gave her a card, which she read and put in her coat pocket.

* * *

The woman at the gate of the yard was her mother.

She was thinner and her hair was going and she had a plastic bag, and she stood at the gate for a while before she knocked, and Carys watched her from the window and could not make herself come down.

“They’re saying it’s your name in the paper,” her mother said. “Six letters, that’s all they’d give me. They’ve been sending me them for two years and I never knew there was a name at the top of it.”

“It’s mine now,” said Carys. “It was always mine, but the paper’s had it.”

“Was there a house?”

“There was a house.”

Her mother looked at the yard, at the line of washing, at the back door with the bad latch.

“Right,” she said. “I’ll have to read the terms, then. I’m not signing anything I haven’t read, and I’m not having you sign anything you haven’t read.” She shifted the bag. “Say the name, love. Go on. I want to hear you say it out loud, because the woman on the telephone said you’d forgotten it, and I said she’d made it up.”

Carys stood in the doorway of a house with a bad latch and no garden, and she said her own name out loud for the first time in two years, and it was hers, and she had not had to pay for it.

Down the road, past the chapel where the notice had been nailed up in the spring and taken down in the summer, the black case sat in a locked drawer in a solicitor’s office, with her name in it. The Fair would keep it there, on the day named. The season would not be renewed, and not one clause of the contract would ever be broken, because the Fair did not do that, and had never once pretended to.
