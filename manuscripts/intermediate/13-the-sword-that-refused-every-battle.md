# The Sword That Refused Every Battle

![Cover art: The Sword That Refused Every Battle](../art/intermediate/13-the-sword-that-refused-every-battle.webp)

The sword broke Prince Ren's practice swing without breaking itself, because the hilt locked as if an invisible door had shut.

Ren staggered into the straw effigy. “Again,” he said. The squires along the awning went so still that even the loose training banners stopped moving.

Ren was sixteen, broad through the shoulders and determined to look older than his years, wearing the blue riding cloak of the heir although it had been cut for a taller man.

At his hip hung a sword older than anyone in the yard, its black grip wound with green thread and its plain steel guard worn smooth by generations of hands.

When he lifted it, the thread tightened. The blade refused to move, and his arms began to shake. “Your Grace,” Weapons Master Iven said, “stop.”

“It is stuck,” Ren answered. Iven told him plainly that it was refusing.

The word traveled through the yard with the weight of a bad omen. Refusing a duel could shame a family. Refusing an enemy could lose a province, and a royal blade that would not move could cost a throne its public legitimacy.

Sera, who was polishing a practice blade, tucked it into the rack and crossed the yard. She was fifteen, small enough to disappear behind the armor stand, and she had learned to read a problem before it became loud.

“Give me the sword,” she said, but Ren told her that she was not touching the heir's blade. “I am your squire,” she replied. “You are also the girl who called my horse Stubborn,” Ren said. “He is not wrong about that.” The smallest squire laughed before covering her mouth, and Ren loosened his fingers.

Sera took the hilt, finding that the sword was warm, ordinary, and balanced; it cut a practice leaf in two and rang cleanly against the straw target. When she raised it in Ren's guard, nothing happened.

When she handed it back, the instant Ren's fingers closed around the grip the blade stiffened. The yard went quiet as Iven walked forward, an old weapons master with one clouded eye.

“Where did you last use it?” he asked, and Ren answered that it had refused at Millford, Salt Road, and Greyfen bridge, though he called those incidents delays rather than refusals.

Iven asked whether the enemy had attacked first, and Ren's silence, together with the sword's renewed rigidity, told Sera more than a confident answer would have. “Master Iven,” she asked, “is it broken?” “No,” he said, and when she asked whether a smith could repair it, he answered that this was not a question for a smith.

“Then what fixes it?” Sera pressed. Iven replied that it depended upon what the prince was asking the sword to do. Ren recited the formal words used before a border action, vowing by the crown's right and the memory of the dead to stand against those who had struck first at Red Ford.

The sword seized, a green thread snapped onto the packed earth, and the declaration that justified the march orders and the searching of border villages became a piece of broken thread on the ground.

Sera's father had lost his courier post for refusing to carry the Red Ford orders, and he spent nine years repairing clocks while insisting that paper could travel farther than truth.

Before Sera could say what the thread meant, Chancellor Varek entered with two guards and a document case. He was lean, gray-bearded, and possessed of the practiced smile that made a hard order look reasonable.

“The troops are ready,” Varek said, explaining that rebels had burned three granaries and villagers refused to surrender millers who had sheltered royal scouts. “They say the scouts started the fires,” Sera answered, and when Varek replied that reports were not always perfect, Ren muttered that this was the first true statement anyone had made about them.

Varek's smile thinned as he said that Ren's sister had died at Red Ford because rebels had risen against the crown, and that the heir must finish what the false heir could not. Sera had always been taught that Princess Elin died before her fifth birthday in a night of fire and screaming.

The official account claimed that rebels had seized the ford and killed the king's daughter, and King Alaric died in the same attack, leaving Ren as heir. The sword was called the Answer because an earlier monarch had sworn to protect the realm with it. The crafters claimed its enchantment would not permit an unjust purpose, but Sera now understood the limitation more precisely: the blade would not help carry a lie, and that was all the magic appeared to offer.

Iven took the sword and announced that the march would wait, but Varek's guards stepped forward, and Ren moved between them. “It will not happen,” Ren said.

Varek replied that Ren’s father had given him both the Answer and the crown, which he did not question merely because a blade was feeling difficult. When Ren pointed out that his father was dead, Varek answered that the dead king lived on in every order, a political argument that Ren met by demanding to know why the sword refused those orders.

“Perhaps your squire is a poor influence,” Varek suggested, but Sera answered that perhaps he was afraid of the answer, and the guards shifted as the document case tightened in his hand. Sera pointed out that the Red Ford, Millford, and Salt Road reports all claimed the rebels had struck first, which meant someone had copied the account.

When Varek replied that paper did not make a kingdom, she said that a person could make paper lie. Varek ordered the cursed sword taken to the armory and declared that the march would begin without it, but Iven held the weapon against his chest and replied that perhaps the prince was refusing the lie.

Varek gave Sera one hour to bring Ren to the council tent before the march started anyway, then left with his guards. Ren sat on the weapon rack with the Answer across his knees, ordinary and mute.

“My sister died,” Ren said, and Sera answered that perhaps she had not, a suggestion that made him turn angrily and demand that she take it back.

“I remember my father coming home without his blue coat,” she told him, explaining that he had been at Red Ford and said the royal soldiers came through the west ford before dawn while the village was a refuge, not a rebel camp, before the official account changed.

Ren asked why her father had kept silent, and Sera answered that the man who contradicted the account had lost his post, because a report could not be argued with once the king's seal was on it.

“You think the sword knows Elin survived,” Ren said, and Sera replied that she thought it refused to help them repeat a story that might be false. “That is not the same as knowing,” he observed. She agreed, because the sword could test a promise but could not decide whether a person had been deceived. Ren rested both hands over the hilt without lifting it, and Sera saw that he had heard her, even if he did not yet know what to do.

* * *

The old maps were kept beneath the western wall, where Sera's father repaired clocks. An older map showed a mill road with a royal camp above it. Its margin read, *West ford passable before rain. Do not disturb the mill.* Captain Oran had signed it, and his name also appeared on the official Red Ford report, although he had died five years earlier.

Sera copied the map and found an unlogged dispatch pouch dated the morning of the attack. Its order read, *To Captain Oran. Take the second company by the west road. Enter before dawn. Burn the records. If the child is found, bring the child alive.* The child was unnamed, but a second trail on the map led away from the village.

Sera folded both papers and ran for the gate. “Paper,” she answered when Iven said so, and when Ren said paper was not an answer, she replied that it was a reason to ask better questions. They rode past barracks, and Ren compared the scouts' red mill road with the older map.

“If the order was false, why does the sword only refuse now?” he asked. “Because you used the false account in the oath,” Sera said, and when he protested that he had believed it, she explained that the enchantment tested a promise, not his mind. It could not know he had been lied to, but it could stop him lending the sword to the lie. “Cruel design,” Ren muttered, and Sera answered that the weapon had not been built to be gentle.

* * *

At the mill, Maeve Holt waited while a broken waterwheel lay across the road. “I was a child at Red Ford,” she said, and after Sera showed her the dispatch, Maeve explained that the order came at night. Her mother hid the baby princess, the soldiers came anyway, and the mill caught fire. She had seen a woman in a blue cloak carry a child toward the hospice.

When Sera asked who had blamed rebels, Maeve said Captain Oran claimed they came at dawn before the bells rang and the story was written down. Ren turned away and admitted that he had met the princess once, when she was four and wanted a paper boat. He had assumed she took it when she died.

“My mother kept the order,” Maeve said, explaining that the soldiers were told to burn records but one escaped, so it survived as evidence.

Beyond the orchard stood a low hospice, where an old woman opened the door, saw Ren's cloak, and began to bow until he stopped her. “Is Mara here?” he asked, and when the woman's face closed, a young woman appeared in a green dress with Elin's dark eyes. “Ren,” she said, and he flinched before asking whether she was Elin. “You have grown taller,” she answered, and when he said he had thought she was dead, Elin replied that he had been told she was dead.

She explained that their mother had staged Elin's death because the council could not agree on a regent. The Chancellor had changed the official account: dead children made better enemies than living ones. Elin remembered the fire and their mother making her recite a horse's name so the soldiers would think she was calling for help. Then the bells rang, and she was gone.

Asked why she had returned, she said someone had used her death to start a war. Sera warned that dawn was approaching and Varek held another order with the same false sentence. The sword might refuse him, but he could still send soldiers. “Then let him send them,” Ren said, adding that he would surrender the lie.

When Elin asked whether he meant to surrender the heirship too, he said the council could decide the succession after hearing the evidence rather than after another battle. “The council must hear it,” Sera said, and Varek's guards were already entering the orchard.

* * *

They came at night with a messenger and a warrant, which named Mara Vale a suspected rebel and ordered the guards to detain Prince Ren for his protection. Sera hid the dispatch behind a millstone, and Elin went inside. Ren stood beneath the hospice window with the sword at his side.

When Sera asked what he would do, he said he would not run, and she answered that running was not the only choice. He acknowledged this before the guard called for the door. Ren drew the Answer, and the blade came free with a clear note. It stiffened when the messenger read the warrant's claim that the princess had been killed by rebels and anyone sheltering her enemies was an enemy of the crown.

“I reject this warrant,” Ren said, and when the guard insisted he had no authority, Ren answered that he had the authority to refuse a lie. He ordered the messenger to tell Varek that Prince Ren would meet him at dawn without soldiers and without the Answer.

The guards left as Sera fetched the old order and map. Elin came out with a bundle, Maeve and her son followed with the greyhound, and they took the mill road because the army would be watching the royal road.

* * *

The council met in the eastern hall at sunrise, where Varek stood with his document case while the army waited beside the river. Every face turned toward Ren when he entered without his sword, and Sera set the old dispatch on the table. She explained that it instructed Captain Oran to enter Red Ford before dawn, burn the records, and bring the child alive. The official account claimed that rebels attacked first and killed the princess.

Maeve said the soldiers came first, then placed a mill lamp before the ministers. Its base bore a royal horseshoe, a date, and the company number, giving it a chain of custody independent of the report. Varek called the lamp a relic and the map a forgery, but Maeve's son said the mill wheel had been broken by a chain rather than a torch. Elin removed the blue cloak so the council could compare its hem with the royal inventory.

The ministers read the records aloud and found the false sentence in the Red Ford, Millford, and Salt Road reports with only the place-name changed. Varek said repetition proved reliability, and Sera replied that her father had taught her a repeated chime proved only that the clock had been wound, not that the bell was correct. “Repetition is not a witness,” she said, pointing to the mill, the order, and the survivors.

Varek asked whether standing before the council without a sword made Ren less king, and Ren looked toward the army's banners before answering that it made him responsible for what he carried. He placed his signet ring on the table and refused to order the march. He refused to accept the Answer as proof of his right, and he refused to call his sister dead to make his claim easier.

The hall waited while the sword remained in the armory, untouched. It had no voice to name a ruler and no answer for the years when a lie had traveled farther than a person could. The council voted to suspend the march, seal the false reports, and recall the border companies, but it did not decide the succession that day.

Elin did not ask to be crowned, and Ren did not ask to be crowned. The ministers agreed that the records must be read and the law heard. Elin left the heir's chair empty and chose the floor beside Sera while soldiers loosened their armor and millers brought carts onto the road that had held an invasion.

Sera walked to the armory after the council, where the Answer hung in its plain rack with green thread loose from the hilt, and she did not touch it. “Do you think it will ever let me fight?” Ren asked, and Sera answered that she did not know, because uncertainty was more honest than a comforting invention.

He admitted that he had thought he wanted the crown more than the truth, but now Elin was alive he would stand beside her if the council gave her the crown. If it gave him the crown, he would have to earn it. Sera observed that he could refuse the crown, and Ren agreed, explaining that the possibility was why he could no longer hide behind it.

Outside, the first cart rolled over the bridge as the soldiers watched it pass, and the Answer stayed still in the armory, cold and mute, offering no one a convenient answer. At sunset, Sera closed the gate behind the last returning company. The old Red Ford report lay on the council table with its false sentence crossed through, and a boy lowered the bridge so the road could open to the river.
