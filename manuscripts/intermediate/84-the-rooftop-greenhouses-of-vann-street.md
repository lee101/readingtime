# The Rooftop Greenhouses of Vann Street

![Cover art: The Rooftop Greenhouses of Vann Street](../art/intermediate/84-the-rooftop-greenhouses-of-vann-street.webp)

Kes had the key on a leather cord round her neck, and by nine in the morning it was warm, and the tin cup in her hand was hot enough to be a problem.

Block four was ninety-one steps up, and the stair was the wide kind built for people carrying things, so it took her four minutes and left her at the top breathing through her mouth. Above her the greenhouse glass ran the roof in a green slope, and under it the tomatoes were already touching, which in February was a thing you did not say out loud.

She counted the taps. Eight of them, brass, one for each roof on the block, and the eighth was hers, and the seventh belonged to a man called Ostrow who had lived in four two since 1971 and had never once come up here on a Monday.

She turned hers. The tank on roof four said nothing at all, which is the correct sound of a full tank, and the pipe came down the wall in a hard cold line that smelled of water, which is the correct smell of water.

Then she wrote the figure on the pad in the box for the day: forty-one. The tank holds sixty. The city says it is filling at a quarter of that.

Her job was nine. She had held it since the Tuesday after her ninth birthday, and nobody had ever explained it, and she had asked her mother twice and been told it was a job for a child.

* * *

The notice came on the fourth of March, printed on one side of a single sheet and taped inside the stairwell at eye height for a grown person.

WATER RESTRICTED. Household use: one bucket per household per day. Roof cisterns: no filling from the main. Baths suspended. The office thanks Vann Street for its patience.

Below the notice somebody had written out a list of eleven blocks in marker, and beside every block a word, and the words were all different and none of them was thank you.

Kes read it twice going up and once more coming down, which was forty minutes she did not have.

The water came to the street in a main under the pavement, and the office turned the main because the reservoir at Kahu was down to a fifth of its wall.

The standpipes in the courtyard ran dry at noon on the sixth. Nine hundred people in nine blocks and one hose, giving a thread of brown from seven in the morning until two in the afternoon, and a queue at it in the dark.

The drought was not weather. She had worked that out by the eighth day. Weather was a thing that happened to you. The drought was a thing being done to them, slowly, on purpose, by people in an office, and everybody she knew was furious at each other about it.

* * *

She caught him on the Thursday at ten past six with a can.

He came up her stairway, which he had no business doing, with a green plastic can on a cord like the one round her own neck, and the lid off the top so he could hear the level. That was the detail Kes kept: he was listening to it.

“Adet.”

“I go home this way.”

“The stairway is in the block. The stairway is not a way anywhere.”

He was ten. He had her height and none of her rules and a face that had been doing sums ever since the notice went up.

“Put the can down.”

“It is for my roof.”

“It is water from my roof. You tapped my tank, and my tank has a plate on it, and the plate has my block on it, so anybody can see which roof a can came from. Which means you did not think that hard.”

He looked at the can. He did not let go of it.

“It is for a roof,” he said. “That is all I am doing. And you are nine. You are not the water. You are only the water today.”

That was true in the way a stone is a true hammer, and Kes took the can off him with both hands, and he let her, which she also kept afterwards.

* * *

She put the can into her own cistern, and the reporting took nine minutes and cost her a brass key she had to explain twice.

Grandmother Pene lived in two one with the good chair and the whole of block two’s roof history, and Kes went up there on the Saturday and told her before anybody else could.

Grandmother Pene listened all the way to the end, which nobody did.

Then she said, “Sit down. Take your shoes off. Hear me once.”

“Grandmother—”

“If you put Adet’s name on the form, the office will come and ask a simple question, and Kes, the office will be right to ask it. They will ask where the water went. And I will not have you standing in a doorway being nine, deciding how much of that answer you need.”

“So I do not put his name on.”

“No. You put nothing on.” She put her hand flat on her knee. “But understand where that water came from, because this is the part you will not enjoy. It is coming out of a roof nobody uses. Number twelve, the storehouse roof over the old bakery, empty since the machines went. It has a gutter that came off in the storm and was never put back, so the water goes where it likes, and Kes, my love, that is exactly the thing the rule exists for.”

“That is not fair.”

“It is not fair,” Grandmother Pene agreed. “It is a nine-year-old’s honest question and I have not got a better one. Nobody is to be trusted. A broken gutter is a fire with a slow fuse. In 1958 this street had one. It started in two four and it was still going in eight, and by the time anybody understood, the water was in the tanks on the roofs, and the tanks were feeding the fire, and eleven blocks went in a night and a half. Afterwards a man wrote a rule. Nobody read the rule. It has been sitting here ever since, protecting gutters.”

* * *

She went up number nine on the Tuesday without a reason, and the woman there was called Mrs Rongo, and she was seventy-one, and it took Kes from the second of April to understand that Mrs Rongo had been doing the feeding and Adet had been doing the lifting.

The roof of nine was not a vegetable roof. It was a field. Beans, maize, pumpkin, chard, aubergines, all of it in salvaged baths, all of it under a wire roof somebody had built out of a bed frame. There were seedling trays with names on them in a hand that was not Mrs Rongo’s hand.

At the top of the stair on nine there were four buckets, three satchels, a thermos, and a note on the door frame saying ANYBODY, ANY DAY, LEAVE IT ON THE LANDING, and under it, smaller, NO NEED TO ASK.

“I have fed the street since the first week,” Mrs Rongo said, filling a kettle. “Not the whole street. Eighty-one. The ones who are not managing, and there are more of them than the office thinks, because the office asks at the wrong times.”

“The water.”

“I could not keep a field like this on a bucket a day. Not for eighty-one. Not for nine.” She looked at the seedling trays for a while. “So I had a boy. He is ten, and he is clever, and he has been up and down these roofs at night for a month, and he was not caught until you. So either you are luckier than I hoped or he got tired.”

“He said, it is for a roof. That is all I am doing.”

“He is right and he is not right.” Mrs Rongo poured. “Say nothing yet. I would rather be a thirsty woman than an informer on a street where I am the water.”

* * *

On the Monday Kes filled in form fourteen, roof faults, in one line: TWELVE, GUTTER MISSING SINCE OCTOBER, WATER RUNNING TO PAVEMENT. She put her own name at the bottom, and she told Adet she had, and he went white and then did not speak for a minute.

The office man came on the Wednesday with a length of gutter on his shoulder, a spirit level in his pocket, and a ledger he did not need to open. He said the fault was correct, and asked who had reported it, and Kes said the roof next door can see it.

Then he put the gutter on, set it level, screwed it home, and went down the ladder, and the water ran out over the front of twelve and hit the pavement eight feet from the downpipe, because nobody had connected the downpipe when the bakery closed in 1991.

“You have a fall,” said Grandmother Pene on the Sunday, “the moment you have a fall, and a fall is a thing you can only learn by making one. I am sixty-eight and I have made four.”

They took the Sunday for it, the two of them, with a ladder borrowed from the bowls club, which leaned wrong and had to be set twice. They dug the old iron out of the grass and it was full of wasps. They cut it back in the road, in front of everybody, with a hacksaw off the bowls club as well. Adet held the offcut and got the angle wrong twice, and then got it right.

At the top they hooked the pipe to the new gutter with a length of flat bar, and they set the fall with a wet sock down it, and the water went where water is supposed to go, down the pipe, along the alley, into the main, away.

“It takes one bucket to prove it,” Kes said, “and then you put two more in, because one is a coincidence and three is a fact.”

Adet watched the third bucket come over the lip and go down the pipe, and then he sat down on the gravel of number twelve with his back to the wall. “Nobody ever asked me to help fix it,” he said. “They only ever ask me to carry.”

“You are very good at carrying.”

“I am good at carrying because I am good at going where I am told,” said Adet. “Which is not the same. I have only just worked that out, and I am not going to stand here and tell you about it.”

Mrs Rongo came out onto the roof of nine at half past eleven to see why two children were shouting, and she looked at the buckets, and the new gutter, and the pipe going down. “I have been on this street fifty-one years,” she said, “and I have never once seen a fall laid properly by anybody, and now two of them have done it on a Sunday with a borrowed ladder.”

Grandmother Pene read the office letter on her own roof on the Tuesday, with her glasses on their cord.

“The office says water officer two and four,” she said. “Two roofs. Kes, do you know what a two is?”

“A smaller job.”

“It is half a job. That is a demotion and there is no way to dress it up.” She folded the letter and handed it back, and put her own key, which had been round her neck since 1961, into Kes’s hand, and shut Kes’s fingers over it. “And now I am going to tell you the true thing, and then I am going down those ninety-one stairs and I am not coming back up. It is the best thing that will happen to you in your life. Because the day you have two roofs, you can be wrong on one of them. I had four roofs for thirty years and I was never wrong once, and there is nothing whatever in that, Kes. There is nothing whatever in being right.”
