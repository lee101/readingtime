# The Knight Who Returned Borrowed Courage

![Cover art: The Knight Who Returned Borrowed Courage](../art/intermediate/64-the-knight-who-returned-borrowed-courage.webp)

The village of Nethercomb had sixty-one souls in it, and Sir Aldric of the Ninth Spur could not make himself walk up to its gate.

He stood in the road in the heat with his hands on his sword and his heart going like a bird against a window, and he could not have said what was wrong with him, and not knowing was the worst part, because a knight of thirty-four with four campaigns should be able to walk into a village.

It was the fourth day of the progress. There had been a storm on the Tuesday, a horse had gone over on the Wednesday, and the road to Nethercomb was a mile of chalk between a hedge and a field of stubble gone to seed.

“Majesty’s business is at the keep,” said Tam, behind him.

“I am aware whose business it is.”

Aldric started forward and stopped, as if he had walked into glass. His horse, a grey called Puppet that had carried him eleven years, turned his head and looked at him kindly, the way horses are.

“Master Aldric,” said Tam. “It is only a village.”

“Did you tell them we were coming?”

“Your herald did, my lord. Two days ago.”

“Then they have had two days.”

“Yes.”

“To do what?”

“To hang something,” said Tam. “There is a market cross and no wine on it.”

“Then let us go in at the gate like a man.”

“That is the gate you will not go near.”

“It is the gate of a village of sixty people.”

“It is a village of sixty-one,” said Tam, “and you have said the number twice.”

* * *

It was heat, obviously. He had drunk a quart of small beer at the last posting house and the sun came off the chalk like a stove. He got as far as the alder tree at the crossroad, took his coat off, and put it on again, because without it he felt worse.

It was the storm. That explained the bad night. He had not slept, his neck had got up stiff on the right side, and a man who has not slept is a man whose body reports a pain it does not have. He had a tooth that had promised him something for two years, and a doctor who had said, in the tone of a man reading a label, that a man of his age should take it easier.

It was his heart. He put his hand flat on the mail under his shirt and let it beat against his palm and thought, without much alarm, that this is what it is, then.

He tried the word middle-aged out in the stubble field, standing still.

The third village had been a good one: a girl with a garland, an old man who wept, a woman who put bread in the cart and joked about it. He had been made to sit under an awning and soothed and asked about the campaign at the bridge. He could still smell the awning.

Nine miles and one day later he was in a hedge corner unable to move, and the man who wept was four villages behind him.

He had not, at any point since the crossroad, considered the fact that his shirt was on the wrong way round.

* * *

Tam noticed it at noon, when Aldric sat down on the wall by the smithy to eat bread and cheese, and said nothing, because Tam was nineteen and had spent four years learning the difference between a thing a knight wanted told and a thing he did not.

The plackets were out. The buckles were on the wrong sides. The linen bunched over the collar in a way no man who had dressed at a glass achieves by accident.

Aldric had dressed in the dark. That was the part he could not get at. He remembered lying down, and being got out of bed by a man with a lantern and a bad cough, the way that man had got him up for eleven years. He did not remember the lamp, or the cold of the floor, or his own two hands.

He could not remember it, and did not think about not being able to remember it, because a thing you cannot remember is a thing that did not happen, and a thing that did not happen cannot be counted against you.

“Ask it plainly,” said Tam, “and I will tell you.”

“What is it.”

“You are inside out, Master.”

Aldric looked down at himself. He took a long time with it, working at the placket with two fingers the way a man works at a knot he has tied himself.

“No, I am not,” he said, and then, in a different voice, entirely ordinary: “I dressed in the dark.”

“Yes.”

“I did not light the lamp.”

“No,” said Tam. “You did not.”

* * *

They did not go in. Aldric sent the herald on with the writ and sat the progress in the shade of the wall, and in that hour, with nothing to do and nowhere to go, the birds came.

There is a stone on the crossroad wall that a man who cares about nothing can look at for a whole afternoon. Low down, in the mortar, somebody long dead had scratched a horse with a date under it readable four ways.

Aldric watched the birds come onto the wall. Small grey things, the size of a thumbnail, and so still they looked like chips of the stone. Lendings.

He had been lent to by the colony on the north face of the Ninth Spur for eleven years, since he was twenty-three, and the terms had been read to him then by a warden in a hut. They were one sentence long. It is said to the person it was done to, in the building where it was done, once, and nobody may say it for you. He had not thought about them in nine years, and thought about them now for the length of a hot afternoon.

The birds walked to the edge of the stone and stopped. Nine. Then eleven. Then thirty on the wall, none nearer than arm’s length, and one stood on the horse’s scratched name and turned its head to look at him, and did not blink.

“Go on, then,” said Aldric to the wall.

Nobody has ever established what the birds can hear. Tam said afterwards that Aldric had said nothing. Aldric said afterwards that he had said a great deal into his teeth, that he finished by saying the word tower twice, and that the birds went off the wall at once.

The stone was empty. The sun went along the stubble and off it.

* * *

In the evening he told Tam about the page, badly and in the wrong order, and got to the part where the man died, and drank some water.

“When I was thirty-one,” he said. “It is not a good thing to have done.”

“No,” said Tam.

“I wrote it because I could write it. That is the whole reason. I had been three weeks at the garrison, and the captain had said, in front of eleven people, that I knew more Latin than he did. I could not let it sit.” He turned the cup round on the table. “I sent it to the tower, and there was a man in the tower, and they read it to him, because that is what they did with everything. Then nothing came back for a year.”

“And then he died.”

“Then he died. That February. I heard of it the following March.” He was quiet. “Nobody ever said I had killed him. I would have taken it if they had. I want that written down, Tam. I do not want it softened.”

“Then why has it not come out of you?”

“Because it is not my fault,” said Aldric. “There were forty men in that garrison, a governor with a warrant and a man in a locked room, and I was a visitor with a grievance, and I am the least part of it. And because it is four years ago, and because it is a village, and because I have a writ to deliver.”

He was not frightened of that any more, and not proud of it, and he had carried it round the whole hot day like a stone in a shoe.

* * *

“Where is the widow?” said Aldric.

“Nether Wray. Six miles.”

“Write to her.”

“She will not come.”

“She will come,” said Aldric, “or I will go and ask her in person, and that will be worse for her, so she will come.”

They went on the third day, because two days was the most he could bear. The tower stood on the far side of Nether Wray with a thorn tree growing out of the top, and the widow was already in the yard when they came up the track, sleeves rolled, a cloth in her hand, because she had known since the herald went past.

He had nine drafts and had burnt eight, and the ninth was in his coat, and he took it out and put it back twice. The first was in Latin. The second was in the third person, and his hand began to shake in a way he could not stop. The third was a joke; he got as far as the joke and then thought of her face and put the pen down. The fourth was five hundred words on the duties of a knight. The fifth blamed the garrison. The sixth said he had not known the man was alive. The seventh was so well turned he was ashamed of it. The eighth contained the word unfortunately, and he knew what the word was doing there and could not get it out.

“Would it be better without the word?” said Tam.

“It is in there because I put it in there.”

“That is a different answer.”

“Read me the ninth,” said Tam.

“If I read it, it is not mine.”

“It is yours. I have only written it down.”

That was the mistake, and it was the last he made, and it taught him the rest.

He had written it in Aldric’s own idiom, out of a letter Aldric had sent at twenty, four lines long and very good, and when Tam read it out in the yard Aldric felt the whole thing go out of him like air out of a bellows. A sentence you have composed and had written down and had somebody else read at you in your own voice is not an admission. It is furniture. And what the birds wanted, and what had walked off a wall in a hot afternoon and taken a man’s shirt off him in the dark, was not furniture.

“No,” said Aldric. “Do not read that.”

He gave the page to the widow, who looked at it and gave it back.

“Will you say it, then,” she said. “You did not come up here to hand me a piece of paper.”

“Give me a moment.”

“You have had a moment since Tuesday. I watched you use it.”

* * *

He got through it once. It took a long time.

He began by calling himself the Ninth Spur, a title and not a name, and had to stop. A page had gone out of his own hand to a tower in the year of the late frost, and the page was written to be admired, and he had sent it so that a garrison captain would have to notice him. And when they read it out to the man in the tower he had been pleased with himself all evening and had not once wondered what the reading would cost the man being read to, and had known it, in the back of his head, and had gone on being pleased anyway.

The widow listened, with the cloth going round and round in her hand, and did not help him, and did not stop him.

“Say it slower,” she said.

“It is the only way it comes out.”

“It came out of you once already. It came out of a piece of paper your squire read, and the walls here are thin.”

So he said it again, worse, the Latin gone out of it and the middle part falling apart, and it took longer, and she let it.

Afterwards there was no forgiveness in the yard and no condemnation either, and then she said, “He would have wanted it in Latin,” which was so unfair and so kind that he had to sit on the tower step.

The birds came back the same afternoon, in ones and twos, over about an hour, from the direction of the spur. They did not come into the yard. They landed on the thorn tree at the top of the tower with their heads turned, and the dead tree came alive with them, a hundred grey shapes in the last of the light, and they stayed.

Aldric went up and put his hand into it and they let him.

They were not the birds he had had, and he knew it as soon as his hand was in the branches. The colony had been eleven hundred pairs, and he had ridden up to the north face every spring of his life to be counted, and these were a thin scatter.

Of the seventeen that settled on his arm, the first to arrive was the smallest.

The wardens say the smallest is always the one that leaves first, and that it is the one they send when they are not yet sure of you.

He took it home in the cuff of his glove.
