# The Envelope That Was Not Handed Back

![Cover art: The Envelope That Was Not Handed Back](../art/intermediate/605-the-envelope-that-was-not-handed-back.webp)

The gold was on the bench in the counting house and had been on the bench since the September of 1998, and it was one hundred pounds of it in bars the width of a hand, and the bars were numbered with a cold iron stamp, and the numbering was the mill’s numbering, and the gold was the mill’s gold in the sense that the mill had weighed it, entered it, and put a figure against it, and nobody had been able to do anything else with it since, including Corve Merrick, who had put it there.

It could not be spent. It could not be melted. It could not be put anywhere, and the reason it could not be put anywhere was that it was security, and security which has been put somewhere is no longer security, and there is a person upstairs for whom that is the whole of the law.

For two years Corve Merrick had paid interest on it out of his own pocket into a tobacco tin on the sill of the counting house window, four pounds a year, on the first of January, in coin, and had entered nothing anywhere, and had shown it to nobody, and had told nobody, and the tin had been under his own eye every working day.

He had believed the whole time that the sum of the matter was one hundred and eight pounds. He had believed it the way a man believes a thing he has gone over every week for a hundred and four weeks.

On the Tuesday in the second week of February he went up to the roll with the lamp, and the balance was one hundred and thirty-two pounds, and the figure had been reached the way a figure is reached, which is by the interest going on to the balance and the balance going on earning.

He stood in a room four steps colder than the loft below it and did the subtraction twice with his own pencil, because he is a man who does everything twice, and got twenty-four pounds over.

* * *

The gold was the security and it counted toward the payment, and the tobacco tin had eight pounds in it, and eight and a hundred is a hundred and eight, and a hundred and eight is not a hundred and thirty-two.

The counting house float is not the mill’s money. It is the Bristol money, the merchant’s, held against the pay and held against the seed and held against nothing else, and it is counted on a Friday by the master and a clerk and put back in the safe and the count is read out on the Monday.

Corve Merrick took twenty-four pounds out of it on the Wednesday and put it in a canvas bag with the tin and the bars’ record and he signed the float book, which is the one book in the building that is not the merchant’s and is not the mill’s and is not anybody’s, and in which a signature is not a note but a flag.

He signed it, and then he wrote on the line beside his signature the words twelve days short, which is a phrase meaning that the float was light by twelve days of takings, and which is a phrase the merchant’s clerk will read in Bristol in eleven weeks and understand as a scheduling difficulty.

* * *

You go up at any hour. That is the arrangement. There is no appointment to make in the ordinary sense; there is a stair and a door and you knock and after a while the door is opened by nobody you will ever be able to describe, and the room is lit by one lamp and a quarter, and there is a stool, and the tin is on the shelf, and the roll is out of the tin and open, and it is open because it is always open, which was the first thing Corve understood and the last thing he stopped resenting.

He took the roll to the third column on the fourteenth of March of 1998 and read the date and the term and the shape of the call and did not read the name, because he knows the name and it is not written in his own hand anywhere in the building, and there is a rule in Nether Combe that has nothing to do with the mill about a man not writing his wife’s name down.

The term was three nights. The shape of the call was the four words. The balance at lending was one hundred and eight pounds. The fifth column, the outcome column, was empty, and had been empty for two years, which is normal, and which is the entire subject of the afternoon.

He put the gold on the bench in the order it had been weighed, bar by bar, and he read the numbers off the stamp as he set them down, because a man who hands over a hundred pounds of another person’s security should be able to say afterwards which bar was the eleventh.

Then he wrote the payment in the fifth column in his own hand, and he wrote the date, and he read it back the way he reads everything, which is aloud, and then he sat down on the stool and put his watch on his knee.

The rule is an hour. Payment releases the name at the moment of payment, and the lender must hand it back in a sealed envelope within the hour, and it is the only clock in this business that anybody has ever managed to keep, and Corve Merrick had read that out in the counting house to two men and a boy and a factor and meant it.

He sat on the stool for one hour and three minutes.

There is no window in the room and there is no fire and it is four steps colder than the loft. After about a quarter of an hour he took his coat off. He read the whole page twice, both columns, and the entry he had just made, and the page before it, which is 1791. At the fifty-minute mark he said the whole thing aloud once, to the room, in a flat voice, giving the date, the term, the shape of the call, the amount, and the balance struck off in full, and then he stopped, because the fourth column does not take a receipt.

At the hour he stood up and put his hand flat on the door, and knocked, which is a thing you do not do on that stair, and said, “The payment is entered. The balance is struck off. I have waited the hour.”

Then he went down, forty steps, which is two more than the ones that come up, because the stair turns.

* * *

There are three things a miller can do about a thing like that, and Corve Merrick went through all three of them in the counting house on the Wednesday evening with the stove going and the lamp hard on the desk.

The first is to take it.

To take it, he would have to go up and take the roll. The roll is bound in five hundred and sixty pages going back to 1791, and it is not a thing with a page in it that can be had; it is a thing with a page in it that can be missed. If the roll is taken and opened on a table, the binder will find the absence inside a week, because the binder has had his hands on every name in one form or another for thirty years and can read about half of them and knows the weight of the leaves before he reads them. And if the mill took the roll, then the mill is the thief, and the roll itself is the only instrument in this valley that can prove it, and the mill would have to write that down in its own hand, in the fifth column, in the same book it had taken.

He turned that over for a long time. It came down to arithmetic. A thief with a book is still a thief, and worse, because now there is a record.

The second is to sue.

To sue, you need a party. Corve Merrick went to the name in the third column in his own mind and stopped, because he cannot put the plaintiff’s name into a heading. He cannot write it on a sheet of paper and hand it to a coroner. He cannot have it read out in a room. He cannot say the words, mine the count, and now I claim, because the claim requires the name, and the name is the thing that has been taken, and so the taking has taken the only instrument that could have been used to complain of the taking.

He wrote that sentence down and looked at it. Then he wrote under it: a claim requires a plaintiff, and a plaintiff requires a name, and the name is the subject of the claim. And then, because he was tired: therefore the remedy and the injury are the same object and the law has nothing to say.

The third is to wait.

Waiting was his whole craft. He had made his career out of it. He had waited out four bad quarters and two Bristol winters and a wheel that had to be rebuilt in a fortnight, and he had never once waited for anything he had not seen, and this was the first thing he had ever been asked to wait for that he could not look at.

And the counting house was the only room in the mill with a fire in it, and the man who stood in it at night was the man whose whole life was the mill’s paper, and the mill’s paper was the only thing anybody in Nether Combe could be lent against, and he had just spent twenty-four pounds of it on a person who was not in the building.

* * *

Pell Harrow had been at the Combe for nine days before the payment, in a grey coat, with a leather document case that was not dust, and he had the Bristol factor’s compliments and a seat at the mill’s table at Michaelmas and a great deal of patience, and on the Friday morning he was in the counting house with his hand on the case.

“I gather there was a difficulty,” he said. “Strictly speaking it is no business of a factor to put a mill’s private arrangements in his mouth.”

“What difficulty?”

“The sort that is usually settled in an hour and is not.” He did not open the case. “For my part I would not have raised it at all. I have a bolt of cloth’s worth of paper in here that I have never had the least occasion to show anybody, and I have carried it about the combe for nine days hoping it would stay in the case.”

“What is it?”

“It is a list,” Harrow said. “An 1841 list, taken by a clerk of the Bristol house, of every name this mill has ever lent. Every one. Two hundred and eleven entries, with dates. It came into my hands with a bolt of cloth and it has been no use to me for thirty years and I have got rather tired of carrying it.”

Corve Merrick put his hand on the iron tally-board and left it there.

“How would you make use of it.”

“By showing that the roll is short. Two hundred and eleven against five hundred and something, and the ones I have are the ones with dates, and the ones I have run from 1791 to 1841 in a clean hand with no gaps I can see, which means the clerk copied a complete thing. If the mill’s roll has a hole in it, and I am not saying it has, then the mill has been lending on a record that is not a record, for a hundred and forty years, and the mill’s paper is the mill’s paper.”

“It is the mill’s paper.”

“It is,” Harrow said. “And I would want to be very careful before I said so in Bristol, and I would want the mill to be extremely careful before it let a person leave a room having said so, and I am not saying the roll has a hole. I am saying I have a list and that I would rather not have it, and that you have had an hour and it has gone past, and I would like to go home and be a person who never carried anything.”

He put the case down on the desk, and did not open it, and left his hand on it, and Corve Merrick understood the entire shape of the next year in about a second and a half, and the shape of it was this: a recorded theft is believed. A fraud is believed when it is on the roll. A fraud is the only kind of proof this valley has ever accepted, and a theft is written in the same column in the same hand, and if the mill writes down that it was robbed, the mill has said out loud, in its own book, in the only script anybody here can read, that the roll cannot be relied on, and then a Bristol factor with a list of two hundred and eleven dates walks into a counting house in Bristol and says so.

The fifth column has four precedents of a name lent and paid and not returned, and Aldis Prowse read all four of them aloud in order on the Monday morning, and three of them are in a hand nobody can date, and the fourth is 1861.

“So the mill took it,” Jory Vasc said. “Then the mill could do it. Then the mill has a precedent.”

“The mill has a precedent and the mill has a page,” Aldis said. “And the page is the reason the binder will not sign and the reason the man at the top of the combe came down the lane on his own two feet and will not put flax under our paper, and it has been sitting in that book for a hundred and forty years, and nobody had to say a word about it. They did not have to explain anything. There is a page.”

“What happened to them?”

“I am not going to read the third column,” Aldis Prowse said, and went on, “and the second of April, eighteen sixty-one, lent for three nights, shape of the call —”

“Wait,” Corve Merrick said. “Read the second one. The one before. Read the whole of the second one.”

She read the whole of the second one. Lent for a year, shape of the call the four words, outcome: HELD TO THE END OF THE YEAR, NOT CALLED, RETURNED AT THE YEARS END. In the fifth column of a page about a year, a page about an afternoon, and a page about a lifetime, written by four hands over two hundred years, and not one word in any of the three saying that anything was owed, and not one word saying that anybody had been paid.

Corve Merrick stood with his hand on the iron tally-board and looked at the numbers on it for a while, and did not touch anything else in the room, and said, “That is the difference. That is the whole of it. If a name comes back, nobody writes about it. There is nothing to say about a thing that was returned. A hundred and forty years of this book and the only sentences anybody has ever needed to write are about the ones that did not come back, and there are four of them, and I have just made five, and I am not writing it, because the fifth one is the only one I could prove.”

“You could prove it by writing it.”

“I could prove it by writing it,” Corve Merrick said, “in a book that goes up a stair, in a hand that a man upstairs will read, in a house that cannot be got at, and I would be putting a sentence in a book that says a lender took a name and kept it, and the lender is the only person in this valley that nobody can name, and so the sentence would be about a person who cannot be named, and everybody in this valley would read it, and every one of them would go up and look at the man they can name. And it would be the mill’s sentence. It is in the mill’s book. It would be the mill that put it there.”

* * *

It took until the last week in April, and nobody did anything, and that is how a town decides things.

The tow shed went first, and it went in quantities, because the shed is where the conversation is not an account, and it went the way a quantity goes. The women in the shed keep a cloth account with the mill between them, a small one, folded into a ledger of their own, and the mill has never asked to see it and has never once been repaid out of it. In the last week of February eleven of them stopped paying in. Then nineteen. Then the whole of the second row.

They gave reasons. Every one of them gave the same reason, in a dozen different words, which was not the same reason at all. The reason was: the mill has paid and got nothing back, and a mill that has paid and got nothing back is a mill that is being robbed, and if it is being robbed, then the man upstairs has it, or the man upstairs is standing next to whoever has it, and either way the mill’s paper is a piece of paper with a person standing behind it, and you do not put your weeks of spinning into a piece of paper with a person standing behind it.

That is not superstition. It is arithmetic with a person in it. Corve Merrick could not have argued with it, and he did not try, because he had the same arithmetic and had done it eleven times.

The binder stopped signing for the mill on the second of March. The binder is not obliged to sign for the mill; the binder signs for the valley, and the binder is a man of about sixty who has handled every name on the roll in one form or another for thirty years and cannot read half of them, and what the binder said was, in the yard, at volume, on a Tuesday, was that he would sign the valley’s linen when the valley had linen, and that the valley had a great deal of linen and no credit whatever, and that these two facts were going to be discussed for a while.

Then the flax. The grower at the top of the combe sold to the mill for eleven years on forward against the November account, which is the single largest piece of credit in the parish, and the flax is the reason the mill runs at all, and on the fourth of March the man at the top of the combe came down the lane on his own two feet to say that he would not put forty acres of flax under the mill’s paper for another year, and that he was sorry, and that he was sorry because of the hour, and that it had nothing to do with the hour and he wanted it understood that it had nothing to do with the hour.

The pay did not stop. That is the thing to hold on to. The pay did not stop, because the pay comes out of the float and the float was still there and had been signed for, and on the first of March two hundred and six people were paid and the pay board was ruled in nine columns and the chalk was the same chalk.

And at the end of that week a woman of about sixty in the third row of the Sunday congregation leaned over and asked a question she had never asked in nineteen years of walking that road, which was whether it was true that the master had gone up and paid a debt and come down with nothing.

* * *

On the ninth of March Corve Merrick went down to Nanny Rowe’s house in the evening, which he had done twice in nine years, and the lamp was down as far as it goes before he was in the door.

He asked her one question, which was that he wanted to know what a sealed envelope was for.

“It’s for not opening,” Nanny Rowe said. “You seal it so that nobody can say afterwards what was in it, and then the not-opening is the whole of the evidence, and I have always thought that was honest of whoever built it. If I seal a thing, I have not touched it, and the not-touching is the seal, and there is no way to be later in my own house and say you were in it.”

“Is that a thing this house knows about?”

“This house was in service in ninety-four,” she said, “and I am telling you now, as though it were this evening, that the only sealed thing I ever carried up that stair was a christening cap, and it was for a woman’s first child, and the cap was magnificent, and it came down again in the afternoon in the same seal, and the child was a boy. And the cap went into a drawer in this house and stayed in the drawer until I married, and that is the whole of what I know about sealed envelopes.”

She turned the lamp down the last half notch, which is not a movement she makes, because there is no half notch, and he noticed it and she did not.

“It comes back to the house it was sealed in,” she said, “or it does not come back. And the sealing is not the part that decides. I would go home now if I were you, for it is nearly nine and the road is the road.”

* * *

He did not write the theft.

That was decided on the Friday, in the counting house, in about four seconds, and it was the only decision he made that month that he did not have to make, and he could not afterwards say what he decided it with.

What he did instead was put the twenty-four pounds into the mill’s book as a debt owed by the mill to a creditor whose name could not be written, which is a thing a mill’s book can carry, because a book will carry anything if the person signing it is the master and will sign it again next week. It is a liability. It is the first liability the Combe Mill has carried since 1861 and it will be copied into the quarter sheet in June along with the seed and the oil and the cartage, and it will be carried as long as the mill stands, and the Bristol clerk will read it in a quarter of a minute and think it a joke.

Then he took the tobacco tin and emptied it, and the eight pounds of interest went back into the float, and he signed for that too, and the flag in the float book is now two flags in a fortnight.

And on the last Friday in April he took the envelope that had come down the stair empty — because that was what it was; it had come down on the Tuesday, pushed under the door at half past two in the afternoon, seal intact, and when he had opened it in the yard there was nothing in it and the paper was the same paper as the roll and had never been near the roll — and he nailed it to the inside of the counting house door, at eye height, where a man coming in for the pay has to stand and look at it.

He wrote nothing on it.

By the middle of June there were four of them on that wall, and three of the men who had put them up had not asked him and had not spoken to him, and the mill took them down on the first of the month the same as it took down the pay.
