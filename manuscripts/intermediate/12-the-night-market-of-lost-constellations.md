# The Night Market of Lost Constellations

![Cover art: The Night Market of Lost Constellations](../art/intermediate/12-the-night-market-of-lost-constellations.webp)

At 11:47 on a rainless night, a market appeared between the abandoned stations of Bellwether.

Wren saw it first, when broken glass replaced the tracks and striped awnings appeared, lanterns dangling from walls that did not exist. People in red raincoats carried baskets while a bell rang once somewhere behind them.

Wren stopped so quickly that Talo walked into her back, and she pointed toward the impossible awnings.

“Look,” she said.

Talo looked, thirteen years old with ink on one cuff and a courier’s red satchel across his chest, and said, “A market,” as if Wren had asked him to confirm the moon.

“An illegal market,” Wren said, though the crooked rows and red-coated sellers offered little evidence of good behavior.

“That part is harder to see,” Talo replied, scanning the market for a keeper.

A brass sign above the nearest awning read NIGHT MARKET OF LOST CONSTELLATIONS. Underneath, in smaller letters, it said: EVERY LIGHT HAS A NAME. SPEAK THE NAME AND PAY FOR THE LIGHT.

Wren touched the sealed tube in her satchel, which held a letter, a star chart, and a warning written by the astronomer Vela. The instructions said to give it to the keeper before the first bell after midnight, while Talo checked the time by the stars.

“Too late,” he said.

A lantern swung open, and a girl with a shaved head and a silver nose ring leaned from the ticket window. “Too late for what?”

“For the first bell,” Talo said, and Nia smiled as she told them they had until the second.

The market smelled of roasted chestnuts, wet wool, and hot metal. Stalls stood in crooked rows, each one displaying a glass dome. Under the glass floated tiny lights: a green spark, a pale blue bead, a gold thread burning without flame. Every dome had a card.

A LIGHT FOR LONELY WINDOWS.

A LIGHT FOR SAILORS WHO FORGET THE SHORE.

A LIGHT TO KEEP A HOUSE FROM FEELING TOO LARGE.

The people moved between the stalls with careful hands and lowered voices, while no one touched a dome. When a child asked about the blue bead, the seller placed both palms on the counter.

The seller explained, “That light belonged to Mavi, who promised never to leave her little brother but went west on a train; the star waited for her voice. Can you hear it?”

The child leaned close. A tiny voice inside the dome said, *Mavi?*

The child jumped back.

The seller sighed. “One night. That is all the time a promise has before it turns into a chain.”

Wren looked up and found no sky above the awnings, only a ceiling of deep-blue cloth sewn with holes. Through each hole showed a star, and many had been removed; their absence made the roof sag beneath the weight of all the dark spaces.

Talo touched the chart in Wren’s satchel and said, “The market is not selling new stars.” Wren studied the empty places overhead. “It is selling old ones,” she said.

Farther down the aisle, a woman arranged jars in a perfect circle. Her long hair was white at the temples, and a brass key hung from her neck. Nia had not mentioned a second keeper.

“Every lost constellation has a story,” the woman said. “And every story has a price. One memory, one promise, or one name. Choose carefully.”

“What happens if the star says no?” Talo asked.

The woman’s fingers stopped on a jar, and her answer was short: “Then it is not for sale.”

A commotion rose near the rear of the market. A small star had escaped its stall.

It rolled through the crowd as a bead of blue fire, leaving sparks on the wet stones, and people stepped aside in surprise. Wren chased it under a table and caught it in her scarf, where it felt cold like winter water.

“Let me go,” said a voice from the scarf.

The voice was neither old nor young. It sounded like someone trying to speak through a closed door.

“We can help you,” Wren said.

“You can help me to stay.”

The words made Talo uneasy, so he leaned over the scarf and asked, “Where is home?”

The star was quiet for a moment. “I do not know. The person who named me left. The people who bought me did not want me. I have been in a box since I was bright.”

Nia pushed through the crowd, took the scarf, and studied the star seriously. “Do not make promises in a market,” she said.

“I want to go home,” the star said.

Nia’s face softened, and she answered with quiet certainty, “Then we must find it.”

Talo unfolded the star chart, where Vela’s handwriting crowded the margins; she had circled missing stars and written, THE SKY IS NOT A CUPBOARD.

“The astronomer knew about this market,” Talo said, tapping the warning, and Nia replied, “She knew about more than the market. Come.”

She led them behind the ticket window and down a stairwell beneath the tracks, where the stairs twisted into a round room filled with maps. Each showed the same region in a different year. On the oldest map, the Night Market stood in a field; on the newest, it stood nowhere at all.

Vela’s letter lay on a reading table, and Nia cut the red seal with a small knife before passing the pages to Wren.

The first page described a change in the sky, explaining how people had promised stars light for generations: *When I cannot see you, be my light. When I am lost, show me the way. When I am afraid, keep one piece of night for me.* The promises were not commands, because a star could answer or refuse; most answered for a while, while some answered for generations. A promise was a bridge, and a bridge could be left standing after both ends changed. But people began leaving their promises behind, moving away, marrying strangers, or finding brighter work. They told themselves the stars were only lights, though they did not hear the small voices that continued to answer.

The abandoned stars fell into the market, and Nia tapped the second page to explain that they gathered there while deciding what to do. Merchants sold some as heirlooms, while others bought them to impress friends. The worst traders tried to bind a star to a new owner with a name and ribbon.

Talo frowned and asked, “Then why haven’t you stopped them?”

Nia looked toward the stair and answered, “Because a cage is not a kindness simply because the jailer is sad.”

The letter’s last paragraph was written in a firm, hurried hand.

> A constellation is not a collection of things people own. If a person abandons a star, the star has the right to leave. If a star chooses to remain, it has the right to choose its new shape. A responsible constellation does not ask, *What do I owe the people who left me?* It asks, *What promise can I make honestly?*

Below that paragraph was a sketch of Bellwether’s old signal tower, drawn in quick, slanting lines.

Nia gave Wren the tube and said, “Vela asked you to deliver this to the tower. She said it can open a way to the upper sky, although I thought she was being dramatic.”

Talo looked toward the sketch and asked, “Is the tower still standing?”

“It is standing in the way ruins stand: too tall, too stubborn.”

They climbed through a curtain made of hanging names while the market folded around them, and Wren heard voices calling from the domes. *Do not go. Do not forget. Stay where you are.* Some voices were angry, while others were tired, and one repeated a name over and over, but no answer came.

At the top of the signal tower, the roof had fallen away, and the old brass lens lay beneath a blanket of rain stars. Beyond it, the real sky appeared, making Talo draw a sharp breath.

A broad band of stars had vanished, leaving a ragged black shape like a torn strip in the night. The remaining stars crowded around its edges in an uneasy ring.

Vela waited beside the lens, older than Wren had expected, with silver hair, a red scarf like Wren’s, and a paper bag in one hand. In the other she held a small wooden box.

“You brought the letter,” Vela said, and Talo pointed at the dark shape. “What happened?”

“I made a mistake,” Vela said, opening the box to reveal a clear, empty stone. “This was the heart of the Bellwether constellation, and I was responsible for it.”

Wren remembered the old stories, in which Vela had been Bellwether’s astronomer, charting storms and teaching children to find north by the angle of the Winking Crown.

“I promised the people of the valley that their sky would keep its shape,” Vela said, “and I promised the stars that I would never abandon them. Then I was offered a position at the northern observatory and took it. I told myself the stars belonged to everyone, so everyone else could care for them.”

“Were they?” Talo asked, and Vela answered, “No. They belonged to themselves. I was the one who made the promise, and I was the one who broke it.”

Vela gave the empty stone to Wren and explained, “The heart broke when I left. The other stars went looking for a new promise, found people who had abandoned theirs, and followed them into the market.”

Talo stared at the black shape and said, “So the sky is disappearing because you were free.”

“Yes,” Vela said. “I wanted to be free of Bellwether. I did not ask whether the stars wanted to be free of me.”

For a moment only the wind moved, and then the blue star in Wren’s scarf stirred. *Do you want us to go back?*

Vela lowered her eyes and admitted, “I want you to forgive me, the old shape restored, and myself no longer afraid whenever I look up.”

The star’s voice came again, asking, *Those are your wishes. What are ours?* Vela had no answer.

Wren placed the stone on the brass lens and said, “We need to ask them.” Vela glanced toward the dark spaces and answered, “There are too many before dawn.” Talo took the stone into his palm. “Then we ask one, and then another.”

The market bell sounded below, marking the second bell as dawn pressed against the horizon.

“A constellation can be made by a promise, but not by a command,” Vela said, “because the old pattern cannot return when its promise is dead.”

“Maybe that is why the market is here,” Talo said, tracing the routes on his courier map. “Constellations do not need to return; they need roads.”

He pointed to the lantern stalls below and explained, “Every light belonged to a promise, and the lost stars know which promises were broken. If we put the lights where the promises began, we can show what each star chose.”

Wren understood immediately, looking from the map to the dark sky. “A new constellation made of choices.”

Vela’s face brightened as she said, “Not a pattern anyone owns, but a pattern that stays because everyone in it agrees to help it remain.”

They returned to the market at a run, where Nia and the keepers lit the lamps while sellers opened the domes one by one. Some stars rushed upward, while others hid behind the curtains. A green spark whispered that it wanted a harbor, not a house, and a pale blue light said it wanted to shine over a road where travelers had once lied. Mavi’s small star remained silent, its light trembling in Wren’s scarf.

Wren carried it to the highest stall and asked, “Do you want to find Mavi?” The star trembled and answered, *No.*

“Would you like to choose something else?” Wren asked, and after a pause the star replied, *Yes.*

It flew toward the roof, and the hole in the blue cloth widened around it. For an instant, the market had no ceiling at all, and the lost stars rose with it in a loose, bright tangle rather than the old shapes. Wren saw the Mavi star settle near a traveling road, where it lit the hands of strangers helping one another across a flooded bridge.

Other stars joined it, including a gold one that chose the schoolhouse. A red one stayed above the station because a courier had once returned a child’s lost button, and a green one became a beacon for fishermen who never lied about the weather.

Some did not rise, sinking into the earth and taking their light with them, and Wren let them go because freedom did not always look like staying visible.

At dawn, the market vanished as the striped awnings folded into the mist and the ticket window became a wall of ivy. Only the brass sign remained, bright in the morning sun.

Wren and Talo walked back toward the abandoned stations with the astronomer beside them. Above them, the new constellation was crooked but steady. Its stars formed a shape no old chart had contained. It looked less like a crown and more like two roads joining at a crossroads.

Talo watched the new lights with a worried frown and asked, “Will it last?”

Vela watched the new lights. “That is not ours to promise. A constellation owes its sky only this: it must keep the truth of its own choosing.”

At the edge of the field, Wren looked back once, and in the last dark corner of the market a single lantern burned beside an empty space on the ground. No voice called for a name, and no chain waited for a buyer. The lantern swung in the morning wind, keeping watch over a door that had finally learned to open both ways.
