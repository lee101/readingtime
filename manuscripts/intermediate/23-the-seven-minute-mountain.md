# The Seven-Minute Mountain

![Cover art: The Seven-Minute Mountain](../art/intermediate/23-the-seven-minute-mountain.webp)

Mero counted the mountain's steps with the tip of her red climbing pole. One. Two. Three.

Her watch jumped seven minutes after each click.

She looked at Aunt Tavi, who stood below on the wet stone stair in a moss-green jacket, both hands steady on the safety rope. A white patch on Tavi's sleeve shone where mist had settled.

"Did you see that?" Mero called.

"I saw it," Tavi answered.

She checked the time again. At ten-thirty she had left the lower station. Now the hands showed ten fifty-one. She had climbed only three steps.

On her map, the summit stood one hundred twenty-six steps above the valley. The little brass badge printed on the back was waiting for a notch beside its picture. If the climb followed the rule, the walk to the top would take more than fourteen ordinary hours.

"You said we could reach the lookout before the storm," Mero said.

"I said we could reach the one-hour marker." Tavi pointed uphill. "Fourteen steps."

Mero slid the badge into her jacket. Her blue rain shell was already damp at the elbows. She had tied her yellow scarf twice, but its loose end kept catching on branches. She pulled harder on the rope and climbed.

They did not hurry. At step four, Tavi checked the knot. At step five, she showed Mero how to move the pole behind the downhill boot before reaching for the next hold. At step six, Mero's shoe slipped on wet rock. Tavi's gloved hand closed around the rope, and the tight line kept Mero from sliding.

"Seven minutes bought us time," Mero said.

"It bought us care," Tavi replied. "Not safety for free."

That was the mountain's rule. The peak's shadow moved at one-seventh speed. A walker stepping from the lower station toward the summit gave the mountain one minute for each step, while the valley's ordinary clock counted seven minutes for each mountain minute. Weather arrived in mountain time, yet a real storm crossed the real sky.

At the eighth step, a wall of cloud swallowed the trees below. The air smelled of wet stone. Far down the slope, the lower station bell sounded for the last safe trail hour.

"How long until the storm?" Mero asked.

Tavi studied the sky. "It reached the western ridge at noon. It reaches us near two. It is moving in ordinary time."

Mero looked at her watch. Eleven fifty-four.

They had passed step twelve when Mero heard a bark below them.

It came from beneath the lowest rail of the lookout stair, a narrow ledge hidden by wet fern. A small brown dog stood there with its front paws braced against the rock. A red harness loop was caught around a branch, and rainwater made the bark tremble.

Mero leaned over the rail. The dog barked again.

"There is a way down," she said.

Tavi crouched, never letting the rope grow slack. "There is a ledge. That is different."

"The way down is three footholds."

"It is wet, and there is a drop below the handhold."

Mero saw the route. It looked easy from above. Each foothold was broad enough for a boot, though the lowest was furred with moss. A length of blue cord hung from the branch holding the dog. The free end ended above the ledge.

Mero pointed to it. "We can tie our rope to that. Then I can climb down while you hold me."

"And if the branch moves?"

"Then we both know."

Tavi's mouth tightened. She checked the anchor around the railing post and the steel wedge above it. Then she clipped Mero's harness to a second loop.

"Half an inch of play," Tavi said. "No more."

Mero descended. The first foothold held. The second rang when her pole touched it but stayed solid. On the third, moss gave beneath her heel. She swung away from the wall, caught the blue cord with one hand, and held herself above the drop.

Tavi locked the belay device. "Mero. Tell me what you need."

"Keep the rope tight."

"I have it. That is not the same as helping you climb."

Mero looked up. Tavi's shoulders leaned back, her boots braced wide. The safety line ran from Tavi's harness to the wedge, over the rail, and into Mero's hands.

"Feed me the red loop," Mero said. "Then pull when I say."

Tavi clipped the red loop onto the rope and fed it down. Mero caught it, secured it to her harness, and let out slack so the line was not fighting her.

"Up," Mero called.

Tavi pulled in measured jerks. Mero climbed from the mossy foothold to the middle one, then to the rail. Her knees struck stone, but the second loop kept her from dropping when her left pole slipped. Tavi hauled until Mero lay safe on the stair.

"Now the dog," Mero said.

"Clipped harness first," Tavi replied.

Tavi descended instead. With the rope secured above, she reached the branch, cut the loop from the collar with her small knife, and fastened the dog to her own harness. The dog pressed against her jacket, trembling.

"Climb back when I say," Tavi called.

Mero hauled. The branch bent, but the wedge held. Tavi climbed in short, firm movements while Mero kept the rope steady. The dog clawed once against the stone and then came over the rail.

Mero clipped on their lead and pointed to the tiny stone shelter ten steps above. "The summit storm door is there. We can wait."

"The lookout is twelve steps farther. Your marker is fourteen."

Mero looked toward the hidden peak. "The dog first."

Tavi nodded. "The dog first."

They climbed slowly. Mero kept count.

Thirteen.

Fourteen.

Fifteen.

Sixteen.

Seventeen.

Eighteen.

Nineteen.

Twenty.

Twenty-one.

Twenty-two.

Mero's watch showed two-ten.

The first gust struck like a hand against her chest. Rain came sideways, needling her cheeks. Thunder rolled from the western ridge without a flash.

The second gust tore the little shelter door from its lower hinge. It slammed against the rock, and the dog scrambled against Tavi's legs.

"Bring the dog inside," Mero said.

Tavi lifted it with both arms. Mero fought the door back into place. Lightning forked over the valley, lighting every fern white. A rock slid down somewhere below them and struck the stair with a hard crack.

Tavi pulled Mero behind the shelter wall. "No more steps outside."

"The lookout door might lock itself."

"It will hold through the storm. We wait."

"But the mountain time—"

"Will not stop for us," Tavi said. "That is the part you must learn."

Mero pressed her watch against her ear. It kept moving, tick after tick. The sound filled the shelter. The dog panted against her boots. Tavi crouched beside her, one hand on the wall, the other resting on the rope.

"How long?" Mero asked.

"We watch the lightning, not the watch. Count the gap."

Mero counted.

One bright flash struck the lower ridge.

She said, "One," when thunder arrived.

The next flash came after a long breath.

"Two."

The storm moved through. Rain drummed on the roof. Wind shook grit against the walls. Once, a heavy stone struck the shelter's outer corner hard enough to send dust over their hoods. Tavi kept Mero low and made her keep hold of the dog.

They waited through three separate waves. When the last thunder faded, the cloud remained. Mero could not see the summit, and her watch said four-thirty-two.

"The minutes are gone," she said.

"They spent themselves while we did what we had to do."

"The lookout route is probably buried."

Tavi stepped outside. The rail held, but the path beyond it was hidden under fresh rubble and shallow water. She tested the first stones with her pole.

"We do not go farther today," she said.

Mero touched the brass badge in her pocket. "I wanted the summit."

"I know."

"The map was clear."

"Clear maps do not account for a dog under every marker."

Mero almost argued. The peak had hidden her choice from above. It had not made the choice for her. That was harder to accept than the broken trail.

Tavi secured the dog across her chest and clipped their rope. "Follow my steps. Short pole. Good feet. No one rushes."

They began the descent. Every step took one mountain minute, or seven ordinary ones. The number no longer felt like a trick. It was the true cost of choosing a path.

At step twelve, Mero's watch passed five-fifty. At step seven, fog began to rise. At step four, the dog began to whine. Tavi rested for only one step, then continued.

The lower station came back slowly. First its bell appeared, then its roof, then a line of lanterns trembling in the rain. The warden came out with a blanket and a warm box for the dog. He looked at the dark peak and shook his head.

Tavi said, "The dog was caught under the lookout rail."

"Your storm report was due at two," Mero said.

"It was," Tavi replied. "We will explain why it was late."

Mero took the report. She wrote the time, the trapped harness, the shelter, and the path they had used. She did not write that the mountain had stolen their climb. It had not. They had spent the climb.

When they reached the station, the real evening came. Mero's hands shook as she unclipped the safety rope. The brass badge stayed in her pocket, unmarked.

Tavi handed her dry socks.

"Tomorrow," Mero said, "we can try again."

Tavi looked toward Emberpeak, gray and dripping.

"Tomorrow we can learn another route," she said. "On an ordinary day, with a proper plan and a long breakfast."

Mero smiled. At the station table, she ate slowly while the rescued dog slept under her chair. Each bite was ordinary time, safely spent.
