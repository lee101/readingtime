# The Opera House Without a Soprano

![Cover art: The Opera House Without a Soprano](../art/intermediate/35-the-opera-house-without-a-soprano.webp)

Wren Arkwright learned the Nine Hinges by their noises. Hinge one was a low hum you felt in your back teeth. The house stood in a fold of the hills where the wind came over the ridge and gave up, and it had a reputation for weather.

Wren was thirteen, in a canvas apron with eleven pockets, and she walked in a permanent crouch, because an apprentice who stood up straight got hit by things.

The house was failing, and Wren had known it three weeks and told nobody, because true things at the Nine Hinges got assigned to you. Then the chandelier over row K came down three inches and stopped there, hanging like a held breath.

* * *

Ilsa Brannock was sweeping the stalls in her house coat with one earring in. She had not sung in eleven years.

"You are early," she said without looking up.

"The chandelier is in the air."

"Mm."

"Ms. Brannock."

"Call me Ilsa, child, and I will call you trouble." She leaned the broom against a seat back. "How long?"

"Three weeks. It started with a door. Stage left, the one to the coal cellar. It opened onto a snowfield. Just white, about four feet deep, with footprints in it, and the footprints went away from us."

Ilsa stopped sweeping, and put something away in her face the way she used to put away a hand before a high note.

"The holding note," Wren said.

"So it has got to the doors."

* * *

The register keeper came on Mondays, and it happened to be a Monday.

His name was Sowerby Quill: four feet nine inches tall, a clerk of the order of theaters, carrying the Nine Hinges' only book in a tin box against the damp.

"The theater is a held thing," he said, setting the tin down. "Nothing here exists from one night to the next unless a voice is holding it in place."

"Whose voice is standing there," he said before she could ask.

He opened the book, worn near the spine into a valley of hands.

"Brannock, Ilsa. Eleven years standing. Retired the spring the boiler was replaced. She did not finish the note."

"She finished the note," Wren said. "Everyone in the balcony heard her finish it."

"She finished the applause." He shut the book. "A held thing requires a held note, in the room, in the present tense, meaning it. If nobody is holding it, the theater is not waiting to be saved. It is falling, and it will fall on whatever is nearest."

Ilsa had come in behind them, and stood in the wings and said, in a very even voice, "He is right."

* * *

They called the crew.

Bram Halloway, the props master, arrived with half a papier-mache horse under one arm. "Every instrument in the props store," he said. "Half are real." Odell Fenn, fourteen, came already singing birdsong, having been told for years he was tone deaf. "I can hold six notes badly and very long. Why not use me."

Petra Vance, the prompter, planted herself in the corner in a coat two sizes too big, arms crossed. "Words are sound. If we are short a voice, we are not short of words." And Grissel Farrow came up the road from the lower pasture, sleeves lined with feathers. "Nine hundred of the best voices in this valley are asleep in the trees."

* * *

They rehearsed for nine days, and the spell did not care for substitutes.

Bram played a swell of strings on a harmonium, a hurdy-gurdy, and a triangle. Petra read the whole Canticle aloud. Thirty-one people held their breath on cue.

The holding note built. Wren felt it in her back teeth, that low hum, the thing the room leaned toward. She got the last line halfway up, and the chandelier over row K began, slowly, to descend.

It fell the rest of the way and hit the floor, and the painted moon on the backdrop sagged like a wet cloth, came away from its frame, and settled onto the boards in a soft blue heap.

* * *

On the tenth night Quill came back with the book, and it was heavier.

He had them all out into the street, Ilsa included, and locked the nine doors behind them with a brass key that turned nine times.

"The held thing must be paid for. That is the second rule, and nobody remembers it, because the first rule is more interesting."

"One true thing," he said. "Not gold. Something you are actually giving up, knowing what it is, so that it stays gone. If you give the theater something you meant to keep, it takes it and gives you nothing." He hugged the tin box. "And it will not take a substitute. Nobody else can pay your price. That is the one mercy in the arrangement."

Bram sat down on a step.

"Whose price?" Wren asked.

Quill turned the book around. There, in Wren's own handwriting, in the column a theater uses to say whose keeping it is, was a line she had written at eleven, because the apprentices wrote the keeping lines.

ARKWRIGHT, W. HOUSE HELD. NO PLEDGE.

"You were a child," Ilsa said. "You did not know there was a question."

"I know now."

"Wren." Ilsa's voice changed. It was a woman on a step in the cold, asking. "Do you know what the book will take?"

"No."

"Then you do not get to choose," Quill said. "You would be gambling. The book only deals with people who know the price. Bring your answer in writing."

* * *

Wren did not write anything for six days. She thought about it in the flies, upside down over row K, with birds settling in the rafters below.

When she was four, her mother had sung her a tune in the dark. One tune, over and over, an old thing about a boat. And Wren had kept it by humming it every night since, walking home. Her mother's voice existed nowhere else on earth except in Wren's mouth, and if she stopped humming it, it would be as if it had never happened.

That was the price, and she knew it exactly, down to the note.

On the seventh day she came to Quill's lodgings with a page of his paper, folded.

"Write it plainly. No cleverness. The book does not read well of them."

She wrote: I, Wren Arkwright, apprentice, keeper of this house since my eleventh year, give the sound of my mother's song. I will not hear it again. I know it was hers and not mine, and I have been keeping it as though it were mine.

Quill read it twice. Then he bowed and laid his hand flat on the page, as though to keep it from being clever about.

"Seventeen years ago a singer brought me a line almost exactly like that one, and it remains the worst thing I have ever read."

He put the page in the book and closed it, and all nine hinges swung once, together, in a wind that had not come down off the ridge.

* * *

They gave the performance on the last night of October, and at Bram's insistence they called it a dress rehearsal.

The house was full. Thirty-one people in the stalls and eleven children of the parish standing on the stage itself. Petra Vance did not prompt once.

The first scene was the boat. Grissel's birds took it: blackbird, thrush, wren, and then a heron making a sound like a rope being drawn in, one note long and the whole length of a held rope. Under it Bram played a low plain chord, and the audience held.

The last scene was the note. That is the entire plot of the Canticle. A woman holds a note so that her house does not fall, and holds it until she cannot, and the house falls, and the song is what is left.

Wren walked out to the center of the stage and sang the last line in the highest key her voice would admit, and it was clear, and it did not hold.

Halfway through the line the chandelier over row K began to rise and the eight others began to sink, and the house disagreed with itself.

And from the fourth seat of row K, Ilsa Brannock stood up.

She sang one note.

It was a bad note: the low thing under her chest, cracked and reedy, pushed up out of sixty years of a throat that had been put in a case and never unlocked. It was the most honest sound made in that building in a hundred and forty years.

"You let it go," she said. "You had four quarters left and every person in that balcony, and you let it go, because you were afraid of the day it would not be there." She set her hand on the seat back. "I was the one who was supposed to hold it. There is no version of this where I was brave. The house is not waiting for me, and I will not make children pay for what I was too frightened to keep giving."

Wren looked at her, and understood the whole shape of it standing there with a paint-thin voice in her ears. The book had taken her mother's tune. That morning she had reached for it and found the place where it had been, warm, and nothing.

She sang.

Not the soprano part. The part underneath, where the melody goes when you have put it into somebody else's mouth and they have taken it out. It was a kid's note, and going to fail in eleven seconds. She sang it anyway, honestly, meaning every word of it.

It failed.

* * *

The house did not go up.

The house went to pieces, and the pieces stayed.

Every candle leaned, and instead of going out they all blew inward toward the middle of the auditorium. Odell Fenn opened his mouth and produced a sound that was not his: the whole keening of the pasture, and every wing asleep in the trees.

And the thirty-one people in the stalls and the eleven children on the stage and Bram Halloway with both hands on the keys and a paper moon coming apart in midair all together gave the one note.

Not a good one. Not a soprano.

A chord. A hundred and forty-odd parts of it: a reed here, a bird there, an old woman's cracked thread, held together by the plain fact that every person in the room was holding it for somebody else.

The chandeliers rose. The painted moon held. The nine hinges swung once and locked, in order, like a long sentence finally finished.

* * *

Wren could not remember the tune. That was the arrangement: the sound of her mother's song, gone, known gone, staying gone.

What she had instead was a thing nobody had told her the book would leave behind. She remembered the windows. She remembered warm sleep coming up over her out of the dark, and being carried.

Some things are taken and some things are left. She told Ilsa so in the morning, on the boards, with a broom in her hands, because the work did not stop.

"I know it was hers," Wren said. "I know it was warm."

"That is not the same as having it," Ilsa said.

"No," Wren said. "It isn't."

* * *

In March the Nine Hinges Opera House took its curtain call, and nobody will tell you what it was for.

The soprano's part is not sung from the stage.

Wren Arkwright, nineteen, an apprentice no longer, walked to the center of the boards at the end of every performance and held a single note in the middle of her chest while the rest of the house built on top of it: the birds from the pasture, Bram's hands, Petra's words, and one cracked reed in row K that could no longer have filled a chapel and did not need to.

The house holds.

But nobody in the Nine Hinges will close a door now without standing in the frame. Bram has nailed all nine hinges open at the top. Petra keeps the register of the evening, and the last line of every page is the same:

house held. no pledge. everyone.
