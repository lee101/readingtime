# The Thirteenth Dancing Princess
![Cover art: The Thirteenth Dancing Princess](../art/intermediate/12-the-thirteenth-dancing-princess.webp)

The palace ballroom had twelve chandeliers.

Lio noticed a thirteenth light in the east window. It was small and green, like a star that had not been polished.

From the gallery, he watched the princesses practice.

The eldest sister, Celene, turned on one toe. A violinist played a bright note. Celene turned again, faster, until her silver shoe flashed.

Second sister Miva practiced a curtsy. Third sister Ora leapt over a painted river. Fourth sister Nessa counted steps with her fingers. Their dresses rustled like bright water.

Their mother stood beside the music master.

“Again,” she said. “From the first mark.”

Celene’s smile stayed fixed. “Mother, I am tired.”

“One dance is not a journey.”

“It feels like a journey when the floor watches.”

The music master raised his bow. The dance began again.

Lio looked toward the green light. Something moved behind the glass. A thin figure stepped into the shadows. It had a crown of black feathers and a skirt stitched from moonless cloth.

The figure lifted one finger to her lips.

Then a bell rang below.

The princesses hurried away. The ballroom doors closed. The green light vanished.

That night, Lio could not sleep. He worked in the palace kitchens, kneading bread and carrying bowls. The cook told him not to wander. Lio listened, then went to the gallery anyway.

The thirteenth princess was waiting there.

“You saw me,” she said.

“Are you a ghost?”

“I am a record.”

Lio stared. The figure was solid enough to cast a shadow. Yet when Lio looked at her face, it shifted from young to old and back again.

“What record?”

“The record of the dance.”

The princess pointed down. A ledger lay open on the floor. Pages rustled without wind. Each page carried a name and a time.

Celene, one hour. Miva, one hour. Ora, one hour and twelve minutes.

Lio read farther. He found all twelve sisters, each with a long list.

“They practice every night,” he said.

“They must.”

“For the prince?”

“For the story.”

The word story sounded heavy.

The princess picked up the ledger. “We are the Twelve Dancing Princesses. Everyone believes we dance because joy calls us here. But the song was written to hide a cost.”

“What cost?”

“Every dance takes something. First the breath. Then the color of the cheeks. Then the desire to rest. At last it takes the memory of why the music matters.”

Lio looked at the pages. Beside Celene’s name, a small line read: *Smile: borrowed. Feet: obedient. Heart: tired.*

“Do the sisters know?”

“They know their bodies hurt. They do not know the palace has taken the meaning of the hurt.”

Below them, a door opened. Princess Miva climbed toward the gallery. She wore a soft blue dress and no shoes.

“Who is there?” she called.

Lio stepped behind a curtain. The thirteenth princess vanished.

Miva saw only the open ledger. She touched the page with her name. Her face softened with relief.

“It is quiet,” she whispered. “When I look, it says I danced for the kingdom.”

“The song is a spell,” Lio said.

Miva caught the curtain. “I know. But if I stop, everyone will say I am lazy.”

The music master’s voice echoed from the stairs. “Your sisters are waiting.”

Miva closed the ledger. “Tomorrow, tell them. If you can make them listen.”

* * *

At the next practice, Lio hid behind a tapestry.

Celene practiced the first dance. She landed on the blue mark perfectly.

Miva copied her. Ora followed. One after another, the sisters moved through the ballroom. Their skirts flashed. Their hairpins trembled.

The music grew faster. The sisters’ smiles stayed the same.

Lio counted. Celene’s steps slowed. Miva’s breathing turned shallow. Ora’s fingers tightened around her partner’s hand. By the final turn, seven sisters leaned on one another.

The music stopped.

Celene bowed. “Beautiful,” the queen called from the balcony.

The sisters bowed lower. Lio saw a thin thread of silver light leaving their shoes and sinking through the floor.

The thirteenth princess appeared beside him.

“Each dance feeds the palace’s old promise,” she whispered. “It promises that beauty will keep the kingdom safe.”

“Does it?”

“The king fears war. The court fears boredom. The people fear losing the dances that make them proud. The promise feeds on their fear.”

Lio stepped from behind the tapestry.

Celene saw him. “Kitchen boy, why are you here?”

Lio’s mouth went dry. He pointed to the floor. A silver thread led toward the throne.

“Your dances are disappearing.”

Miva stepped forward. “What do you mean?”

“The palace is taking pieces of you and calling them a performance.”

The music master frowned. “A child’s fantasy.”

Lio pulled the ledger from his sleeve. He had brought it. Its pages opened by themselves.

*The first princess gave her joy.*

*The second gave her patience.*

*The third gave her morning.*

The sisters stared.

Celene’s face went pale. “We were told the dances were our duty.”

“Duty does not grow roots in the floor,” Lio said.

The music master raised his bow. “Finish the dance.”

The sisters looked at one another. None moved.

For the first time, the ballroom waited.

* * *

The queen came down from the balcony.

She wore silver at her temples. Her eyes were tired in a way Lio had not noticed before.

“Who gave you that book?” she asked.

“The palace did,” Lio said.

The queen touched the ledger. A page turned to a portrait of her own younger self, dancing beneath the same chandeliers. Her face shone with joy. The court clapped at the edge of the picture.

“I loved the dances,” she said.

The music master bowed low. “Your majesty, the celebration must continue. The people expect—”

“The people expect to be dazzled.” The queen closed the ledger. “As I did.”

The sisters looked at her.

“I was told the old promise protected the kingdom,” she said. “When a neighboring army threatened us, the court danced for seven nights. We won. No soldier came near our borders.”

“Did the spell do that?” Miva asked.

“I think the army feared a kingdom that could make such a beautiful song. I think beauty can be a wall. But I also think a wall can become a room with no windows.”

She looked at the green light in the east window.

“Who are you?” she asked.

The thirteenth princess stepped into the ballroom. Her crown of black feathers trembled.

“I am what the dance left behind,” she said. “Every sister gives something. I keep it so it is not lost.”

The sisters gathered around her. Ora reached out and took her hand. The princess’s fingers were cold.

“Can we give it back?” Nessa asked.

“Not by dancing harder,” said the thirteenth. “You must choose what the dance is for.”

The queen asked, “What if the kingdom falls without the spell?”

The thirteenth princess looked toward the windows. Beyond them lay fields, gardens, fishing boats, and roads crowded with people. There were no enemy banners.

“Perhaps the kingdom has learned to protect itself,” she said.

The queen nodded slowly. “Then we release the promise.”

She turned to the sisters. “Tomorrow, I will announce that the nightly dance is ended. We will have one celebration. It will be short, imperfect, and chosen by all of you.”

Celene lowered her eyes. “Father may be angry.”

“He may.”

“Will we dance again?”

“You may,” the queen said. “But you will not dance because a promise is pulling at your feet.”

* * *

The celebration took place on the last night of summer.

The sisters planned it themselves.

Celene invited the cooks. Miva invited the gardeners. Ora invited the musicians. Nessa invited the guards and their families. They placed long tables on the terrace. They served bread, honey cakes, cherries, and cold mint tea.

No one wore a crown.

The court arrived in bright clothes, carrying expectations. The queen entered without her usual silver ornaments. The king followed her, looking uncertain. Lio stood near the kitchen doors, holding a tray of warm rolls.

At the first music, the sisters did not begin with the royal dance.

They invited the court onto the floor. Children ran between the skirts. A guard taught a cook a step. The king tripped over a loose tile and laughed so loudly that everyone joined him.

Celene danced with Lio. He had never learned the steps, so he watched her feet and tried to copy.

“You are late,” she said.

“I am honest.”

“That is late.”

She changed the step to match him. The room cheered when he missed the turn. He felt heat rise to his ears, but he kept dancing. Celene laughed, and the sound was not a borrowed note.

Near midnight, the green light in the east window grew bright. The thirteenth princess stood on the balcony. Her black feathers were turning gray.

The sisters gathered around her.

“What will happen to you?” Miva asked.

“The record will open,” she said. “The pieces will return where they belong.”

Celene reached into her own hair and removed a small silver ribbon. She placed it in the princess’s hand. The ribbon shone with the color of dawn.

Miva gave her a blue thread. Ora gave her a pin shaped like a leaf. Nessa gave her a silver shoe that had never touched the floor.

“We do not want to lose the dances,” Celene said. “We want them to belong to us.”

The thirteenth princess looked at the gifts. “Then the record is no longer mine.”

She opened the ledger. Light rose from its pages. The lost pieces streamed through the ballroom, returning to the sisters as memories. Celene remembered a first clumsy turn. Miva remembered laughing at a wrong note. Ora remembered choosing music for herself.

The black feathers fell from the princess’s crown. Her skirt became ordinary gray cloth. She smiled, then became faint in the moonlight.

“Will you disappear?” Lio asked.

“Only when no one needs me to remember for them.”

The green light went out.

The sisters danced until dawn. They rested on the terrace steps. Some fell asleep with their shoes in their hands. The king carried two sleepy children to the palace. The queen sat beside Celene and listened to the first song they chose together.

Lio found the ledger beneath a table. Its pages were blank except for one line.

*What was taken can be returned. What is given freely can be kept.*

The next morning, the ballroom chandeliers were dark. No magic made them shine. Yet the floor was warm where the sisters had danced, and every footstep left a small, real mark.
