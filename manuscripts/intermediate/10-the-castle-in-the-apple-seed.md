# The Castle in the Apple Seed

![Cover art: The Castle in the Apple Seed](../art/intermediate/10-the-castle-in-the-apple-seed.webp)

On the morning the apple seed began to hum, Nia thought it was a bee trapped in her pocket.

The vibration was soft at first, a tiny tune against her hip, and she had found the seed in the orchard the day before, nestled in a curl of damp leaves. It was no bigger than a plum, smooth and glossy brown, with a pale seam down one side. Nia’s mother, Mara, was a gardener who spoke to roots when she thought no one was listening, and that morning she was speaking to the apple trees.

“Steady,” Mara whispered, “you have enough rain.”

Nia slipped the seed from her pocket and placed it on the kitchen table, where the humming grew louder until the pale seam split with a soft *pop*.

Warm apple-scented air curled out as a door appeared in the middle of the seed, no larger than a doll’s hand, and its tiny brass handle turned.

Nia leaned close and saw a courtyard filled with lanterns, green vines, and people about the size of her thumb.

One of them spotted her and squeaked, “A giant!”

The door swung wider, and a round-faced woman in a green shawl stepped out carrying a basket no bigger than a thimble. She bowed so low that her hat fell off.

“Please don’t crush us,” she said, “we are the Seed Castle, and we are very glad you found us.”

Nia said, “We found the seed, and it belongs to our orchard.”

The woman introduced herself as Pom, the castle’s keeper, and led Nia through a door fit for a rabbit. The halls were filled with polished apple pits, staircases made of cinnamon bark, and windows that looked out onto a forest larger than any forest Nia had seen. In the great hall, workers rolled red and gold fruit across the floor, and each apple shone as if a tiny lantern had been tucked inside it.

“They are forest apples,” Pom explained. “When they fall, roots drink their sweetness, squirrels carry the seeds, flowers open, and even the old stones near the forest stay green.”

Nia looked out at the castle and said, “They are beautiful.”

“They are also all we have,” Pom said, her voice lowering, “but our heartseed is shrinking.”

In the center of the castle stood a round chamber, where a seed the size of a melon rested inside silver roots. It was no longer plump: its brown skin had wrinkles, and each time it breathed, the castle walls gave a small, worried creak.

Nia touched the heartseed and found that it felt like a dry leaf.

“What happened?” she asked.

Pom led her past rows of tiny orchards, where the trees were leafy but their branches were thin. Workers carried buckets of water so small that a single drop filled them, and at the moat a wooden bridge had rotted down to a single plank. The castle’s great clock had stopped at six.

“A long summer came,” Pom said. “Rain stayed away, so the thirsty forest roots received every drop the castle had. The heartseed gave its last stores to grow the fruit, and the fruit fed the forest, but the seed became smaller.”

“Can’t you make more water?” Nia asked.

“Not without roots,” Pom replied. “The castle needs roots, and roots need soil. We have only the little earth inside the seed, while the forest has soil but has tangled, sleeping roots.”

Nia pictured the outside world, with its garden at home, its bucket, and her mother, who knew where every hose connection was. She could bring water, dig holes, and pull the heartseed from its silver roots to carry it to the real orchard. The seed was small enough to fit in her basket, so she could keep it safe in the biggest pot by the kitchen door.

“Could I take it home?” she asked.

Pom looked at her. “You could. It would live longer in your warm house.”

The answer made Nia’s cheeks warm as she imagined the castle glowing on a shelf, the tiny people thanking her, and her mother seeing a castle no bigger than a jar.

But Pom lowered her eyes. “The forest would receive no more apples. The fruit is meant to travel outward. If we keep the heartseed in one place, it will only be safe for that place.”

Nia looked at the tiny workers: a girl with a leaf backpack was trying to push a heavy apple, an old man carried two buckets, and a gardener-shaped beetle waited with a rake near the broken bridge.

“We need help,” Nia said.

Pom blinked. “We have tried to ask. The forest is too far away, and people do not notice a seed.”

“I notice seeds,” Nia replied.

The next morning, she returned to the kitchen table with a notebook and told Mara about the castle, though she left out the part about wanting to keep the heartseed. Mara listened with a seed in one hand and a cup of cold tea in the other.

“What do you need?” Mara asked.

Nia’s first list was very long: carts, buckets, shovels, ladders, rope, baskets, a ladder tall enough for the forest, and a castle-shaped wagon. Mara laughed but did not dismiss the list, and together they made a smaller one.

They gathered five things: a wide tin watering can to hold enough water for the castle, a coil of garden hose to become a bridge, a basket of cherry stones for seeds, bean poles for fence posts, and a paper bag of garden compost to travel safely inside the heartseed chamber.

Mara packed everything into a matchbox, and Nia waited until the kitchen was quiet. When she touched the seed, the tiny door appeared again.

The castle had changed while she was gone: the wind through its halls carried a dry rustle, and the workers moved so slowly that some stopped to lean against the walls.

Nia opened the matchbox and called, “Everyone, listen. We cannot bring a whole forest here, but we can bring the beginning of one.”

She showed them the cherry stones. “These are seeds,” she said, “and we can plant them in every empty patch of soil.”

She showed them the hose. “We can carry water to the roots that are asleep.”

She showed them the bean poles. “We can make safe places for the forest animals to plant and dig.”

The tiny people looked doubtful, and Bracken, a gardener, said, “The seed will still shrink.”

“Yes,” Nia replied, “but roots can hold a castle together.”

Pom studied the supplies and said, “Then we must not wait for a giant to do it all.”

They worked in teams: Nia watered the little orchards while the castle gardeners loosened the earth around the heartseed with tiny rakes. They placed cherry stones in gaps, one seed at a time, and Bracken rigged the hose over the dry moat. It spilled a shining stream into the roots below.

The stream was not magic, and ordinary water could not undo a drought by itself, but the roots received it. They drank, then reached toward the buried seeds, and a pale green shoot pushed through the soil.

At the same time, the workers began packing apples, wrapping each one in leaves and tying it to a thread of vine. Squirrels, mice, hedgehogs, and birds agreed to carry them because the old trees had once given them shade and shelter. Now it was their turn to carry the next season.

One apple was meant for the sleeping roots, another went to the squirrel burrow, and a third was tied to the branch of a crooked oak. Nia tied the last one herself, using both thumbs to knot the vine.

“Why that tree?” she asked a blue jay named Pip.

Pip tilted his head. “Because it is lonely.”

The jay flew toward the forest, and soon the workers saw tiny colored bundles moving through the trees. Red apples flashed between green leaves, squirrels rolled them downhill, mice carried them through roots, and birds dropped them into hidden hollows.

The castle’s fruit harvest was not large, but it was shared.

Still, the heartseed remained small, and its breathing grew weaker each afternoon. Nia’s heart tightened every time she saw its wrinkled skin.

“There must be another way,” she said.

Pom pointed to the seed’s silver roots and explained that the heartseed could be strong again if every root had soil and every tree had a share. The roots did not end at the castle: they reached through the forest, through the old orchard, and into the earth beneath Nia’s house.

Nia understood then that the castle was part of a larger living system, not a little place hidden inside a seed. The seed had connected their orchard to the forest, and the forest had connected its roots to the castle.

“We should plant the cherry stones in our orchard too,” she said.

The tiny people stared at her.

“Your orchard?” Pom asked.

“My mother’s orchard. And the gardens of our neighbors. And the empty lot by the old bridge. If all of us plant them, the roots can find one another.”

“We cannot reach those places,” Bracken said.

Nia took out her notebook. “You can reach the roots that are already there. We will carry the seeds above ground.”

That night, Mara listened to the plan with a serious face, and the next day she told the neighbors. The baker brought cherry pits, the schoolchildren brought empty bottles for measuring water, and the old bridge keeper found clay pots. Even the person who disliked digging brought a wheelbarrow.

They planted the pits in every empty place they could reach, beside the apple trees, along the fence, around the old bridge, and in the little gardens where nothing had grown. Each child dropped one seed into the soil, each adult added compost, and everyone watered the spots and marked them with bright stones so no one would forget.

Back inside the seed castle, the workers followed the same pattern, planting the tiny orchards in every empty corner. They saved some of the harvest for themselves but sent most of it outward.

The forest changed first: new leaves opened along the roots, flowers lifted their heads between old trees, and birds returned to branches that had been bare since Nia’s grandmother was a girl. The streams ran clear, though they were still small.

Then came the answer to the seed.

One morning, Nia opened the matchbox and found the heartseed resting in a ring of new roots, with tiny cherry trees rising around it and their leaves shining. The old silver roots had joined the fresh ones, and although the seed was still small, it was no longer shriveled. Its skin was smooth, and each breath made the castle walls hum a complete note.

Nia laughed and pressed her palm to the seed. The castle’s people cheered.

The gardener’s child had brought water, tools, and a plan, but the castle’s future had been built by the castle’s people and all the hands that chose to help.
