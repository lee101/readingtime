# The Pension Ledger

![Cover art: The Pension Ledger](../art/intermediate/206-the-pension-ledger.webp)

The fund was solvent on the second of March, to the penny, and the committee sat on the sixth, and Sura Vane had four days in which to keep it that way, which is the way the guild's pension book has been kept every year since the office was built, which is to say that the number at the top of the right-hand page is very rarely wrong before somebody comes to look at it.

The pension office is a room off the north side of the Guildhall with one stove, one shuttered window, and a desk that the frost has lifted a quarter of an inch off its feet at the back corner, so that a sheet of paper laid on it runs downhill to the left and has to be weighted with the iron rule. Sura Vane is sixty-one. She is one of the guild's nine minor officers, which is a title that means she is paid four pounds less than she is worth and is not permitted to resign, and she has held the wrights' pension book for nineteen years.

Her reading spectacles sit on a broad beaded cord, and the cord is long enough that she can get them down over her collar without taking her head out of the cold, which matters, because the office is not as cold as the assay room but it is colder than it looks. Her right hand is in her coat. It has been in her coat since she was thirty-four, and the reason is a plate, and the plate is at the Iron Row, and she keeps the hand in the coat in winter and out of it in summer and never explains, and nobody has asked in twenty-seven years.

The book is ruled in three columns of receipts and four of payments, and it is kept with a strictness that the Coldmark notices and admires. Every entry has a date. Every entry has a figure. Nothing is ever written in the book that is not a date and a figure.

Sura did the sum on the morning of the second, because the sum is the whole of her job and she has never in nineteen years found a way to do it any other way.

Levy at the Frostgate, one penny in the pound on every yard assayed, Michaelmas to Candlemas: one thousand and sixty-six pounds, fourteen shillings and twopence. That was seventy-nine pounds, five shillings and tenpence down on last year, which is what a thin crop does to a levy, and which the committee would have heard about by the middle of January and had not, because a levy is a matter for the assay office and the assay office had not been in a mood.

Allocation from the guild chest: four hundred and eighty pounds.

Sundry: forty-one pounds and eight shillings.

Total: one thousand five hundred and eighty-eight pounds, two shillings and twopence.

Committed against it: the twelve lump pensions at forty-one pounds apiece, being four hundred and ninety-two pounds, and the standing pensions and the advances and the funeral grants, which together came to one thousand and ninety-six pounds, two shillings and twopence.

One thousand five hundred and eighty-eight pounds, two shillings and twopence, against one thousand five hundred and eighty-eight pounds, two shillings and twopence.

Solvent. To the penny. It had been solvent to the penny every year for eleven years, which is not a thing a fund does by accident.

The twelve are the oldest wrights on the line, and the lump pension is a winter's money handed over in one afternoon in March, which is a thing nobody wrote down as a rule because it had been done since the office was built, and which exists because a wright who is eighty-eight cannot make four trips to an office in a month of minus nine and would not come four times. The oldest of the twelve is Marda Fell, ninety-one, who has drawn her winter's money on the second of March in person, in a good coat, since nineteen thirty-one, and who is the reason the twelve are paid in one afternoon and the reason Sura Vane had wanted, for eleven years, to be able to say the money was there.

* * *

The sundry column was the whole of it.

The guild's receipts are entered in three columns — levy, allocation, sundry — and the sundry column has not been itemised in the two hundred years of the book's existence, not once, on the ground that nothing has ever come into it that was worth the trouble of a second line. It is four inches wide and it is ruled and it is empty in every year but eleven.

Sura's wage as a minor officer of the guild is forty-one pounds and eight shillings a year. It has been forty-one pounds and eight shillings since before she took the office, and it is paid four times a year, and the whole of it has gone into the pension book every year since the fourteenth of February, under the third column, with a date, in her own hand, because there is no one else to write it.

And out of that column, and out of the fund, and out of nothing at all, Sura Vane has been advancing money to wrights for eleven years.

The advances are legal. The office is expressly permitted to advance on a pension where a wright is in distress, and the advance is entered against the pensioner's name with a date and is repayable out of the first pension, and every one of them was repaid, because she entered them as repaid on a date, and a date in the column is a date in the column.

The column was the only place in the Coldmark where the two facts stood next to each other. Not the sundry column; the other one, on the facing page, in a smaller hand, because a smaller hand is harder to read at a glance and this page is read at a glance. It ran from the fourteenth of February eleven years ago to the second of March this morning, and it had a rule at the top of it, and the rule said: advanced, out of the fund, not recovered.

The eleventh of October, seven years ago: four pounds eleven shillings, to Hesk Sarrow, for a funeral, wife, no insurance on the line. She had entered the funeral grant as paid in the payments column, and had made the arithmetic work by entering the grant twice, once as a payment to the family and once as a repayment to the fund, on different pages and in different columns, eleven years apart.

The sixth of January, five years ago: two pounds three shillings, to Mereth Vask, for coal and a physician, after a charging season. She had nearly written the name wrong, because the Vask and Ostmark lines are neighbours and the Ostmark family had been in her office that same week asking after the same physician, and it was Bram Ostmark who settled that account out loud in front of the store, and who did not thank her, and did not need to.

The second of March, three years ago: eight pounds, to Jorl Sund, aged twenty-nine, laid up after a thaw, and the guild's rule is that a laid-up wright is paid nothing until he is forty, because a wright is paid for what he gave up, and a man of twenty-nine has not given anything up yet. That rule is correct, and it is the rule that made it an advance.

Forty-one pounds and eight shillings a year, eleven years, four hundred and fifty-six pounds and sixteen shillings, entered as sundry, against a book that would have been short by the whole of it in every year of the eleven.

The facing page was never locked and never sealed and lived in the same drawer as the book, and it was the reason she slept. That is not sentiment, it is arithmetic. A clerk who can be asked to produce the reason for a figure can be held to it, and a clerk who cannot be asked has to hold it alone, at night, for eleven years, and Sura understood by about the fourth year that she was not doing the second thing and had only ever been doing the first.

Sura Vane has one hand in her coat, and the other has an injury the guild owes her a pension for, an old plate burn, and she has never once entered a claim, and the reason is that the fund is one pot and she is the one who signs the chits, and a woman who signs the chits does not draw on them, and she worked out at about forty that this was not going to change, and at about fifty she stopped finding it remarkable.

* * *

The guild chest sends a factor up from the valley twice a year to inspect the books of the nine minor officers, and his name is Corvin Ashe, and he is fifty-eight, and he has been a factor for thirty-one years, and he does not read a book to find out whether it is honest. He reads it to find out what it does, and then he works out whether what it does is what it says it does.

He came up on the second, in the afternoon, in the same sledge as a load of assay discs for the Iron Row, and he got through the pension book in an hour and a half, which was faster than usual, and then he sat down opposite her and asked her one question.

“Sundry,” he said.

“Second of March,” said Sura.

“That is not what I asked.”

“The sundry column is entered as of the second of March,” said Sura, “the fourth of January, the fourth of October, the fourteenth of July, and the fourteenth of February, which is the date the wage is paid on.”

“The fourteenth of February,” said Ashe. “Nineteen fifty-six, nineteen fifty-seven, nineteen fifty-eight, nineteen fifty-nine, nineteen sixty, nineteen sixty-one, nineteen sixty-two, nineteen sixty-three, nineteen sixty-four, nineteen sixty-five, nineteen sixty-six. Forty-one pounds, eight shillings, and no pence, in every one of the eleven years.”

“Yes.”

“Where does sundry come from, Sura.”

“It comes from the guild.”

“The guild has not paid sundry since the year I was apprenticed, which was eighteen twenty-nine,” said Ashe. “I have read this book for thirty-one years. There is sundry in the years I have read and nothing at all in any of the others, and never a second line, and the column is four inches wide, and I have never had a reason to look at it before today.”

He put his finger on the line.

“Tell me the date it comes in and what it is, and I will take the paper back to the valley and put it in the chest and nobody will ever ask about it. That is what I am offering you. I am not going to open the sundry. I am asking you to write the thing down, because a book with an unexplained four hundred and fifty-six pounds in it is a book that somebody in two hundred years is going to have to defend.”

Sura sat with her right hand in her coat and her left hand flat on the page, and the stove ticked, and the shutter blew in on its staple.

“It is my wage,” she said.

* * *

She did not sleep much that night and she did not do arithmetic, because she had known for eleven years what the arithmetic was.

The thing she could not do was the thing she wanted to do, which was to write a rule.

The pension fund's difficulty is not a difficulty of money. One thousand five hundred and eighty-eight pounds is a real sum and it can be made to go round, and the levy is levied and collected and honest. The difficulty is that between the levy and the twelve old wrights there is a hole, and the hole has been filled for eleven years by one woman out of her own wages, and the arrangement is called kindness and it is not a rule, and a rule is the only thing that survives the person who made it.

She got up at the fifth hour and did the only thing there was to do, which was to list every decision she had made in eleven years and ask of each one whether it would still make sense if she were not there.

The funeral for Hesk Sarrow's wife: yes, it would make sense.

The coal for Mereth Vask: yes.

The eight pounds to Jorl Sund: yes, and it should have been the rule, because a wright of twenty-nine laid up after a charging season is the exact case the rule about not paying under forty was written around, and the guild wrote the rule for the guild and did not write it for him.

Eleven years of forty-one pounds and eight shillings: no. That would not make sense at all, and if it stopped tomorrow the fund would be short by the whole of the twelve, and the twelve would be paid in January instead of March, and one of them is eighty-eight and one is eighty-four and paying them in January is a different thing from paying them in March, and she had made that argument to herself in November every year and had never once let it stand as what it was.

There is no argument in the world that makes one woman's salary a structural component of a guild institution. It worked for eleven years. It worked because she was careful and honest and kept the facing page and could do the sum in her head. It would have stopped in a fortnight if she had had a bad winter, and permanently if she had died, and the twelve oldest wrights in the Coldmark would have discovered the arrangement by finding the fund forty-one pounds and eight shillings short, in the month she was buried.

Sura Vane put her left hand flat on the page and thought: the fund is solvent and it is solvent because of a woman, and the sum of those two sentences is a number that can be added up.

* * *

Ansel Rime came on the third, in the middle of the afternoon, on the day that the south wind made the shutter bang, which it did most days in March and which he had complained about at the Retirees Bench for nine years.

The word for what happened to him is unsold, and it is the guild's word, and it means that a wright who leaves the line takes a single sum for the whole of his service and holds no claim on the fund afterwards, ever, for anything. The guild does not buy the wright; it buys the years. And the difference between a sum on a Thursday and a quarter in every quarter for the rest of his life is the whole of the difference, and the guild has offered the first for four hundred years and has never once offered the second.

He is seventy-four, a white beard in a good coat, the tops of all ten fingers gone the blue shine of an old cold-burn, so that he holds the paper Sura gave him the way a child holds a cup, with two fingers and a thumb and his wrist turned in. He was the last man to cut a void by hand. He was paid out of the line in nineteen forty-seven, on a Thursday, and has never held a guild post since, and has no pension and no claim and no business in the pension office, and came anyway, because Sura had sent him a note.

He put the paper on the desk and kept his hand on it, and he did not sit.

“Forty-one pounds and eight shillings, a year of service,” he said. “That is the schedule. Eight pounds four shillings, and I had thirty-one years, and it is a figure you can do in your head.”

“You can,” said Sura.

“It is two hundred and fifty-eight pounds and four shillings,” said Ansel Rime. “I was paid a hundred and eighteen pounds, six shillings. I counted it, because it was in coin, on a table, on a Thursday, and I have never had a reason to remember the number and I remember it exactly.”

Sura did not say anything for a while. The dates she had. The eleventh of June, nineteen forty-seven, the date of the entry, was in the book in her own hand and had been in her own hand since before Sura took the office, and the figure beside it was a hundred and eighteen pounds and six shillings.

“The rule is the average levy over the service,” she said. “Fourth of June, eighteen ninety-eight. Allocation of pension on retirement, clause three, paragraph two. The average of the levy in the years of service, and not the levy of the last year.”

“Then they did it wrong.”

“They did it in nineteen forty-seven what every clerk has done since, which is take the last year, because it is the nearest to hand and because in nineteen forty-seven the levy was flat enough that it did not occur to anybody to look.” She put her spectacles up on the cord. “It is a hundred and thirty-nine pounds, eighteen shillings, short.”

“I am seventy-four,” said Ansel Rime. “What am I going to do with a hundred and thirty-nine pounds and eighteen shillings.”

That was a fair question and it had a very short answer, which was that the fund had forty-one pounds and eight shillings of slack in it, and that without the slack three of the twelve could not be paid at all before the end of March, and the committee met on the sixth, and Sura had been in the office since the sixth hour and could do that sum standing up.

And the rule that stood in the way was not the schedule. The schedule was dated and printed and in the guild's standing orders, and it said average, and it said average in nineteen forty-seven as clearly as it said it now.

The rule that stood in the way had no date, which was how Sura knew it was the dangerous kind. An unsold claim does not reopen. That was what every pension clerk in the Coldmark would say, and every one would say it unasked, and not one could tell you which year it began, and the reason none of them could is that it never began. It had never been permitted to begin. It was the shape the question made when the fund did not have the money, and it had been hardening into a rule for forty years, and every time it was said it got a little more like a rule and a little less like an excuse, and the two are indistinguishable on a page, because neither of them has a date.

* * *

The sixth of March was a Tuesday. The committee is nine men and a quorum is five, and Sura was asked to attend with the pension book, which she had done every year for nineteen years, and this year she was asked to attend with the book and a statement, and the statement was three lines long and took her four evenings and it was the first thing she had ever put in writing for that room.

The levy is short by seventy-nine pounds, five shillings and tenpence.

The fund is solvent to the penny, and has been for eleven years, because the sundry column has carried forty-one pounds and eight shillings a year from the clerk's own wage, and that is disclosed now, in the room, by the clerk, on the sixth of March, with a date on it.

The fund will be short by the whole of it on the seventh of March next year unless a rule is made.

Nobody spoke for some time. Ashe, who was there as a visitor and not a member and had been given a chair at the back, did not speak, and the reason he did not speak was that he had already done the only thing he had come to do.

“Forty-one pounds and eight shillings,” said the steward at last, “is your wage.”

“It is,” said Sura.

“For eleven years.”

“For eleven years.”

“Where is the fund without it.”

“Short by forty-one pounds and eight shillings,” said Sura, “on the numbers in front of you, and short by a great deal more in any year the levy is short, which this year it is, and which it has been twice in the last six, and which it will be again.”

“Then the fund is not solvent. It has appeared to be solvent for eleven years. That is a different thing from being solvent and it is a worse thing, because it is a false number that somebody has been keeping.”

“Yes,” said Sura.

“And you have kept it.”

“Yes,” said Sura. “The date on the first entry is the fourteenth of February, seven years ago. The date on the last is the fourteenth of February this year. They are the dates I paid myself, and the entries are the only ones in the sundry column in two hundred years, and Mr Ashe has been asking me about them since Tuesday, and he asked correctly.”

The steward read the statement again, which nobody does, and then he asked the question the whole morning had been walking towards.

“What is the fund for, Mrs Vane.”

Sura had known it was coming since the sixth hour on the fourth, and she had a sentence ready, and the sentence was about widows and retired labour and the cost of a season's sleep, and she found, standing in front of nine men with the book in her hand, that she did not want to say it, because it was true and it was not the answer.

“The fund is for the wrights who cannot work,” she said. “Not for the wrights who have been paid out. Not for the lines. Not for the trade. For the person who sowed a winter into the ground and then could not sow the next one, and who is poorer for it by a decade of sleep, which is the price and is not recoverable.”

“Then make it so.”

“That is what I am asking for,” said Sura. “Not a sum. A rule. A pension at eight pounds four shillings for every year of service, payable from the levy, from Candlemas onward, to every wright who is off the line, and the levy to be the whole of the fund, with no sundry and no discretion and no advance except a physician, which is to be a separate line and is to be voted every year like everything else. It costs more in the first three years than it costs now, because the levy is short and the twelve are expensive and the annuity cannot be paid out of nothing. It costs less in every year after the third, and it does not stop when I stop.”

Halvard Skell, of the Fell account, said the thing that had to be said, which was that the guild had not carried an annuity for sixty years, and that the members would find that out at the first hard year, and that they would blame the nine men who had said yes.

“Two hundred and ninety-one wrights are on the line,” said Sura. “I could give you every one of their years of service in an afternoon, and the number would be about forty-one thousand pounds, and the levy this year was one thousand and sixty-six, so the rule costs three times what the fund holds. I am not asking you to find forty-one thousand pounds this year. I am asking you to start owing them, on the record, from Candlemas, at a rate the levy meets in a good year, and to be short in a bad year the way a guild is short of everything in a bad year, in public, where the book shows it.”

They voted at four in the afternoon. Four for, five against, and the five were not persuaded and the four were not certain, and the steward wrote the minority view into the minutes, which is what stewards do, and which was the only time in nineteen years Sura had seen a thing she had written come out of that room in somebody else's hand.

* * *

They sat until the lamp wanted trimming, and what they gave her was not what she asked for and it was not what she wanted either, and she took it as it came, which is the thing about a committee that there is no other way to take it.

The annuity was carried, from Candlemas, at eight pounds four shillings the year of service, payable in four quarterly instalments, and the advance for a physician was carried as a separate voted line, and the sundry column was closed. That part she had and she had expected.

Two hundred and six unsold claims is not a rhetorical figure. Sura had done it on the fifth, in her own head, with the book shut, because the steward had asked what reopening would cost and somebody had to answer him. At the schedule rate the two hundred and six came to nine thousand four hundred pounds over thirty years, most of it payable after the men owed it were dead, and there was no way to say that sentence to the members of the Coldmark except as the news that the guild had been getting it wrong since the beginning and had never said.

The buy-outs were not reopened. It was the second part of her case and it was refused in one sentence, and the sentence was that the guild had two hundred and six unsold claims on the line and if the schedule was opened on the nineteenth of June, nineteen forty-seven, it opened on every other date in the book, and the chest would not carry it, and the committee was not prepared to go to the members and say that the last sixty years had been computed wrong.

Sura had expected that too, and she asked the only question left to ask, which was what a claim is worth if it is not reopened.

“It is worth what you can pay,” said the steward, “and what I can pay is a discretionary sum, and I will need it put in front of me as a sum and not as a grievance.”

“Forty-one pounds,” said Sura.

“Forty-one pounds to Ansel Rime, and enter it as a gratuity, and the date is the sixth of March, and it is a gift and not a settlement, and if you put the word settlement on it I will strike it out.”

“Gratuity,” said Sura Vane. “Sixth of March.”

She wrote it in the book with the date and the figure, because that is the whole of what the book takes, and it is all it has ever taken from anybody, including herself, and it was the only sentence in the room that nobody argued with.

* * *

The statement of three lines was copied into the minutes, the sundry column was ruled across, and the book was closed on the sixth of March with the fund solvent to the penny, one thousand five hundred and eighty-eight pounds, two shillings and twopence. There was a rule in the guild that would be in force from Candlemas, and Sura sat alone in the pension office with the stove going and the shutter banging and did the thing she had been putting off since Tuesday.

She took out a sheet, and she ruled it in three columns of receipts and four of payments, the way the fund is ruled, and she wrote the head of it in her own hand, and the head said PENSION CLAIM, ORDINARY, INJURY TO HAND, PLATE, and she put the date against it, which is the nineteenth of March, because the committee was on the sixth and she wanted the interval to be visible.

Twenty-seven years, one plate, at the Iron Row, in the year before she took the office. The guild pays four pounds a year for a permanent loss of the use of a hand to a minor officer, and there is a schedule for it, and the schedule is not generous, and she had never once looked at it, because a woman who signs the chits does not draw on them.

Four pounds a year. Less than the coal for a wright of twenty-nine laid up after a thaw. Less than a third of what she had put in the sundry column in a single year, in every year, for eleven years.

She wrote the date and the figure and signed it with her left hand, because the right hand is in her coat and has been for twenty-seven years, and a hand that cannot close properly cannot make a legible signature, and a signature on a pension claim is worth nothing at all unless the hand that made it could have closed.

Then she put the spectacles up on the cord, and squared the book, and shut the stove down, and went out into the flat blue light with four days of a ledger in her head and one line of her own in a book that would be open in a hundred years, and the date on it was the nineteenth of March, and the date is the only part of the whole of it that anybody is certain of.
