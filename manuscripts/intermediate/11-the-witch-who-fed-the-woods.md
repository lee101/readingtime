# The Witch Who Fed the Woods
![Cover art: The Witch Who Fed the Woods](../art/intermediate/11-the-witch-who-fed-the-woods.webp)

The crumbs in Hansel’s pocket tasted like burnt honey.

He and Gretel followed the crumbs through the green dark. They crunched under their boots, flew into the brambles, and somehow grew larger. One crumb became the size of a plate. Another landed on Gretel’s red shoe.

“Do not eat them,” Gretel said.

Hansel licked the honey from his finger. “The bird said they would show us home.”

Ahead, gingerbread walls sparkled between the trees. The roof was striped with white icing. Candy windows shone like little suns. A chocolate chimney sent up a lazy curl of smoke.

Gretel stopped. “That house is breathing.”

Hansel heard it: a soft whoosh, whoosh, like bellows filling a kettle.

The door opened before they knocked.

A woman stood inside. She wore a green apron and no hat. Her hair was black as a river stone. A wooden spoon rested behind her ear. She looked less like a witch than a neighbor who had come home late.

“Welcome,” she said. “Have you eaten?”

Hansel’s stomach growled. Gretel’s stomach answered with a squeak.

The woman brought out bowls of mushroom soup. The smell was warm and peppery. She gave them wooden spoons and a heel of brown bread. For one minute, they ate without speaking.

Then Gretel looked at the walls. The wallpaper showed shelves filled with jars. Each jar held a color: rain-blue, beetle-green, the pale gold of morning.

“Is this your house?” she asked.

“It is everyone’s kitchen.”

Outside, a branch tapped the wall.

The woman looked toward it. “The forest is hungry.”

Gretel glanced at the gingerbread roof. “We heard a witch lived here.”

“I have a pointy hat,” the woman said. “I keep it for mushrooms. And rain.”

She pointed to a little wooden sign above the stove. It read: *The woods eat what is taken. Give what is grown.*

Gretel frowned. “That sounds like a riddle.”

“It sounds like a rule.”

A floorboard creaked. A thin green root pushed through the crack.

* * *

The woman’s name was Maribel, and she was the keeper of the forest kitchen.

She showed the children a long table. A brown bear sat at one end, eating a basket of mushrooms. A family of rabbits shared a bowl of chopped roots. A small deer drank from a cup shaped like an acorn.

Beyond the table, the forest was thin.

Trees stood far apart. Their branches were bare in the middle of summer. Every patch of moss had been scraped away. Near the edge of the clearing, a heap of blue berries spilled across the ground. None had seeds left inside.

“Who took them?” Hansel asked.

“Many hands,” Maribel said. “Each hand told itself it was only taking a little.”

She showed them blue nets in the brambles and a long row of empty baskets. She showed them a little town beyond the trees, where houses had bright windows and stores full of food.

“The people say the forest gives freely,” Maribel continued. “So they take freely. But the woods are made of gifts, not stores.”

Gretel picked up a berry. It was dry inside. “What happens when there is nothing left?”

“The magic weakens. Then the forest cannot hold its shape. Then it cannot feed what depends on it.”

Hansel thought of the crumbs that had grown in his pocket. He felt the trail he had followed begin to feel like a greedy thing.

Maribel opened a cupboard. Inside were labeled jars: *Moss for shelter. Seeds for spring. Dew for clean water. Berries for jam.* Each jar held only a pinch.

“We need to gather,” Maribel said. “Not everything. Enough.”

“Can we help?” Gretel asked.

Maribel looked at their empty bowls. “First, you learn the difference between taking and taking too much.”

She gave Hansel a small basket. “Pick only fallen berries. Leave the green ones for the birds.”

She gave Gretel a wooden spoon. “Stir the soil where you pull weeds. The ground needs to know your hands are helping, not taking.”

The children worked until sunset. Hansel’s fingers stained purple. Gretel’s knees grew brown. They carried baskets to the kitchen door and placed them on the table.

The bear ate a handful of berries and handed Hansel a tin cup of mint tea.

“You are small,” the bear said. “Small does not mean empty.”

Outside, a branch cracked.

Something had entered the clearing.

It was tall and thin, with a coat made of bark and a crown of yellow leaves. Its fingers were branches. It stood on the edge of the kitchen, staring at the full baskets.

Maribel stepped between it and the children.

“What do you need?” she asked.

The forest creature pointed toward the town. “They took the blue nests. They took the singing stones. They took the hollow where the old deer sleeps.”

Hansel’s stomach tightened. “We can show them.”

“Can you?” Maribel asked.

Gretel took a step forward. “We know the way.”

“Then go. But do not make promises you cannot keep.”

* * *

The town looked ordinary until the children pointed out what had been hidden.

The baker had a shelf of stolen fungi. The schoolyard fence was made from roots pulled from the sleeping forest. In the town square, a fountain ran with clear water while a thin stream trickled between the trees.

Children gathered around them.

“They only wanted a little,” said the baker.

“The forest had more than we needed,” said a boy with a blue jar.

“We did not know,” said another.

Hansel opened his mouth to make a smooth speech. Then he stopped.

“We knew,” he said. “We just did not want to know.”

The children stared at him.

Gretel pointed to the blue jar. “That is a nest.”

The boy dropped it. It broke, and a tiny bird flew out, chirping in confusion.

The crowd fell quiet.

Hansel told them about the kitchen. He told them about the empty jars. He described the bare branches and the thirsty roots. He did not call the people wicked. He said, “You can carry things home only if the forest can carry on.”

Some people argued. Some blamed the forest for being old and slow. Others looked at their baskets and began to sort what they had taken.

The baker carried mushrooms back. The school children planted seeds. The boy with the jar repaired the nest. By evening, a line of people walked into the forest with bowls, baskets, and empty hands.

The forest creature watched them from behind a tree.

“It is not a punishment,” Hansel said suddenly.

Maribel had followed them. “No,” she said. “The punishment is that a hungry forest cannot feed a hungry village forever. This is repair.”

They built a new kitchen table. It was not made of gingerbread. It was made of rough wood and warm stones. The old house stayed too, its roof sparkling, but its door stayed open.

Maribel cooked a meal for everyone. She used a little of everything: berries, roots, mushrooms, grains, and water. The bowls were not full to the brim. Nobody minded. Each person tasted the food and learned where it had come from.

When the last spoon scraped the pot, the forest creature knelt beside the table.

“I had forgotten the kitchen,” it said.

Maribel placed a cup in its bark hands. “You can remember now.”

A sound rose from the woods. It was not a roar. It was the soft, busy hum of roots drinking rain. Leaves uncurled overhead. Blue lights blinked between the trunks.

Hansel looked at Gretel. “Do you think we helped?”

“We helped the first part,” she said. “The rest takes longer.”

* * *

The next spring, Hansel and Gretel returned to the kitchen.

A new sign hung beside the door: *Take one. Leave one. Bring a story.*

Inside, the jars were full again. Maribel’s hair had a streak of white at one temple. The brown bear was teaching two young bears to sort mushrooms. The little deer slept in a cushioned bed made of moss.

The forest creature stood outside with a basket of seeds.

“Will you stay?” Gretel asked.

Maribel handed them each a small spoon. “There is always stirring to do.”

They stayed for a season. They stirred soil. They counted berries. They listened to the stories of people who had taken more than they meant to. Some stories ended with apologies. Some ended with hard work. Some people could not give back everything, but they gave what they could.

One evening, a little girl from the town asked Maribel if she was a witch.

“Witch is a word people use when they do not want to ask who fed them,” Maribel said.

The girl frowned. “But your house is made of candy.”

“It is made of old bread, flour, honey, and a great deal of stubbornness. The forest makes the bread sweet. The oven makes it strong.”

She gave the girl a small bowl of soup.

At dawn, Hansel and Gretel left the kitchen. They carried no magical crumbs. They carried three jars of seeds, a loaf wrapped in leaves, and a promise to return.

The path was easier to follow because the forest had grown close around it. Blue berries rolled under the brambles, but Hansel did not pick them. The birds knew where they belonged.

Gretel looked back once. Smoke rose from the kitchen chimney, but it did not hide the stars. Maribel stood in the doorway, feeding the first warm bowls to the birds.

The children walked home. Behind them, the woods grew wider. Between the roots, green threads pushed upward. In the spaces where the people had carried too much away, new signs appeared in the bark.

*Take one. Leave one. Bring a story.*

The forest did not thank them. It did not need to. The shelves were filling, the roots were drinking, and the kitchen door remained open.
