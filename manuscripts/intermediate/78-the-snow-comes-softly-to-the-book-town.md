# The Snow Comes Softly to the Book Town

![Cover art: The Snow Comes Softly to the Book Town](../art/intermediate/78-the-snow-comes-softly-to-the-book-town.webp)

The scraping house smelled of lime, wet wool and nothing else, and Aurelie could have found her way home from the vats blindfold, in February, in the dark.

She had been born in the room over the wetherpit, and Sablon was three streets long, and every street ended in something that made vellum.

Late was the snow. That was the whole of the trouble, said everybody who wanted the whole of something to be the trouble.

Flocks were due on the high pasture before the lambing, and the high pasture was white and empty, and the wind came in under the shed doors as if it had somewhere to be. Twenty-two weeks of wages hung on weather that had not arrived. In the middle of the scraping house floor the big Y-frame stood with nothing on it, which in Sablon was a scandal, and had been since the first week of February.

Her grandmother turned the lamp down and looked at the empty frame.

“It will be here,” she said.

“It was here in January.”

“January was a liar.”

“You said that about December.”

“I said a lot of things in December,” said Ysabel. “Ask the book.”

The book was on the bench. Four hundred years of it, in nine hands, every page ruled and stitched, and Aurelie could find any year’s receipts with her eyes shut. She put her thumb on the open page, which was clean from top to bottom, and said nothing at all. Her grandmother watched her do it.

* * *

Monsieur Ferrand came on the Tuesday, with a clerk and a letter from the guild, and the expression of a man waiting to be disappointed.

The letter said that the assize of 1602, the price at which vellum might lawfully be sold in Sablon, had stood for four hundred years while the price of everything else had gone on without it, and that the guild of the parchmentmakers at the capital had begun to suspect the valley of drowning the market. Somebody had done a sum and decided that the only way a town holds a price for four centuries is by cheating, and had written to the magistrate to find out how.

“He wants a crime,” Aurelie said.

“He wants a crime,” said Ysabel, “and he has been sent four hundred pages in which to look for one, and I am sorry for him, because it is very cold and he is here until Thursday.”

Ferrand asked to see the book in the room over the wetherpit, with the door shut, because he said the light in there was better.

Sablon could show him the receipts for one hundred and nine years in a single morning. The killing days with the tally struck against them. The weights on the beam and the man who kept the beam. The tallow, the lime, the wages. The day of the week the cart went down to the abbey, and who signed for it, and what he said when he signed it.

“What am I looking for?” Ferrand said. “I have a warrant for false weights and a warrant for a short measure, and I will take either, and I will go back in a good temper.”

“A lamb that was taken,” Aurelie said.

He looked at her. “Yes.”

“There is not one in the book,” she said. “Not in four hundred years. I have not read all of it, but my grandmother has read all of it twice.”

He read. This took most of the day.

The town had a system, and the system was on the page. Every animal that came into Sablon was entered twice, once in the giver’s book and once in Sablon’s, with the date, the weight, and the reason. A ram turned four and past working. A ewe that had lost her lamb. A gimmer nobody wanted, given away in April because a gimmer in April is a mouth. Nothing was taken. The rule had four words to it and had not been altered since 1602, and the town wrote them at the head of a page every January, in nine different hands, like a promise made out loud in an empty room.

By the time the lamp came on, Ferrand had a blot on his cuff and no crime at all, and he sat looking at the open book the way you look at a wall you have just walked into.

“I am obliged to tell you,” he said, “that I was sent here to find a fraud.”

“Yes, sir.”

“And there is not one.”

“No, sir.”

“That is an extremely annoying outcome for me,” he said, “and a man at the capital who has never seen a sheep will read the word innocent in my hand and take it to mean careless.”

“You will write what is in the book,” said Ysabel.

“I will write what is in the book,” said Ferrand. “The book is very good, and I have a clerk who will tell me until dinner that I ought to have found something in it.”

* * *

The snow came on the ninth of March, softly, without one hard finger in it, and settled on four hundred years of scrupulousness and closed the passes behind it.

The flocks came down eleven days later, when the price of staying up there any longer was worse than the price of coming down. Forty ewes to the shed and a hundred to the field, and the scraping house hired two more hands out of wages not paid since November.

The hides came up off the low pasture in April, in a cart that could only take four at a time, and the scraping house stopped being empty and started being frantic.

The abbey’s order for the spring quires was dated in May. The assize said what a hide fetched. The wages said what a week of work fetched. Between those two numbers there was a gap that had been the same shape for four hundred years and was now, in April, a gap a man could starve in.

Sablon had been kind in the book since 1602 and was now, for the first time in its history, going to be short of money in the world. Kindness in a ledger and money in a pocket had never been the same subject.

* * *

Aurelie walked up to the Ledoux holding on the Saturday, because Aurelie had decided that somebody ought to be sent, and it turned out that somebody was her.

Tam Ledoux was sixteen and had been doing a man’s work since he was nine. His father had taken the north fell off the Sablon common in 1899, on a lease, and had paid the rent every year, and the rent was in the book in Aurelie’s grandmother’s hand. His yard was the only one in the valley where a man could stand still and see all four sides of what he owned. Two hundred ewes, every one of them up, every one heavy, and a concrete byre built in 1881 that the Ledouxes still owned outright.

“The town gave you the fell,” Aurelie said. “The book says so. It was a lease and not a gift, and you have paid the rent, and I have read the page.”

Tam looked at her the way you look at weather. “We have never had a book.”

“The town keeps a book of everything.”

“The town keeps a book of everything it is given,” said Tam. “There is not one of us in it. There has not been one of us in it in four hundred years, in any of them. We did not give, because nobody ever asked us. We were always the ones on the outside of the page.”

That evening Ferrand was in the room over the wetherpit going through the last hundred years with the door shut, and Aurelie went in to say the cart had come back early, and found him holding the book the way a man holds a thing he has just noticed.

“Tam Ledoux,” he said. “You know the name.”

“He owns the north fell.”

“Yes,” said Ferrand. “Your family has never had a ledger. Your family has never given anything to anybody, including this town, and there is a seizure notice in this book on the eleventh of March, seventeen hundred and eleven, against a house that had nothing in it, because there was nothing in it to take, and he is the only household in this valley that is not standing in a shed waiting for the weather.”

He squared the book with both hands and did not say another word about it then or afterwards.

* * *

The vote was on the Thursday, in the shed, because the shed was the only room in Sablon that would hold forty people, and Aurelie’s grandmother was seventy-one years old and had not spoken in public since her sister’s burial.

It did not go well. The farms had a bad name with the scraping house and the scraping house had a bad name with the farms, and there was a long argument about the abbey and a longer one about the price of flour.

“You may not pay above the assize,” Ferrand said. He had said it four times. “It is the law. If I allow it, it is allowed by me, and then I am the man who allowed it, and there will be a man somewhere next year who copies me.”

“Who is the assize for?” said Ysabel.

“It is for the buyer.”

“Then it is the wrong buyer,” said Ysabel, and put both hands flat on the bench. “The book was never a promise to the animals. Read the first page.”

“There is a first page.”

“There is a first page,” said Ysabel, “and I have read it eleven times, and it does not say that we are to be kind. It says: this is what we did, and this is why, and let the next one see it. That is the whole of it. Four hundred years of a town saying out loud, to whoever picked the book up next, here is what we took and here is what we gave and you may judge us by it. It was never a promise to the sheep. The sheep cannot read. They have been dead since 1602. It was a promise to whoever read it next, and for four hundred years that was us, and we have read it very well, and reading it has not fed anybody since 1881.”

Nobody said anything for a while. Ferrand took off his hat and held it.

“Do it,” said Ysabel. “Write it as it is. Three times the assize, in coin and in lamb, and let the next one judge us by that as well.”

The vote was taken at twenty past six by the light of two lamps. Sixty-one hands in favour, two against, and one hand that belonged to a man who was not from Sablon and had wandered in from the road looking for somewhere warm.

Aurelie wrote the entry on the Sunday at the bench over the wetherpit, in the ruled space, with the ink they had been using since 1602 because it was the only ink in the valley anybody could afford.

Bought of Tam Ledoux, twelve hides, this the second of May, at three times the assize, and the next one to read this will know why.

Her grandmother came and read it twice and did not change a word of it.

Then they hung the book on its nail, and Aurelie went down into the yard to help peg the first hide onto the frame, and the snow came softly down on three streets and on one valley, and the Y-frame had not stood empty since the first week of February.
