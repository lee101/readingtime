# The King Who Was Also a Lock

![Cover art: The King Who Was Also a Lock](../art/intermediate/72-the-king-who-was-also-a-lock.webp)

The Rolls House of Ammory had forty-one presses and one window, and the good chair was the one under the window.

Isolde had wanted that chair for six years. She was nineteen and the senior clerk, and whoever sat in it read the Regent’s letters, and the letters decided everything else.

Sir Alard Fenn sat in it, and had for eleven years.

“You will have it,” he said, without looking up, on a Thursday. “Not this year. You are nineteen. In my day they gave it at twenty-six, and I had a war.”

“You had a shortage of clerks,” Isolde said. “I have read every roll in the north range twice.”

“Then you have read it twice in the wrong range.”

The accession rolls stood in the west presses, one for each year, and every one ended the same way in the same three hands: the king was not found; the Regency continued. Two hundred years of that, and Ammory had never once been in the market to buy a king.

Under the Hall, in the undercroft, there was one. A door of black iron, nine feet by four, set into the floor with no handle on the inside, and a keyhole shaped like a river. The rail round the stair said, by order of the Regency, no entry. Fenn had said once that the King was a large door and very old and had nothing behind it.

* * *

She found it in the sixth year of the Regency, copying fair for the assize, because a coroner’s name was spelled wrong in the original.

The rule was in the margin, in the Registrar’s hand, unruled for two hundred years, and that hand was small and dry and without any drama in it.

“And to be opened once a generation, by the king’s own subject, in the presence of the realm, else the country is held but not held.”

A generation was a hundred years. She took out the roll of the opening of the Regency and read the entry under the old king. “Opened, in the presence of the realm, by a subject of the realm, and the country lay easy after it.” Two hundred and four years ago.

She did the arithmetic three times in a cold press, because arithmetic was the part of her work she trusted.

* * *

That evening she brought the margin to Fenn in the good chair.

“I am going to say a plain thing and you are going to tell me it is a form of words,” she said. “It is. It has sat in a margin for two hundred years and nobody has ever enforced it, which is why nobody has argued about it. I am not asking you to argue about it. I am telling you that in nine months it becomes a second overdue, and then somebody will say the rule has been dead for two centuries, and the file closes.”

Fenn put down his pen.

“You have that in your hands and you brought it to me,” he said. “Most girls your age would have burned it.”

“It is his hand, is it not,” she said. “The Registrar who ruled on the fair copies in your first year, and kept on ruling on them for forty years after he was dead, and you have never once found a fault in a rule he wrote.”

Then Fenn looked at the window for a while and said, not unkindly, that a girl with a ladder and a cold head would get herself noticed.

* * *

The under-gallery held the attempts, and she went through them on her own time over four weeks, with a lamp.

There had been twelve in four hundred years, and every one had stopped, and every one had stopped for the same reason nobody ever wrote down.

In 1410 a Regent got as far as the fifth verse and would not read it aloud. In 1466 the wording was good to the sixth, which named the Regent’s house, and his own son stopped the reading. In 1631 a Regent who had drunk for courage read it entire, and the clerk at the door put the paper down.

In 1770 the last verse was not known to the writer. And under 1804, in a hand that had given up: “the door does not take a half sentence.”

A door does not take a half sentence. That was the whole of the difficulty and the whole of the two hundred years, and nobody ever wrote down anything else.

The last verse was the only verse nobody ever finished, and a reasonable Regency will agree to anything except that, and reasonableness is cheap.

Nobody hid the last verse. Nobody wrote it down twice, because a Regent who wants it opened and one who does not both assume he is the man it is about.

She found it on the back of the first roll, where the sheet doubles and a thing can hide, written backwards in the first Regent’s own hand, because he wanted it found only by somebody prepared to work for it. Her hand refused seven times and the eighth it came forward.

“The Regent is unseated, but not destroyed; the lock remains; the country is lighter.”

* * *

She went down to see what she had written a wording for.

The stair was nine steps and colder than the stairwell. The King took up the whole floor of the undercroft, and it was warm, which was the strangest of it: nine feet of iron shut in a cellar since before her mother was born, at the temperature of a hand, humming low.

“They will tell you that opening me is violence,” said the King, “and it is true that nobody has opened me in two hundred years, because I have been holding, in the dark, alone, and calling that stability is the Regency’s word and not mine.”

Isolde did not ask what was behind the door. It was not in the wording, and the wording was what she had, and she had the assize on her hands.

“Tell me the conditions,” she said.

“The words said in daylight,” said the King. “In the open, with a crowd, and the last of them said to the face of the man they concern, and one of them said by whoever holds the key. Not in a cellar with a lamp. Anything less is only a locked door and calling it a reign.”

* * *

On the Thursday she went down to the forge in the lower town and put a hairpin on the anvil.

She had worn it since she was twelve. Plain bent iron, black at the head where the flame had been, bought in a tray of forty from a man who sold pins.

Gerta Bell turned it over, put it in the vice, took the polish off with a file, and then stopped filing.

“There is a bitting on this,” she said. “Two of them, and a ward, and a shoulder. It is a key, and it is a key that somebody has been using to hold up hair for two hundred years.”

“I have been using it for seven.”

“I know you have,” said Gerta, and looked at the small letter struck into the head of the iron. “That is my grandmother’s mark. She made nothing but pins, and there is nothing else in this forge with a bitting on it.”

“A key is appointed by the finding of a lock, and a lock is appointed by the finding of a key.” It was in the first roll in the plainest language in the book, and she had read it as a rule about a hunt.

She put the pin in. It went as far as the wards and stopped, the way a key stops in a lock that has not been turned in a long time, and the humming under her hand stopped too.

The pin was the key. She was the king’s subject. It had been in the hair of a woman in the lower town every year since a Regent whose name she would never read.

* * *

The rolls required two hands. She wrote the appointment out fair, left it on Fenn’s desk with the pen beside it, and went out to the stair.

He was in the good chair when she came back, and the paper was where she had left it.

“You will not countersign it,” she said.

“No,” said Fenn.

“Because you are the Regent’s man.”

“Because I am forty-one,” he said, “and two hands is not a law, it is a habit, and I have kept every habit in this house for twenty-two years. What happens if the door does not take a half sentence and you have no witness?”

“Then it is entered of herself, no witness, her hand alone,” she said. “There is a column for it, unused since 1611, in the back of the third book, and it is there for exactly this.”

Fenn sat with it a long time. Then he said that if she went on to the assize he would read her fair copy out word for word, and she should not mistake that for forgiveness.

The next day the Regent sent for her, and he was extremely kind. He gave her the good chair in writing, a house on the river, an annuity of forty marks, and a month to go down the mountain.

“It is a door,” he said. “Nine feet of iron and two hundred years of a very good reign. There is nothing behind it that needs you. I have looked, and I will not be the man who opens it to find out.”

* * *

She fixed the reading for the assize day, because the assize was the one day of the month the realm was already in the square for a better reason.

The notice went up at every third gate. Nine lines: a thing in the first roll was overdue, a subject of the realm would read it out on the Hall steps at noon, and attendance was not commanded but was customary.

They came anyway. The weavers’ guild at the front, the fishwives, the mill men, two hundred by noon, and shutters half closed along the west row, and a carter saying to nobody that his grandmother was born under a Regency and never knew another kind.

Tobit Roke, who was sixteen and copied fair, stood behind her with the notice and could not look at the steps.

“You are going to have to look at the paper,” Isolde said, and then you are going to have to look at the steps. “Stand on my left. The left is where Fenn is.”

Fenn was there, with the fair copy under his arm and a second candle in a tin. He set the candle on the step and said nothing at all to her.

* * *

The verses took a long time, because a wording that is complete cannot be hurried, and every one of them has to be said whole.

Isolde read them out on the steps of the Hall with the whole realm in front of her, and the King listened from under the floor, and nobody in the square could afterwards say whether the air had moved.

The last verse is about the Regent, and she turned round and read it to his face, and she watched him sit down on the step before she had finished saying it, the way a man sits when a thing he has been carrying is taken off him. Nobody touched him. He set the chain down.

Then they went down.

Gerta had the pin, because Fenn had sent for her at the fourth hour, and she would not give it to Isolde. She put it in herself, because she was the one who had said that a key is appointed by the finding of a lock.

It turned with a sound like a boot coming out of deep mud, and nothing came out. What came in was a draught: grey air that had been standing in a cellar for two hundred years went up the nine steps, through the Hall, and every hanging in the building moved, and the candles leaned.

It smelled of nothing for a moment, and then of the southern road, of wet grass and warm stone, and of April off a plain nobody in that square had ever stood on. It was spring, it was from the south, and it was the middle of a Tuesday.

Corven Hale got up off the step and went down and across the square and out through the wicket gate on foot at noon, with the realm on both sides of the lane and nobody making a sound.

The King spoke from the dark at the bottom of the stair, and only Isolde heard it.

“In a hundred years there will be another of you,” he said, “and I shall be harder to reach, and you are far too junior.”

Isolde went up and wrote the day up in a fair hand, signed with her own name and not the Regent’s, then took a thorn out of her boot and put her hair up, because the pin belonged to the building.
