# The Boy Who Tracked the Whale Flukes

![Cover art: The Boy Who Tracked the Whale Flukes](../art/intermediate/163-the-boy-who-tracked-the-whale-flukes.webp)

The fluke log at Kaikōura is a school exercise book, forty-two centimetres by twenty-nine, with a rubber band round it, and it has sat on the table in the watch hut since the winter of 1978.

Ewan Rigg is thirteen. He has sandy hair that has been cut twice by his mother and once by a sheep-shearer, and he wears a dark green knitted wool jumper with the cuffs turned back, and he carries a camera with a 200 millimetre lens on a strap his father made out of a dog collar.

The hut is corrugated iron on four piles above the boat sheds. There is a bench, a kettle, a barometer in a wooden case, and a box of slides on the sill that has to be carried inside before it rains.

The log has one line for every sighting, and every line wants five things: date, time, place, what it was, and the number of the photograph.

A sighting with a fluke photograph can be matched against the cards. A sighting without one is a sighting and nothing more.

That rule is Gil Harrow's. Gil has kept the cards since 1978, when he was a schoolteacher, and he is sixty-one now, and he has one brown eye and one blue one and a hand that shakes when he is tired.

In 1978, in the first season, Gil and two students ran the log off the Kaikōura boats and ended up with sixty-one cards in a card index tray.

Nobody expected sixty-one. It is a small stubborn number, and the paper Dr Kearney wrote about it in 1996 was about that number and not about whales.

Sixty-one is not a population. It is one headland's list of the animals that could be told apart from one another there, and the trust wanted the other number, the one out in the water. The only handle anybody had was this tray.

Every animal matched to a card is an animal seen twice, and an animal seen twice has pulled somebody's total up. A list one too long does not only add a whale. It makes the survey say the sea is fuller than it is, and the funding application for the winter watch went out on the eleventh of June quoting sixty-one.

* * *

Matching is not difficult and it is not safe. Two animals are the same animal only when the trailing edge of the fluke carries the same marks in the same places: the notch, the wave, the short straight piece, the wider lobe.

A humpback's fluke is about three and a half metres across, and it is the only part of the animal with a pattern on it.

Gil's rule is that both lobes must be in the frame, the whole trailing edge, seen from behind, sharp. A fluke half out of frame is a fluke that will match something.

You get the picture from behind, when the animal arches and lifts the fluke clear, and it is back in the water in about three seconds.

At two hundred metres a three and a half metre fluke is a thumbnail in a 200 millimetre frame. The boy works at a hundred metres in good light and will not fire at all beyond two hundred, because a fluke that is too small in the frame will match anything, and anything is the shape of every other whale.

Of every hundred flukes photographed from that headland he had decided that sixty-one could be told apart from one another. The other thirty-nine he would not try, and the reason was not mystery. It was that the trailing edge did not carry enough.

Thirty-nine whales over nineteen seasons, seen once, and never certain.

* * *

On the 12th of August 1996 the boy saw a pair come in under the point, the big one fluking at eleven minutes past eight in the morning, and the calf beside her fluking a minute later.

He wrote it in the log straight away, because it was a good morning and because the calf was doing something he had not seen before, lifting a fluke clear and holding it out of the water for a second and a half.

He had no fluke photograph of it. The 200 was on the shelf with the cap on, because he had spent the whole morning on the 50 with the gulls, and by the time he had the 200 off the shelf and screwed on and shouldered, the pair had gone.

He wrote the reason at the end of the line in brackets. No fluke photograph.

He was thirteen, and he wrote the reason, and that is the only reason he still has the sighting at all.

* * *

Gil made a card for the animal in September and matched it in October, against a card from 1978. Number 41, a cow seen in four seasons between 1978 and 1985, with a notch a third of the way along the right lobe.

The print he matched it with is a black and white print, seven by five inches, made in the school darkroom off a slide from roll 78, frame 19.

That is the whole of the mechanism and it is a good one. The animal is not recognised by being seen again. It is recognised by two photographs that match, and both of them have to be photographs of that animal.

* * *

The boy found it in September, sorting the archive for Dr Kearney, because she had asked him to check the 1996 card before it went to press.

He held the 1978 print and the 1996 print up together against the window light, because matching is done by holding them up together, and that is what Gil had done.

A thumbprint sits in the lower right corner of both of them. The same crescent, the same two whorls in it.

Above it, a round speck of dust out of the enlarger, three millimetres across, in the same place.

Along the top edge, a thin dark line where the negative had been scratched in its carrier sleeve, in the same place.

Three defects on two different negatives cannot happen, and a defect is a better fingerprint than a notch, because a notch changes with the years and a defect does not.

He took the 1996 roll out of the tin and put slide eleven under the lens. Slide eleven was open water and the light on it, and it stayed open water and the light on it, and slide twelve was the calf going down, and eleven was nothing at all.

* * *

He told Gil first, in the hut, with the door shut. Gil said the prints are prints and he had made them both, and hold them up yourself if you like.

Hold them up yourself was not a denial. It was worse than a denial, because it meant he had not looked.

So the boy rang Dr Kearney in Christchurch on the Wednesday. She was quiet for a while, and then she asked him to read the two positions out again and to write them down where a fax could reach them.

The paper was in press. It went out in the autumn number with one paragraph cut out of it, and a note under the table of results four lines long, and Dr Kearney's name at the bottom of the note, and nobody else's.

Sixty-one became sixty. Nineteen years became no record at all. And the animal that Ewan saw on the 12th of August has no card, because a card is a photograph, so it stays in the sighting book for ever with a bracket at the end of the line that nobody will ever be able to close.

Gil did not speak to him for a fortnight. The work went on, because the whales did not care about it. They sat in the hut at the same hours and passed the same prints across the same bench, and Gil handed him the cards in the order he had always handed them over, and the order was still the right order, and neither of them said so.

* * *

They reprinted the whole run of 1978 to 1985 in the winter, print against print, on the bench, with the tin of slides and the two boxes of cards.

They found one more pair that was the same print filed twice, in 1981 and in 1989, and took the second card out of the tray and wrote the other one on the back of it.

And they found that card 47 would not match anything. It is a fluke in low light in July 1979 with the right lobe out of frame and the trailing edge running off the side, and it could be any one of thirty animals already in the tray.

Gil marked it unmatched rather than new, which cost him the only argument he had ever won, and the count went to fifty-nine, plus one that might be sixty.

* * *

The trust took him off the matching in November, nineteen seasons after he started, and gave it to the boy, who was fourteen in October and still could not be paid, because the trust pays Gil.

Ewan does the matching now. The cards live in one index tray under the window, and there are two hundred and six of them, and he writes on them in pencil, because a pencil is the only thing on that table that can be taken back.

The count is written at the foot of every season's page. It is written in pencil.

* * *

The pair came back in on the 3rd of August 1997 at twenty minutes past six in the evening, and Ewan had two rolls in the camera, and he got both flukes on the same frame at f/8 and a thousandth of a second, and the notch on the right lobe was still a third of the way along it.

He matched it to card 41 in eleven minutes. Then he sat on the bench with the two prints and looked at the lower right corner of each of them for a long time, and then he opened the log and read the line for the 12th of August 1996 all the way down to the bracket.

Then he made the card and wrote 41 on the back of it, and 61 in the tray.

Sixty is the number that will go in the paper, and it is two photographs out of date, and there is one animal in the tray that might be two animals, and nobody who reads the paper will ever know that.

Ewan knows it, and that is the job. To know a number and to say it, and to write down beside it the thing that is wrong with it.

On the last night of the season he shut the hut and the cards went into the tray and the two rolls went into the tin, and the count in his book stayed at sixty, because sixty was the number he had, and the water came up the point and went over the shingle the way it does, and the pencil would be the thing that moved.
