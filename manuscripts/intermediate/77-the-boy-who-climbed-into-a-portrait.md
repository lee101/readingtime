# The Boy Who Climbed Into a Portrait

![Cover art: The Boy Who Climbed Into a Portrait](../art/intermediate/77-the-boy-who-climbed-into-a-portrait.webp)

Ned counted the house as he went in, because it gave his hands something to do. A hall with a floor of black and white squares. A stair with a rope knotted at the bottom. A Long Gallery with eleven windows and a floor polished by somebody on their knees.

The red ball came off his uncle’s desk without warning, crossed nine feet of waxed oak in one bounce, struck the skirting, and stopped dead against a plinth.

His uncle would be home on Thursday. Mrs Ivey was somewhere below doing the silver, and Ned had spent two summers learning that Mrs Ivey does not look up at noises.

He put his left hand flat on the frame, and then he was up, and his toes were sliding in the wax, and his right hand went past the frame into the picture, and the picture gave.

* * *

It gave the way a wet wall gives. His wrist went in to the elbow. He hauled backwards on the frame, and instead of coming out he went forward, the wrong way, and landed nine inches onto a floor.

He was in the picture.

The light came from the left, low and gold, the colour of a late afternoon in a month with no name. The eleven windows were all there, but the glass had given up on being glass and settled for being the idea of a window, and the sky outside it had not moved since the painter laid it down.

The ball was on the table.

Ned put his hand in his pocket. The ball was in his pocket. It had been there since Tuesday.

A maid came through with a duster and went through Ned on her way past. Her shoulder went through his ribs the way weather goes through a coat. She dusted the table and went out. She had not touched him. She had not avoided him either.

* * *

He tried being a nuisance first. He moved the duster when she set it down, dropped a picture brush, and talked at her from the doorway for a quarter of an hour, and then held her broom while she swept the dust straight through his fingers. So he tried being large. He put both palms on the table and shoved until his arms shook, and he hung off the frame kicking, and the girl painted underneath him took no notice at all.

On the fourth day somebody took his hand.

She was nine, with a smudge of blue on her chin where a sleeve had gone through wet paint, and both her hands held up to her own cheeks.

“Oh,” she said. “You are in it. I did not think anybody could get in it.”

Ned had eleven words ready and used none of them. “Can you see me?”

“I can see you.” She took her hands off her face. “Can you see me?”

“Yes.”

“Good,” said the girl. “Nobody has been able to see me for months.”

* * *

Her name was Alice Hollis, and her father was the man in the frame over the door at the top of the stairs. The picture Ned had climbed into was a room with her in it, at the table, in that same light, and the painter had put a great deal of care into the rug and none at all into the girl.

“When does the year end?” he said.

Alice sat down on the painted chair, which took her weight the way a chair takes weight in a dream. “On the last day of the year. There is a day when the light stops, and then the clock has nothing to say about it.”

“And then what?”

“And then I go in the frame,” she said. “That is the arrangement. Father paid a great deal for it, and I am not going to be difficult about the money.”

“You do not want to go in the frame.”

“I want to be nine in a house with a hall in it,” Alice said. “That is the whole of it. It is better than being nine in a house with a man who is looking at you.”

“Have you never tried to get out?”

“I have decided that it is fine.”

“That is not the same as wanting it.”

“It is the same as wanting it,” said Alice. “I have decided it is fine, and I have been deciding it a long time now, and you are going to have to be extremely good if you want me to stop.”

* * *

He found out about the paint on the sixth day, by accident, and nearly missed it, because he was tired of being talked past.

He stepped through the frame to look at the real room. It was exactly as he had left it, with Mrs Ivey’s cloth on the sideboard and his coat over the newel post, and it smelled of cold wax and old paper. He thought about the fire. He put his hand flat on the painted mantelpiece and pushed, and the chimneypiece four feet away moved half an inch and scraped on the tiles.

He went through and looked. In the picture, the corner of the rug had come up, standing off the boards on end. He turned the blue china dog a quarter turn, and the dog turned. He held his breath, and the clock on the shelf, which had not gone in six days, ticked forward and ticked back, twice, and Ned felt both of them in his chest like a gulp.

“It is me,” he told Alice. “Whatever I do out there, it turns up in here.”

“That is how a portrait works,” she said, from the painted stair. “It simply usually takes a better painter than you.”

Then she looked past him at the table, where the ball sat in a shadow of its own.

“Yes,” she said. “You came in for that ball and it is still in your pocket, and there it is on the table, and it is the only thing in this picture that is not in the story. Father never knew we had a red ball. Nobody would miss it.”

And she went back up the painted stair, and did not say another word about it for as long as he was there.

* * *

He had been counting. The painter’s year was in a drawer in the real hall, and every time the light went out in the picture one leaf came loose, and he kept the leaves in his pocket in the order they fell. There were eleven left.

He went at the portrait like a man cutting a fence, and he was better at it than a boy of twelve has any right to be.

He got a hearth brush and worked at the canvas from the left edge inward, and the picture took the brush and swallowed it. The paint came off in grey dust and underneath the gallery was brighter, and the crack that had run along the ceiling since he arrived was filled in.

He fetched a poker and took the lower corner, where the perspective was worst, and the corner lifted away in a sheet like a scab coming off, and behind it there was not wall but more gallery, and the more was better.

He held a candle to the canvas until the varnish smoked, then thought about Mrs Ivey and blew it out. The scorch mark was the only mark on the picture, and it looked deliberate.

He sat down on the real floor with his back against the plinth and cried until he had nothing left in him, and the picture over his head grew steadier with every sob. Every time he damaged it, it improved. It had been waiting a long time for somebody to try.

* * *

On the ninth day he stopped moving.

He went into the picture and stood in the middle of the gallery with his hands at his sides and made himself breathe the way a post breathes. The candle flame stopped bending. The maid did not come in. The light stayed exactly where it was, and Ned understood, with a cold drop going through him, that this was not the picture being polite. This was the picture agreeing with him. The picture was his memory of this day, and the day was not going anywhere, because he was not letting it.

He stepped through the frame to see whether the real room was still a room, and the rain that had been coming down the windows all morning was hanging in the air.

Not stopped. Hanging. Every drop where it was, four inches off the glass. The garden full of them. The water standing on the shoulder of his coat on the newel post in a single bead that would not fall off.

He could hold the day where it was, or he could change it, and while he held it so did everything in it, and there were people in this house who needed him to be a boy who came in and had his tea.

He said the word out loud, once, to nobody at all. “Still.”

* * *

“You are looking at me the way my father looked at the canvas,” Alice said. “What is wrong with me?”

“Your face,” Ned said.

“My face is fine.”

“Your face is wrong in the picture. Look at it.”

She did not want to. He made her. Her father had gone back to her face nine times, and the plaster under the frame was thicker on the left than on the right, which is what happens when a man keeps returning to the same part of a canvas. Every version of her in it was smiling, chin down, teeth showing, the fixed bright arrangement of a child being good in front of a visitor, and the ninth went over the eighth so thickly that the corners of her mouth went out of the picture altogether.

“He repainted you because you would not sit still,” Ned said. “That is the only wrong thing here. Everything else holds together. You are the loose part.”

Alice was quiet a long while. “I am not going to be still for him.”

“You are not going to be still for him,” Ned agreed. “You are going to be still for one second, for me, and then you are going to do exactly as you like for the rest of your life.”

She thought about it. “One second.”

“One second, and then we are gone.”

Ned went through to the real room. He got the stub of carpenter’s chalk out of his pocket, the one he had carried since the summer he wanted to number the new paling posts, and reached up and laid the flat of it on the red ball where it sat on the painted table, and rubbed it out.

The ball did not flake. It thinned. The red went pale, then went to the colour of old brick, then went to the colour of the table underneath it, and the little shadow it had been casting joined the other shadows on the cloth, and then there was a table.

Alice made a sound. In the picture her mouth came unsticky.

The frame took what was left of her in one long breath, and the gallery went all at once, ceiling and windows and the smell of cold wax, and what was left was a wall in a country house with a table on it and a rug and a fire that had gone out some time ago.

He took his hand out of the frame. His sleeve was grey with dust to the elbow and the chalk was down to a nub.

She was in the hall with him. Nine years old and one bare foot.

“Where is my father?” she said.

“In the frame over the door. He was painted before either of us got here.”

“Oh,” said Alice. “Well, I shall want that explaining, and I want it now. Begin by telling me what is for tea, because I am extremely hungry, and I have been since roughly the middle of the year.”

Mrs Ivey found them at half past four. She did not ask where a strange barefoot child had come from, and she did not say that there was no strange barefoot child. She set a place without being asked and put too much butter in the bread.

The portrait hangs at the top of the stairs to this day. People have measured it, and it is nine inches deep. Stand in front of it on a wet afternoon and you will see a room with a table, a rug, a blue china dog, and a wall, and the corner of the rug standing up.
