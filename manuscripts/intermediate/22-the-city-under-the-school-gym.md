# The City Under the School Gym

![Cover art: The City Under the School Gym](../art/intermediate/22-the-city-under-the-school-gym.webp)

Kit Maren was eleven, small enough to hide behind her grandfather’s canvas field pack and stubborn enough to measure twice. At archaeology club, she wore a faded blue work shirt, red boots, and a striped cap.

The rest of the club crowded around the gym wall, testing a storeroom beam that someone claimed was older than the school. Kit knelt near the northern corner instead. The basketball lines there did not quite meet. One white stripe stopped a finger’s width before the wall.

“Survey first,” Ms. Saye said. She was the school’s visiting archaeology adviser, a wiry woman in a tan utility vest over a green button-down shirt. “We mark what we see before we decide what it means.”

Kit measured the gap, sketched it, and tapped the corner with her knuckles. The floor replied with a clear metallic note.

The others looked back.

“It’s probably a floor beam,” said Ben, adjusting his football-shaped pencil case.

“Beams don’t tick,” Kit said.

Ms. Saye moved close. “Record it. Ben, notify Principal Ruiz. No one touches that tile.”

That was the first excavation rule: report, mark, wait. Kit obeyed while curiosity kicked her shins. They cleared the corner, laid a one-meter grid on the gym floor, and found two old bolts beside a faint brass line. Principal Ruiz, a broad man in a gray school blazer, called the school district’s facilities manager. By evening, a warning cone stood over the spot and orange barriers enclosed half the court.

Kit’s father arrived with a flashlight and tape measure. Roen Maren was a building inspector who wore steel-toed shoes and a yellow vest over his old green sweater.

“It’s a crawlspace,” he said after looking beneath the loose baseboard. “We seal it with concrete before the rain.”

Kit stared at the brass line. “It isn’t empty.”

“That is what worries me.”

Her mother came twenty minutes later. Architect and historian Deva Maren wore a long burgundy coat and carried rolled drawings under one arm. She crouched beside Kit and examined the line without touching it.

“There may be something worth preserving,” Deva said.

“There may be a child above weak supports,” Roen replied. “That wins.”

“Only if the child uses rules.”

Kit felt both their eyes on her. Before she could answer, Principal Ruiz said the school board had scheduled a vote the next morning. The district’s repair proposal would replace the gym floor with poured concrete.

Ms. Saye stepped between Kit and her parents. “An excavation permit requires a grid map, a safety check, and two adult signatures. We may inspect visible surfaces. We may not enter, lift, or dig without all three.”

“I can map it,” Kit said.

“You may help map it,” Ms. Saye corrected. “After school.”

* * *

The next morning, the board met between library shelves. Board chair Helena Ward presented Roen’s report. Photographs showed an unexplained void beneath the gym.

“Concrete is cheapest and safest,” she said. “We vote today.”

Deva displayed century-old plans showing a sealed chamber. “The void may be an archive.”

“An archive that can drop through the floor,” Roen replied.

Ms. Saye placed their grid on the table. “We found movement.”

Her map showed the brass line twelve inches from its afternoon position. The board members leaned closer.

“A proper survey can wait,” Helena said.

“The survey is what we need time to do,” Deva replied.

“Concrete is what we need time to do,” Roen said.

Kit studied the old plans. A 1968 renovation had rotated the gym ninety degrees, but the buried chamber still matched the original building. A brass street ran beneath it. Four marks surrounded the street: an eye, a flame, a wave, and a closed hand.

“Those are stations,” Kit said. “The plans call them the four towers. The school is lying about which way it faces.”

Roen gave a tired smile. “Architecture is a matter of direction.”

“The pump room is under the north tower,” Deva said. “The authority room is under the east tower.”

Ms. Saye studied Kit’s map. “A real clue. Not a permit.”

After school, Deva quietly handed Kit the blue notebook.

“You were right about the clue,” her mother said. “You were also right to report it before entering. Rules keep one person’s excitement from becoming everyone’s danger.”

Kit looked at her careful father across the gym. “You and Dad disagree.”

“We do. The board needs a plan strong enough to answer both of us.”

At 4:10, Ms. Saye brought school engineer Mr. Okafor and Ada Venn, age sixty-four, the city’s timekeeper. Ada wore a moss-green coat with brass buttons and carried narrow steel keys.

Ada studied the map. “The century is cracked.”

The settling roof pipes went silent.

“A century is one hundred years,” Kit said.

“One hundred hours that must agree,” Ada replied. “A crack makes neighboring hours disagree. When the century loses its last full chime, my living stairway folds shut.”

“People live below?” Roen asked.

“My rooms, my kitchen, the records room. Thirty others use the streets for work and messages.” Ada showed them a brass token bearing a hundred marks and one chipped place. “The poured floor would seal my last stair. I can repair the century, but not alone. No keeper ever could.”

* * *

The board allowed one hour before the concrete crew arrived. Principal Ruiz approved a limited inspection: no digging, no unsupported floor, and no student beyond the marked grid. The rules slowed Kit enough to see the city clearly.

Strings divided the floor into squares. When the PE bell rang, the brass marks slipped between squares, then returned to positions around the original towers. A photograph through the inspection hole showed curved streets far below.

“Sound nudges it,” Kit said, comparing watches.

Mr. Okafor lowered a vibration pencil through an inspection hole. Its tip trembled over the water tower’s inlet.

“The pumps are part of the city,” he said.

“The city is a clock. Streets are hands. When all four towers strike together, the stair opens,” Ada explained.

Kit drew the four marks. “The gym moved, but the stations stayed connected to their old places.”

A 1954 fire plan showed the former open-frame gym. The north station matched the pump room, the south the music room, the east the current stairwell, and the west the kitchen store. The vibration pencil confirmed each point.

“The living stair is below west,” Ms. Saye said. “It opens only when all four towers answer together.”

At 5:03, the concrete truck sounded its horn. The brass lines jumped.

Ada returned with a long case containing a bridge piece shaped like a golden tooth. “With four towers aligned, I can lift the cracked ring and fit this. Then the stair stays open.”

Roen placed jacks beneath beams clear of the buried streets. Mr. Okafor controlled the pump. Principal Ruiz locked the gym doors. Ms. Saye watched the grid and clock. Kit held the vibration pencil at west while Roen and Deva carried the case.

“No signal,” Kit called.

The concrete pump rumbled. The buried marks shifted.

“Now,” Ada said. “North.”

Mr. Okafor struck the pump pipe once with a mallet.

“East,” called Deva, touching the stair rail.

“South,” Ms. Saye said, pressing a tuning fork to the music-room wall.

Kit felt the pencil jump against her palm. “West.”

Four notes traveled through stone, steel, and string. The floor beside the western wall lifted a finger’s width, revealing a narrow brass stair descending into golden dark.

Ada started down. “One at a time. Touch the rail, and step only when it is still.”

The stair folded sideways with a deep clockwork sigh, blocking the lower steps. Ada climbed back before it trapped her.

“We lost the alignment,” Kit said.

Ada checked her token. “The pump note reaches the buried north station before the outside rail reaches west. One-tenth second apart.”

Deva pointed to the old plans. “The original stations were outside, but the pipes run inside.”

“The buried street can carry the outside notes,” Kit said. “We time them to arrive together.”

Ms. Saye produced two small tap hammers. “South first, then north one beat later. When those tones meet, east and west together.”

Principal Ruiz looked toward the concrete truck. “The crew is backing away. We have three minutes.”

Kit held the map. Roen and Mr. Okafor leaned close enough to hear.

“South first. One beat. North. At their meeting beat, east and west together,” Kit said.

Ada listened with her eyes closed. “Correct.”

Ms. Saye sounded the south station. One beat later, Mr. Okafor struck north. At that same beat, Deva sounded east while Roen struck west.

The tones met at the buried crossing and ran through all four towers as one note. The brass stair straightened. Ada descended. Roen and Mr. Okafor lowered the case by rope, guided by Deva and Kit. They brought the golden bridge piece to the cracked ring. Ada guided it into place as the joined note held.

The century rang a hundred notes. Kit counted every one on her fingers and felt each beat climb the old building walls. Then she heard one more, small and clear.

The stair stayed open.

* * *

The school did not pour concrete that evening. The board approved a stronger plan: screw supports around the buried streets, a reversible floor panel over the stair, and monthly visits by Ada and the city preservation office. The work cost more than a simple slab. It also kept the gym safe.

Roen signed the new support drawings. Deva became the school’s records steward, responsible for every map and permit. They still argued, but now their arguments lived in the same folder.

Kit’s new emergency responsibilities came with limits. She lost the right to call the project club for a month. Ms. Saye gave her a junior field badge and a copy of the excavation code she promised to memorize.

On the next clear Saturday, Kit stood beside Ada on the open stair. The timekeeper had mended the century, but the city still moved when school bells rang. Kit wore her blue work shirt, red boots, and a new striped cap. She measured the change in the brass street below.

“Does the century need repairing again?” she asked.

“No,” Ada said. “It needs tending, like anything shared.”

The morning bell rang. Far below, four towers answered, and the buried city took one careful step forward without asking the earth above to move.
