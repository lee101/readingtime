# The Beekeeper of the Sleeping Giants

![Cover art: The Beekeeper of the Sleeping Giants](../art/intermediate/73-the-beekeeper-of-the-sleeping-giants.webp)

Above Karst the giants sleep face-down in the long grass, and to see the guild’s whole work you climb the mill and look up at them.

They are eleven along the shoulder of the hill, lying face-down in the grass, so that from the mill roof you see a line of knuckles and one ear the size of a cart wheel. The oldest has a thrush nest on his shoulder, and the village calls it the long sleep.

Ottoline Vell was fourteen, the youngest keeper the guild had, and a keeper is named at sixteen, which meant two more years of carrying things up ladders for other people.

The guild yard sat at the bottom of the hill, hives in two rows, the extractor, the wax pot, and a smell of hot comb that got into a girl’s clothes for a week. Master Bram Sallow had kept bees for fifty years and never gone up the hill, and said he was not starting at his age.

It was the buckets nobody talked about. Twelve of them, covered with sacking, on a barrow by the back gate every evening, carried up and brought down empty, and four hundred years of the guild had called that the round.

* * *

Ottoline went up on the round with Hulda Pream, who was forty-one and said almost nothing between the ladder and the top.

The pegs went in under the nails of a hand the size of a ploughed field, the ladder was thirty-one rungs of ash, and at the top the shoulder came up under you like a hill under a boat.

Then the ear. She had read about ears and never had one in front of her: a yard across, and moving very slowly, the way a harbour moves.

“You put the ladle in,” Hulda said, “and you pour, and you keep your face on the inside of the ladle.”

The honey went in and the throat worked, once, without waking. The breath that came back over Ottoline’s arm smelled of turned earth and of rain that fell in a year nobody living had been born in.

“There is no arguing with that,” Hulda said, going down. “Forty years and no arguing.”

At the bottom of the ladder a girl in a dye-house apron waited with a board of charcoal against the barrow.

* * *

The girl was called Sim, she was fifteen, she worked in the dye-house three days a week, and the question on the board said: “What is the honey for.”

Nobody would answer it, and the answer was not in a book, and Ottoline had asked at nine and been told that a keeper who asks what the round is for is half a child who used to work here.

Now there was a second question under the first one, and Ottoline read it standing up, with a hive roaring forty feet away.

“Does the long sleep have to be tonight,” it said. “Can it be some other night.”

Sallow took the board, looked at it, and gave it back.

“You can ask the giants,” he said. “Nobody has ever come back with an answer, and the round is at dusk, and you will carry the bucket.”

* * *

She began with her own hives, because she was better at bees than anybody in the yard.

In the third week of July, at seventy degrees in the shade, a strong colony is a noise like a river with stones in it. Ottoline had a hive raised from a swarm in April, and on the first hot Thursday she put her ear to the stand and heard nothing.

She sat with her face to the straw and listened until her knees went numb.

Bees do not go quiet. They cannot. A colony is thirty thousand insects with one job, and the job is loud, and a silent hive is dead, or robbed, or has swarmed and gone.

Her hive was not finished. It was fat, it was heavy, and it was doing nothing.

Sim found her out on the grass with her ear on the straw.

“What are you doing,” Sim said.

“Listening.”

“To what?”

“To them.”

Sim knelt and put her ear to the box, and came up with grass on her cheek.

“There is nothing there,” she said.

“There is going to be nothing there,” Ottoline said, “and that is the problem. Listen to the row by the gate and then come back and listen to this one.”

Sim went and came back and said the same thing both times.

With her hand on the box she went up and down both rows. The row by the field edge was roaring, and then it went quiet in a line that ran up the hill to where the big one was breathing.

* * *

She took the master up the hill to hear it with her.

He got as far as the second row and stood in front of a hive making no sound at all, and listened to it the way you listen to somebody lying.

“That is the breathing,” he said.

“It is the air,” she said, and put her hand flat on the ground. The earth under it was moving very slightly, in and out. “The air moves. The bees do not.”

“I have stood beside a hive every day since I was nine,” he said. “That is the sound of a colony that is comfortable, and you are telling me a comfortable colony is quiet.”

“I am telling you they have stopped. The box is not empty, and I can feel the weight of it.”

Hulda said that young people were always certain, and that certainty came out of the same place as the mistakes.

The master looked at the row of silent boxes for a long time.

“Fifty years,” he said. “I have been in this yard since before your mother could reach a handle. I have never gone up that hill and I am not starting at my age. Put the rest of it in your report, and go and do the round.”

“The round is at dusk,” she said, “and they will be quiet at dusk as well. I am not carrying anything up for you tonight.”

“It is not for you to decide that.”

“It is my hive,” Ottoline said, “and it is quiet, and nobody up here will say why.”

* * *

There was a fence at the back of the mill and long grass behind it, and the grass was full of people.

She had played at the far end of that field at nine, and remembered it now as a shape in a field rather than as a thing that was there.

There were about two hundred of them, lying on their sides in rows, the way a village lies down in a bad year.

Her father was in the second row under a coat she recognized, his eyes moved when she said his name, and his hands had once taken a scythe off her and thrown it over the wall.

She put her mouth near his ear.

“Wyl,” she said. “It is me. I have brought you a comb and it is a bad one, and I am sorry about that.”

His eyes went to her hand, and his right hand came up out of the grass and stopped, and stayed.

“You are not to be in this field,” said Hulda, from the gate.

“I am not doing anything.”

“You are standing in it.”

He had been under nineteen years, and he was not a giant. Nettie had carried him out on the guild’s cart and never once said what was in the bucket.

She held the comb up where he could see the light through it. It was dark and bitter, made in a month with nothing to work with.

Then Master Sallow came up the lane, did not go past her, said the thing once, and walked on without turning round.

“Your father is asleep in the north field and has been for nineteen years and he is not a giant, and there are two hundred of him in that grass, and the honey is not for the giants, it is for us, and we have been calling it a kindness.”

* * *

She sat in the yard for a day, and then she asked the master for the oldest rule in the guild.

The rule was that a colony cannot be commanded, only persuaded. He had taught it to her in April in about nine words, and then made her do the walking herself.

“You have been the master for nineteen years,” she said. “You have never once gone up.”

“No,” he said. “And you have been a keeper for two.”

“It has to walk,” she said. “Not carried. A colony will follow a moving outline on the ground, and that is all anybody has ever got, and you taught me that.”

The reeve came in, unrolled his list, and said the hives were the village’s winter, and would have them carried to the low farms by Friday if the guild touched anything on that hill.

Hulda carried the buckets and put the ladle in the ear, and when Ottoline said the name Alwin Tarr out loud in the yard, Hulda went white and stayed an hour.

The master said no for two days. On the third evening he came with the ladder, set it against the first box, held the foot of it while Ottoline worked, and said nothing for two hours, which was the most he had said in a year.

* * *

Sim’s board had a new line on it that morning, in charcoal.

“Is there something you want said,” it said. And underneath: “We are here.”

“If I can get one hive to walk,” Ottoline said, “they will all see it.”

“They will see one,” Sim said, “and then they will look away. Take two.”

Ottoline put the board on the box and stood four feet off, and did not tell the bees where to go. She told them what she wanted and walked backwards, and the beard came over the lip in a brown sheet that smelled of hot wax and sounded like a fire in a far field.

Nobody said anything, because a moving colony is a thing you do not talk over. It went up the lane with Ottoline walking backwards in front of it, and Sim before it with the board held up like a sail.

They reached the first head at noon.

Hulda put the ladle in where she had put it every dusk for nineteen years, in daylight, in front of two hundred people, and the honey went in, and the big one was not asleep.

It opened its eye.

It came up off the hill on one elbow, slow, the grass pouring off it in sheets, and the mill shed most of its slates and nobody was under them. Then the second, and the third, until eleven of them stood in a line in the noon sun, and a shadow went over the square and put the street lamps out.

They were careful, and Ottoline saw it better than anyone: careful the way a man is with a full cup where a baby is asleep. They moved four hundred years of stillness out of their joints one at a time, and not one put a foot on a person.

The reeve Alwin Tarr ran for his cart and got as far as the wicket gate, which was full of people and bees, and stood in it with his list.

* * *

It was Sim who asked the first question, standing in front of the first one with her board turned over and blank, in the ordinary voice, because there was nothing to be careful about now.

The first giant answered her at once, and politely, and in the wrong language.

It went on. The valley got a word in the first minute and no more, and by the end people at the back were sitting in the grass, which is how Karst heard all of it, sitting.

The second answered it too, and so did the third. Then somebody said the word for honey and there was a lot of laughing, and one enormous hand went halfway down towards a house, and stopped, and went back up.

Somebody at the back said: did it say the buckets.

“Say it again,” Sim said. “And slowly.”

Sim asked it a third time, slowly, with her hands, and the first one looked at the board, then at her, then took a very long time and answered in the valley’s own language, in four words.

It said: “We know about the buckets.”

Nobody made any noise at all, and above them, along the two rows of hives that had been quiet for four hundred years, every colony was roaring at once, and Ottoline sat down in the row and let them.
