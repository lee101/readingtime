# The Snowglobe Train at the End of Winter

![Cover art: The Snowglobe Train at the End of Winter](../art/intermediate/09-the-snowglobe-train-at-the-end-of-winter.webp)

The first passenger to board the train was a mouse wearing a yellow thimble hat. Her coat had a hole in the pocket.

“Ticket, please,” said the guard, who held out his brass stamp.

The mouse searched through her coat, then her boot. At last she handed him a raindrop frozen solid.

The guard sniffed it and decided it was worth exactly one shower. “Where are you going?”

“Window Street. My match is cold.”

He punched the raindrop and stamped it twice. *PITTER-PATTER, PITTER-PATTER.*

Behind the little engine came a green carriage, a blue caboose, and a freight car packed with snow stored since the year’s first snowfall. As the mouse hurried inside, the train shook a branch and dust fell through the gray attic light.

Nessa stood beside the engine with a brass key in her hand, which she had found that morning rolling beneath her grandmother’s bed like a silver beetle. It was the only bright thing inside the neglected snow globe that filled the attic, and she had been awake since dawn.

Outside the glass walls, Bellwether sat beneath a winter that had stopped moving. Yesterday’s footprints were still fresh, because the snow had frozen over them before anyone could take a step.

“We must go now,” said the guard, “because if the thaw bell rings before midnight, the stored snow will melt loose.”

Without that snow, Bellwether would have spring for the first time in a year, but the thaw had forgotten how to warm the ground. Meltwater would rush down the valley before noon and freeze into walls of ice around Pennyfold.

Nessa climbed into the engineer’s seat.

“All aboard!” she called.

“Last train of winter!” answered the guard, and the engine coughed awake.

He slammed his stamp twice, and behind the glass Bellwether shrank to the size of a toy as the train began to roll.

The rails shone under the snow as the train crossed the village in two broad curves, and a blue thread snapped into a staircase beneath the frozen Window Street sign. The mouse hurried down it, leaving one tiny footprint in the glass, and the train passed below the bridge at Silver Sill.

Other passengers boarded with their own troubles: a baker carried a jar of yeast that had stopped rising, and a postman brought twelve letters nobody could read yet. Pip had one pocketful of ladybugs, which he planned to release when spring came.

At the high fence, mountains flashed white beyond the glass. Meltwater trembled at their feet, trapped in a hard green wall.

“Snow for Pennyfold,” called the guard, and the freight car opened.

A loading crew scooped out the stored snow, packing each shovelful into a clear cube no larger than a teacup. Every cube was stamped with the day on which it fell, as if each flake remembered which winter it belonged to.

“Take the oldest first,” Nessa called. “It has waited longest.”

The crew hurried, and a mountain of fresh snow dwindled to a foothill. Then only two shovelfuls were left under the freight car window.

Nessa lifted a scoop and dropped it onto the rail, where it crumbled into glitter.

One small train whistle sounded far away, so low and clear that it trembled through the frozen valley. A light moved behind Pennyfold’s tallest chimney.

“Pennyfold is ringing for snow,” said Pip, “and they have never done that before.”

“Because they have been out of snow since midsummer,” Nessa said.

Pennyfold’s people once knew a safe thaw, and their thaw-master mixed the first meltwater with river sand and flower roots so it would warm seeds without racing downhill. But the town clockmaker cracked the great thaw bell one night, and that melt poured into the valley as a flood that froze before it reached the fields.

The thaw-master had promised the waiting spring, “You may wake when every bell in Bellwether rings for joy.” The spring had mistaken every alarm for joy ever since. Now only rain could carry Pennyfold’s bell across the valley.

Pip climbed onto the coal box and offered to whistle louder. But Nessa told him the stored snow would melt on the rail.

The guard checked his watch and announced that two hours remained until midnight.

“Bringing the snow will not help,” said Pip, “because the spring does not know how to melt.”

Across the valley, people carried lamps from room to room. Seven bright circles appeared in the upper panes.

“Seven panes,” Nessa said, remembering the thaw-master’s blue manual. Its first chapter used seven hollow snowflakes, and its last was titled *A Promise Is a Bridge*.

“Seven sledge tracks,” she said. “If the guides can reach the base of the wall, we can cut a gate.”

The loading shovels struck faster, and a crack wound across the winter sky above the neglected snow globe.

“The globe is waking,” said the guard, “and when its cracks join, the world may see the train and shake all this winter free.”

Something enormous thumped on the glass. A giant child’s face pressed into the roof of the globe. One eye looked down, and a pink finger pointed at the thinning freight car.

“What does the world see?” Pip whispered.

“A chance,” Nessa said, and she thought of the blue ballast crystals that kept the train steady on the sharpest curves. Each one was made from safely saved melt, with a tiny springtime sound trapped inside.

“We need every crystal.”

“If we unload them here, we cannot reach Pennyfold in time,” said the guard, “and you are not leaving your post.”

“Then give me directions,” Nessa said. “Couple the engine to the guide car, reverse down the slope, and gather the crew on the way.”

The guard blinked and slammed the whistle three times. Pip pulled the reverse lever until the engine puffed backward. Snow sprayed from the wheels as the little train slid faster and faster between the white trees. Its bell rang as the crew chased it, waving their shovels.

The engine struck the crystal car, and the small coupling hook caught. A cage rolled into place around the front of the engine.

The train raced downhill and curled around a frozen waterfall. It came to rest beside the loading crew, whom the guard pulled aboard one by one, and the trainmaster bent over the last heap to fill a shovel with the oldest snow.

“You are nine,” he said as he worked. “I will guard the globe.”

The trainmaster passed the shovel up, and the guard dumped it into the freight car before he climbed aboard himself. “I will guard it very carefully,” Nessa answered.

As he passed her, the trainmaster touched the brass key. “For the first whistle,” he said, and it was all the promise Nessa could ask for.

The guide car thundered across the valley, and a brass hopper opened between the engine and the crystals. The crew tipped in the remaining stored snow, and each clear cube melted inside its own crystal cup. The first sounded like a door opening, and the second like a sparrow waking. The third sounded like boots crossing a bridge.

When the first six sounded together, blue light rolled over the train, and the crack in the sky froze with a chiming note. The child’s enormous face smiled.

Ahead, Pennyfold’s sledge lamps blinked at the ice wall. Only six crystals were on the engine car. The seventh hung beneath the last carriage, where a loose chain had tangled around it.

Nessa climbed outside while the wind tore at her hood, and below them the empty valley seemed to wait inside the snow globe with Bellwether’s church small as a toy.

“Train, slow to walking pace!” she called. Pip dragged the brake halfway down.

Nessa crawled beneath the last car, where snow packed around her sleeves and the chain was caught through a ring that would not turn. She gripped it and pulled, but her mittens slipped on the frozen metal.

Above her, the first star of the thaw bell appeared in the attic sky.

“Keep the promise!” the guard shouted from the doorway.

Which promise? Nessa’s mind raced faster than the wheels, past the trainmaster’s promise and her grandmother’s unfinished promise, until it reached the thaw-master’s impossible promise to wake the spring safely. A promise, she understood, was not a wish, but the thing you did after the words.

The thaw-master had promised waiting spring that it might wake. But he had never promised the water where to go.

Nessa released one mitten and pushed the crystal free with her bare hand. The chain snapped, and the crystal flew up its safety string into the engine’s wheel.

“Full speed!” she shouted, and Pip pulled the brake home.

The train dashed up the ledge while the blue light made seven shadows. Each one threw a narrow path across the ice wall. Where the paths crossed, the thick green ice began to melt. But not with a flood.

Instead the meltwater slipped through seven channels and spread gently over the roots hidden below. It sank into the frozen earth, and far beneath the train, seeds opened like tiny hands.

The thaw bell began to ring above Bellwether, and Pennyfold’s people climbed the ladder on the far side of the new gate. They hung buckets to catch the last clear drops while children carried lamps to the buried windows, and grown-ups rolled the fallen clock hands forward until each tick came harder than the one before it and the hands agreed.

A quiet sound passed through the snow globe. Nessa realized it was the valley breathing out. The new snow was still white, but the air had begun to shine.

Pip looked into his empty pocket. “My ladybugs?”

“Still asleep,” Nessa said. “They can wait for a real spring.”

Above them, the roof of the globe was opening, and the child’s face lifted away. A broad hand turned the whole thing until morning sunlight swept down through the glass.

The village clock struck eight for the first time in a year. Brown shoots stood in circles around the waiting sledge. Far away, water dripped from the eaves of Bellwether’s houses and gathered in the gutters instead of racing down the mountain. Her grandmother’s key was warm in her hand, and she closed her fingers around it.

A promise, it seemed, could melt a winter and show the spring where to go.
