# The Rooftop Academy for Impossible Weather

![Cover art: The Rooftop Academy for Impossible Weather](../art/intermediate/11-the-rooftop-academy-for-impossible-weather.webp)

The first thunderclap of the morning rang like a dropped tray, and twelve-year-old Nia Vale spun on the rooftop toward the dark copper bell. A silver crackle crawled over its rim while the same sound rolled across the sky, making the windows shiver in their frames.

"Is that a storm?" Nia asked.

"Not exactly," said Benji Arrows, the student nearest the lightning rod, who was holding a wool blanket over his head and trying to look brave. "That was a forecast disagreement."

The roof tilted under Nia's boots as a directionless wind tugged at the laundry lines and carried the bitter smell of winter roses. A second later came a hot gust smelling of toast and old engines, and students shouted while the schoolhouse chimneys smoked in July weather.

At the center of the roof stood the Forecast Hall, a round room with a glass dome where brass needles trembled over maps of the city. Each needle pointed toward a different street, and the instruments disagreed as loudly as the students.

Nia's father had called the academy a school for people who could not find jobs elsewhere, not mentioning that its classrooms were above the clouds and its lessons were made of dangerous weather. He had also neglected to say that the newest student was expected to forecast a storm before lunch.

"Nia Vale," called Headmistress Orris from the dome doorway, "you are late for orientation."

"I was trying not to be struck by a disagreement."

"Excellent," said Orris. "It means the instruments are awake."

Orris was small enough to disappear behind a stack of lesson plans, but she never vanished when weather was nearby; her silver braid hung down her back like a plumb line, and her spectacles reflected green and gold lights. She handed Nia a brass card with a blank space for a name.

"Apprentices do not announce certainty," Orris explained. "They announce what they know, what they suspect, and what they need to learn, so fill this in honestly."

Nia looked at the card, whose first line read FORECAST, and at the boxes labeled Wind, Heat, Visibility, and Something Not Yet Measured. "I'll need more pencils," she said.

"Take an entire drawer."

Nia's first task was sorting weather reports, and the common table held twelve scrolls from the city districts: fog that made apologies appear as silver fish, heat rising from a school where no one had spoken an obvious rule, and a courage-storm that rolled through alleys whenever someone refused a safe step.

Every report ended with a confident number, including the one Nia read aloud. "Apology-fog: thirty-seven percent, clearing at noon."

"That forecast is wrong," said a girl in a cloud-colored scarf.

The girl introduced herself as Lio, a third-year apprentice with a tight braid and ink stains on both thumbs. "The fog began at sunrise," she said, "so it cannot clear at noon because the apology that caused it has not happened yet."

"Then how do you know?"

"Because I am inside it." Lio pulled her scarf over her mouth, and a small silver fish appeared between the fibers, flicking its tail. "Sorry."

Nia reached toward it, but the fish leapt into the air and became a wet spot on the table.

"Better not touch those," Lio warned. "They are apologies that have nowhere to go."

Their mentor, Teodor Quill, was arranging brass cups on the rooftop rail, broad-shouldered and narrow-minded about the proper use of umbrellas. He had no magic of his own, only a talent for making complicated things into lists.

"Apprentices," he said, "a forecast is not a prophecy; it is a map with a warning on the edge."

"Then why does the Hall of Forecasts announce its work as certain?" Nia asked.

Teodor's cup stopped moving. "The Hall is preparing a presentation for the Weather Council, including a new forecast called Bright Certainty."

A cheer rose from the stairwell as students poured onto the roof in their best blue coats. At the front walked Soren Vale, Nia's sixteen-year-old cousin, who was famous for never being caught by surprise and carried a silver case marked with the academy seal.

Soren had been chosen to present Bright Certainty, which would guarantee perfect weather for the next three days: no courage-storms, no apology-fog, and no heat from unspoken rules. A single calm forecast for the whole city made Nia's stomach tighten.

"That cannot be true," she said.

"Certainty is the point," Soren said, holding the case away from the wind. "People are tired of carrying umbrellas for possibilities."

Teodor's jaw tightened. "Certainty is a useful tool when it describes the instruments, but it is dangerous when it replaces them."

"The Council likes clean answers," Soren said with a smile.

Nia watched the needles in the glass dome, one pointing at the orphanage district and another trembling over the old market, where shoppers argued beneath awnings about which rules had always been rules. A third pointed directly at Bright Certainty's display case.

"Those needles disagree," Nia said.

"Forecasts are not supposed to disagree," Soren replied.

"Then how will we know when one is wrong?" Nia asked.

Before he could answer, the glass dome flashed and a crack of courage-storm lightning struck the horizon, sending every weather-vane spinning west at once.

The storm hit the school like a running crowd, though it did not knock anyone over; it made them brave. Students stepped onto narrow ledges to prove they could, one apprentice climbed the bell-tower ladder, and another announced that he could tell the Headmistress exactly what to do.

A third sprinted toward the edge of the roof, laughing as if fear were a coat that no longer fit. Nia felt the courage-storm in her legs, whispering that she could walk to the glass dome, take Soren's case, and climb the highest weather tower without ever coming down.

Then she heard Benji cry out.

He had wandered to the rain gutter, where the storm's wild courage had made him step onto a narrow pipe over the street. His blanket was gone, his face was white, and his feet kept moving.

Nia grabbed the nearest object, which happened to be the Headmistress's silver braid, and it came loose in her hand.

"Nia, do not pull me toward the roof!" Orris shouted.

Nia stared at the braid and then at the bell, realizing that the storm fed on bold actions and made the next action seem like the only possible one. She had pulled because she was certain pulling would save Benji, but certainty was not the same as knowing what to do.

"I need a rope," Nia called.

"There is no rope," Teodor said.

"Then I need a question."

Nia let go of the braid and shouted over the thunder. "Benji, can you hear me?"

He nodded.

"Do you want me to tell you where to step?"

"Yes," he shouted, "but I also want to prove I can do it alone."

"Which one is true?"

Benji's answer came after a long, cracking burst of wind. "Both."

"Then we do both." Nia turned to Lio. "Make a bridge."

Lio blinked. "With what?"

"Fog. Please."

Lio ran to the bell and pulled the apology rope, which answered with a low note. Silver fish poured from the stairwell and swam through the air in a shining arch, gathering into a slippery bridge between the roof and the gutter pipe.

Teodor looped one end around the rail while Benji crawled across, and Nia held his sleeve as the courage-storm howled around them. When he reached solid tiles, he threw his arms around her.

"Why didn't you just shout that I should be brave?" he asked.

"Because being brave is not the same as being safe," Nia said.

Soren stood inside the dome, silver case still in his hands. "The storm is weakening."

"No," Nia said, pointing at the needles. "It is changing."

Apology-fog rolled across the city, and beneath it a red glow appeared in the market district. Heat shimmered over the school where a new rule had been posted: NO QUESTIONS ASKED IN THE EAST HALL.

No one had needed to say the rule aloud; it had simply become true, and the shame of everyone who obeyed it had turned into heat. Bright Certainty could not cover that glow, only hide it behind a number.

* * *

Two days later, the academy held its forecast trial, where students presented readings to a panel made up of a weather courier, a roof engineer, and a retired mapmaker. Soren stood beneath the glass dome with his silver case, and his forecast was beautiful: Wind, twelve percent; Heat, eight percent; Visibility, perfect; Chance of courage-storm, none.

He had checked every instrument three times, but Nia stood at the back with Lio, Teodor, and Benji, whose evidence was messier after a day spent climbing water towers, asking shopkeepers about storms, and interviewing children who hid under their beds during thunder.

Some notes disagreed, and one page was stained with soup, which made the judges lean forward rather than away.

"Your reading is unacceptable," a judge told Nia. "You have not predicted a single outcome."

"I have predicted conditions," Nia said. "The courage-storm began when people felt pushed, the apology-fog is carrying a wrong, and the market heat is growing under an unsaid rule; none of those things can be removed by predicting a nicer day."

Soren's face tightened. "People cannot carry uncertainty forever."

"No," said Nia, "but they should not be punished for carrying it honestly."

He opened the silver case, revealing white ribbons woven with single unbreakable threads, each designed to mark one certain direction in the sky. When Bright Certainty was announced, those ribbons would be tied to every weather-vane in the city.

Teodor stood. "If the forecast is wrong, who corrects it?"

"The forecast will be right," Soren said.

"Being right later is not the same as being certain now."

The judges exchanged glances, but the mapmaker smiled, remembering the year a kingdom had sealed its borders because one forecast promised endless rain. By the third week, people had burned their gardens rather than admit the forecast had failed.

"How do you propose to change the forecast?" the mapmaker asked Nia.

Nia had no answer ready, so she looked at the instruments, where every needle pointed at a different place, and then at the white ribbons, the empty spaces between them, and the words on the brass card in her pocket: what I suspect, what I need to learn.

"Let the uncertainty stay visible," she said. "Give every district a chance to report what it sees; if the readings agree, publish the agreement, and if they do not, publish the difference. Name the things causing the weather."

"That would leave no room for a single clean forecast," Soren said.

"There is room for a forecast everyone can use."

The academy bell rang, and a gust of hot wind blew the dome open as white ribbons lifted from the case and raced across the roof, each seeking a weather-vane. The courage-storm had not passed; it had joined the heat and fog, and a dark column formed beyond the eastern wall.

Nia grabbed the brass card. "Benji, the alarm cord. Lio, the apology bell. Teodor, open the lower shutters!"

They moved together: Benji pulled the cord until it jammed halfway, Lio rang the apology bell so silver fish spilled across the floor, and Teodor threw open the shutters, letting cold air rush through the school. The gust lifted the ribbons and tangled them around the weather-vane needles.

Soren lunged for the case.

"Don't!" Nia shouted. "The ribbons are trying to make one answer out of every different sky."

He stopped, one ribbon wrapped around his wrist and his skin flushed beneath it. The unspoken rule of the academy had entered the room: the Headmistress's forecast must always be correct, though no one had spoken it and everyone had obeyed it for years.

Nia reached for the ribbon, then stopped herself, knowing she could cut it and save Soren but not whether doing so would release the heat onto the city.

"Soren," she said, "what do you want?"

He stared at the white thread. "I want my forecast to matter."

"Your uncertainty matters too."

The ribbon tightened, and Soren held out his hand. "Cut it."

Nia used Teodor's field knife, and the white thread snapped. Heat burst from the eastern wall, apology-fog surged over the market, courage-storm thundered across the roofs, and every instrument screamed for one breath.

Then Nia looked at the brass card and read aloud, "Wind: shifting. Heat: rising where a rule is unspoken. Visibility: uncertain, but improving. Courage: available to anyone who chooses to use it."

Nia looked at Lio. "Read yours."

Lio held the stained notes. "The fog is carrying an apology from the east hall; someone is sorry for obeying the rule without speaking it."

"Read the market's report."

A courier's voice came through the roof hatch. "Shopkeepers say the rule is not law but a habit, and they are willing to say so."

Nia faced the judges. "Then our forecast is a map with warnings: no promise of calm, only a request for honesty."

The mapmaker took the brass card. "That is not a forecast that punishes uncertainty."

The weather engineer tied the white ribbons to the dome as markers, not commands. "Bright Certainty is withdrawn."

Soren stood alone for a moment, then helped Nia secure the alarms, his hands shaking but no longer asking for a better number.

* * *

At sunset, the first apology-fog lifted from the market, and people stepped into the streets to speak the rules they had followed without noticing. Some admitted pretending not to hear a friend, while others said they had kept a queue in the wrong place because no one dared question it, and the heat broke into warm, ordinary breezes.

The courage-storm remained for a while, rolling around the academy, but it no longer pushed anyone toward danger. Students met it in pairs, stepping across narrow ledges together, holding ropes, counting breaths, and choosing when to stop.

By night, the rooftop held a new kind of weather board whose needles still pointed in different directions. Beneath them, students had written changing conditions, questions, and reports from every district, and no number claimed to speak for the whole city.

Nia stood beside Soren, watching the first stars appear in a clear patch of sky.

"It's not certain," she said.

"No," he said, "but we know where to look."

Below them, the city answered with bells, each carrying a different note without trying to silence the others. The academy opened its rooftop doors, and students hurried out to meet a forecast that was alive, unfinished, and shared.
