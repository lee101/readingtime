# The Mask Was a Contract

![Cover art: The Mask Was a Contract](../art/intermediate/141-the-mask-was-a-contract.webp)

The Marchands of Halversgate Row have made capes and masks since the year before the harbour wall was finished, which is four generations, and nothing they have made has ever been a costume. A Marchand coat has one thing in it and the thing is not on the outside, and everybody in this town knows that a caped figure is a man in a mask and almost nobody knows that the mask is a document.

Roza Marchand was sixteen when her uncle died in the March. His name was Kellan Marchand. He had lived in the two rooms over the workshop, he had made masks for forty-two years, and he had made the mask and the coat that were found in the tin under his bed, and the tin had a piece of tape on it with the date written on it in his own hand, which was the week he went into the almshouse, which was the week he could not go on.

The small print was in the lining of the mask, printed in a small clean type on a strip of linen, and this is the whole of it. That the wearer shall answer when called, on four nights in every month, for so long as the coat is worn in Halversgate. That the payment of the coat be taken from the line of the maker and not from the wearer, being one year of it for every hour of service. That the contract end when the balance be paid, or when the coat be returned to the maker, or, being the twelfth clause, when the coat be unmade by the hand of the maker or by the blood of the maker that has read it.

* * *

The coat was the family style. That is all anybody ever said about it and it is enough. Green wool, cut long at the back, with a brass clasp at the throat and a hood at the shoulder, and it is not shaped to a body, it is hung on a peg. The mask was white, and the eye holes were not oval, they were cut square at the corners because a Marchand had cut them that way in the second generation and nobody since has asked why.

Roza held the mask up to the window on Halversgate Row and looked at the strip of linen inside it and read it twice, and then she went and got her coat on and went down to the workshop and looked for the mask her uncle had actually worn.

It was hanging on the third hook, under two finished ones, where he had put it to keep it out of the dust, and it was the same mask, and the strip was the same, and she read it a third time because she had not believed it the first two.

“Four nights a month,” she said, out loud, in an empty workshop.

Then she found, on the bench, in the groove where the shears lay, a tin of black ink and a fine brush, and she looked at that for a while, and she put it back.

* * *

The evidence was not hidden. That is the thing about Halversgate Row and it took her a fortnight to accept it.

In the corner of the workshop under the window there were forty-one notepads, one a year, and each of them had the same thing in it: the dates, four to a month, for forty years, and beside each date the street, and the time he was called and the time he was home again. He never wrote what he had done. He wrote where he had been.

His hands were the hands of a small child. He could not open a jar, and in the last year he could not hold the shears at all, and Roza found his grip cloths in a box, eleven of them, cut for four different hands and all of them used.

His eyes went after his hands. He had read a paper by the window for four months before he went into the almshouse, and then he had asked Roza's mother to read it to him, and then he had asked Roza to read it to him, and Roza was thirteen and did it every day for the last eleven weeks of his life.

And nobody knew. That was the part she could not put down.

* * *

Halversgate thought Kellan Marchand was a volunteer.

She went to the Register on Coombe Street on the Thursday, because she had run out of people to ask, and Verity Sallow was in the room with the kettle on and the roster sheet on the table, and Roza put her hand flat on the roster sheet and asked whether a man called Kellan Marchand was on it.

Verity looked at the sheet. It was the master sheet, the whole of it, eleven years in one column.

“There is no Marchand on this,” said Verity.

“Forty years.”

“No.”

“He answered for forty years.”

“He answered,” said Verity, “and he was never on it, and I have thought about that more than you have, because I make a thing of not having debts in this room and I have had that man on my conscience since I was thirty-four.”

The rule of the Register is that there is no money in it and no credit, and a man who gives a night a week for forty years is not a volunteer in Halversgate, he is a creditor, and Verity Sallow had never once been able to explain to a committee what the difference was between a person who needs help and a person who is owed.

* * *

Roza went through the notepads in the workshop in the April, one a year, sitting on the bench, and she built a street list, and the street list was the fourth generation's answer to the question nobody had ever asked.

Foil Street. Gaunt Street. The steps behind the Assembly Room. Pell Court. Water Lane, twice. The eleven houses off Coble Row, on four separate nights, at two in the morning, over four years, and he had never once written what was happening at them.

In nineteen ninety-nine he had been out nine times in one month. The twelfth clause was in his mask the whole time and he was out nine times in one month.

On the back of the notepad for that year he had written one line in pencil and nothing else on it, and the line said, SHE WILL GET HURT IF I STOP, and there was no way of telling whether SHE meant any particular person, and Roza decided not to try, and has not since.

* * *

Then she went through the coat.

A Marchand coat comes apart, and not easily, and not by cutting. Every seam on it is a seam a Marchand sewed, and it comes apart the way a thing comes apart when it is unpicked: the thread has to be drawn out with a blunt needle from the inside so that the stitch holes close behind it, and you cannot hurry it and you cannot leave it half done, because a coat in Halversgate is on somebody tonight or it is in a drawer with the lining out.

Roza had watched him do it eleven times by the time she was fifteen. She sat at the bench on the ninth of May with the twelve strips of linen laid out in order on the boards beside her, reading them as she unpicked, and the strips did not change, and the twelfth clause was on the last one, at the hem, on the left side of the lining, where the seam is the strongest and where the hem is doubled and where it is the least possible place to print anything that anybody is going to read.

Four generations had read eleven clauses.

* * *

She could have ended it in one minute. That is the thing she has never been able to get past. Unmade by the blood of the maker that has read it, and she had read it, and she had the blunt needle in her hand, and she could have taken the coat to pieces and put the lining on the table and been finished with her uncle's forty years.

Instead she hung it on the second hook and put the mask on.

* * *

It came on a Friday in the June, a little after ten, from the Quay Steps, where a man had gone down on the tide line.

She had the coat on and the mask under her arm and she did not put the mask on, and that is the part she will not be talked out of, because she wanted to know what it was before she decided about it.

She said, “I can do it.”

The man on the steps looked at her for a while, and then he looked at the coat, and then he said, “Don't do that.”

“I can do it,” she said again, because she had not said the second thing and the second thing was the important one. “But I want you to know what it is before I do. There are four nights a month and an hour costs a year and it does not come back, and I am telling you that as a person, not as a Marchand.”

He got up. He said, “Where.”

They were down on the flats for two hours and ten minutes with a boat rope off the second shed and her back under the load, and he was fifteen and his arm was broken in two places and it did not come out until late in the second hour, and then he sat down on the mud and put his face in his good hand.

Then she went home along the Quay Steps in the dark, up past the rope store, along Halversgate Row, and she was not tired at all, and the two years are not a thing she can feel anywhere, and she has never been able to explain that to anybody.

* * *

There was no scar, and there was no place on her that a doctor could have found.

What there was was the hem of the lining. The Marchand coats have a printed column at the hem with a name in each row and a ruled line against each row, and a maker crosses the line when a year of the line is taken. Her uncle's name had forty lines crossed in black ink, and every one of the forty had been crossed in his own hand, because he had done his own accounting for forty years and had never once asked the town for a single hour of it.

Roza went to the bench on the Sunday and looked at her own row.

Two lines were crossed, thin and even, and they were not in her handwriting, and there is nobody she can ask whether the ink is permanent because the only person who would have known was the man whose ink it was.

She put the coat on the bench on the eleventh of June and unpicked it in a day and a half, and the wool came off in panels and the lining came off in strips, and she laid the twelve strips out in order on the boards, and the ink on the hem went last, and the two crosses on her row were the last thing she looked at and the first thing she folded away.

Four generations had read eleven clauses, and a coat comes apart at every seam a Marchand sewed, and Halversgate has paid for that in four generations of hands that could not open a jar and was never once asked what it had bought.
