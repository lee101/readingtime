# The Lighthouse of the Inland Sea

![Cover art: The Lighthouse of the Inland Sea](../art/intermediate/71-the-lighthouse-of-the-inland-sea.webp)

Reuben came down the tower stairs backwards, three at a time, because the fourth step from the bottom has been loose since before his grandfather’s time, and a boy who trusts it goes into the wall.

In the oil room the lamp was his first job of the morning and his last job of the night. He trimmed the wick, filled the reservoir, and set the clockwork weight on its chain. It went down all day, and every night somebody had to wind it back up. Through the glass the beam went out flat over the water, and the water was the plainest in the world: two hundred miles from any coast, with tides, a bar, a shoal, and thirty boats in Ashmere harbour.

He wrote the day in the log. “Wind south-west three, falling toward evening. Visibility good. Sea slight. Lamp lit at half past six.”

The words were his. The hand was not. Nine hands had written in that book before him over four hundred years, and he was the third of his family to climb the stairs.

He came here at five years old, over the passes, with his father and one bag, because his father was a Halloway and the keeper had died that winter without a family. His father called it a mistake, stayed two seasons, and then went back over the mountain and left him.

Nobody in Ashmere had ever seen the ocean. Nine people were old enough to have walked west, and all nine stopped at a market. The sea in that log was a word, and he was the only one here who knew what it meant.

So the town was proud, and proud of him in particular, because a lighthouse here is not a building. It is a job, done by a person who comes down the harbour steps on Sunday. A mile out past the shoal, an old man called Tern kept a brazier on a raft, and every night his light answered the tower’s, one answer for one.

* * *

He had begun writing in the margins in the autumn, and he told nobody, and there were forty-one notes.

They were small and neat and in pencil, the way a boy writes when he wants to be argued with rather than believed. “The bar is sand and not rock. Take a lead line. On the Teeth: nothing, mud and eelgrass to the knee, go and try it.”

Halvard Ostrow ran the harbour, took the fees, and kept a tide board by the harbour door, where the ninth hour of the morning said there is less water on the bar. Everybody in Ashmere had been reading that sentence since before Reuben was born.

It is a useful kind of lie, Reuben thought, with his hands behind his back. Out on the bar at the ninth hour, a man is in four feet of mud going nowhere.

“You should learn to read your own log before you start correcting it,” Ostrow said, without any heat in it. “The shoal has taken three boats in ninety years.”

“The shoal has taken three boats because of the ninth hour on that board,” Reuben said. “Nobody drowns on a shoal. They stop on one.”

“The tide board is paid for out of the harbour,” Ostrow said. “If it is wrong, tell me what is right, and write it in a hand I can read.”

* * *

Wynn was ten and wanted the job more than he wanted bread, so he copied the forty-one notes out in his best fair hand and thought he was being useful.

Then the town read them.

It went the way weather goes. Then people found the parts they liked best, and the parts they liked best agreed with them. The pie woman stopped giving him two. A woman on the far quay told him that her mother had crossed the bar in a herring smack in the year the tower burned, and did not need a boy to explain mud.

Wynn’s father put Wynn’s name down for the harbour post. It was meant kindly. It cost the guild its last pair of hands.

Reuben stopped writing in the margins for eleven days. Then he started again, because he had begun to understand the book, and the book was the only object in Ashmere that had never once been careful about his feelings.

* * *

On a Thursday he rowed out to the Teeth at half tide, because a thing you cannot show anybody is only a thing you have said in a bad temper.

He took Ostrow’s smallest boat, since that was the one nobody would lend him, and stopped where the log says the Teeth are, and there was nothing there.

There was a hummock of grey mud with eelgrass on it, and beyond it the bottom went down in steps, like a staircase into a house. He put his hand in to the wrist, and then his leg, boot off, and the water took his leg and did not pull on it.

A sea takes. He had watched one take a bucket, a boy and a dog off a shingle beach at Seaward Cove, and it had not given either of them back. This water did not take. It rose and fell over a floor it was not interested in.

He rowed home with his trousers soaked to the knee and walked up the harbour steps in front of the whole town.

Nobody said anything. Wynn was the only one who spoke, and he said his father had told him to say nothing and would have preferred to say it himself.

* * *

He took the log off the shelf at dusk and sat with it on the oil-room floor, because the lamp was lit and the lamp was the only light in the building.

Forty pages in, he found the first mention of the Teeth: a line of stones at the harbour mouth, three feet under at low water. He had read that line a thousand times and thought it good writing.

Then he found that on the first page the word lake had been struck through, and the word sea written above it. Not crossed out and left. Struck through and replaced, in different ink, by a good confident hand, in the second year. Somebody had made a decision about that word, and everything after the first page said sea.

He turned to the outer margin, where the first keeper had crowded in a small cramped hand and wasted nothing.

The letters were still there. Somebody had written a name in a hand thinner than the rest, and damp had got into the page in a winter nobody wrote down, and the name had gone to a brown ghost you could see and not read.

He got the lamp glass down off its hinge and held the page sideways against the flame and moved it, because that is the trick, you move it, the letters come up and go down. An A. Another A. A letter with a V. Then a gap where the damp had taken the middle out.

He sat there with a four-hundred-year-old page in both hands while the wind got up and the weight went down its chain, and he could not make the name come up a second time.

* * *

Marget Halloway was seventy-two and lived below the harbour road with two chairs and a fire she did not need. She was the last person in the world who had been told anything.

When he was seven she had told him the light had to be kept whatever else was wrong, and he had taken that to be about the light.

He came down with the log under his arm and put it on her table, open at the first page, and she put her spectacles on and read the margin for a long time.

“That is a name,” he said. “I know it is a name. I want to know whose.”

She took the spectacles off and looked at the fire.

“I was told it once, by a woman who had it from a woman, and nobody ever asked me to keep it. I have kept it forty years because there was nobody else to keep it with. It is not mine to hand to a boy who wants it for the log.”

* * *

Ostrow came up the tower on the Thursday after, having thought about it for six days, and he was almost pleasant about it.

“Say it out loud,” Reuben said. “In the hall, once, with the whole town in it. Say the ninth hour is wrong. Say the Teeth are mud. Then stop writing in the margins, and let the log have a new binding. Wynn comes up the stairs with me on Sundays, and in four years it is Wynn’s job.”

“You would be first,” Ostrow said. “That is the offer. First, and quiet, and no board, and no shoal, and no teeth, and the pie woman will go back to two for the price of one.”

“And the log?”

“Nothing happens to the log. It stays on the shelf. Nobody will ever open it again.”

Then Reuben went down and told Marget Halloway, and she was standing in her doorway with her coat already on, because she had seen the light in his hand from the road.

“The promise was to keep the light burning so that she could be found,” she said, “and she was found on the first night by a man who did not live here, and we have kept burning for four hundred years out of a story that stopped being true before your father was born.”

* * *

He walked the two days up the passes and back down with his father, and neither of them said one word about the reason for the first three hours. At the bottom of the last pass Osgar said the lamp looked small from here, and Reuben said it did.

They took the first page up into the lamp room and lit the lamp at half past six, in front of the whole thing.

Then Reuben put the page flat against the lens and they moved it, all three of them, taking turns, holding it to the flame and away from it. Marget counted the letters under her breath. Osgar held the corner steady, because he was the only one whose hands did not shake.

It came up in pieces. An A. An N. An E. Anne. Then Ke, and a gap, and a V that had been a Y, and a name with the whole middle of it standing up out of the brown.

“Anneke Vahl, taken out of the water the first night the light burned, found alive by Joss Rennick of the west shore, who did not live here, and the fee of the oil is paid for ever in the keeping of it.”

Osgar read it twice. He put his finger on the name and left it there a while, and then he took the finger off, wiped the lamp glass with his sleeve, and asked whether anybody had been paying for the oil. “The town had,” Reuben said. “It still will.”

* * *

The light stays. The weight has to be wound, the wick trimmed, and somebody on the stairs at half past six, and there is nobody behind Reuben yet, and there is a boy of ten on the quay who will do it badly for a while first.

In the morning he wrote the day’s weather on a fair sheet in a good hand, and then he turned the log to the last written page, because the rule of the book is that a correction is entered and never scraped out, and the man who made the mistake has to leave his name to it.

“Entered by Reuben Halloway, keeper, being of this house: this water is a lake and not a sea. The bar is sand. The Teeth are mud. The ninth hour on the board is wrong. I say so on my own hand, and I will answer for it to my grandchildren.”

He blew the ink dry and put the book back on the shelf with the others, and outside the beam went out flat over the water with nothing to break on, and a mile out past the shoal, an old man called Tern lit his brazier and got his answer.
