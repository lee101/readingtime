# The Garden Gate That Needed Two Keys

![Cover art: The Garden Gate That Needed Two Keys](../art/intermediate/90-the-garden-gate-that-needed-two-keys.webp)

The wall was eleven feet of red brick on the lane side and six of flint on the other, and the gate in the middle of it was eight feet of wrought iron with two locks set in a column, one above the other, so that the two keyholes sat as close together as a pair of eyes.

A bar went across the inside of it, wrought too, and had gone across the inside of it since before Nadia was born.

Sol was standing six feet away with a pair of shears and a bundle of grass he was not cutting, watching the gate the way a man watches a wall he has been meaning to repoint for forty years.

“Nothing in there but grass and the pond,” he said, before she had asked. “The pond’s silted. The yews are half gone. It’s a hundred yards of dead ground in a heatwave, and that is the whole of it.”

“How do you know what’s in there if you have never been in there?”

“I know what a hundred years does to a garden,” Sol said. “I have kept the outside of it since I was seventeen. Grass, path, wall, the lot. The gate is not my job. The gate is a door with two locks in it and nobody to open it, and that has been the position since before I could reach the top of it.”

There was a laminated notice screwed to the gate at eye level, sun-bleached almost white, and beside it a small brass plaque that said GARDEN GATE: DUAL CONTROL, PENDING.

Nadia had the inventory in her school bag. She had read page forty-one so many times she could say it without looking. Two keys, family settlement, not produced.

* * *

Her grandmother kept the key in the drawer under the telephone, wrapped in a piece of a tea towel that had been red once.

“I have not touched that drawer since your mother’s wedding,” she said. “The phone works fine where it is.”

“It does not work fine, it is unplugged.”

“Then it is unplugged for reasons of my own.”

Nadia had counted. There was a clean rectangle of unfaded wood inside the drawer, and a clean rectangle of unfaded dust on the tea towel, and no way at all of working out how long either of those rectangles had been sitting there being exactly the same size.

The key had been taken out and put back eleven times. Nadia knew because her grandmother told her, in one flat sentence, and then went back to the crossword and did not say anything else about it.

Her grandmother did not want to go in. She wanted to be asked, and she was not going to ask, and the gate was the only thing either of them had ever wanted.

“You went in once,” Nadia said. “You were nineteen. You said it was as big as a church.”

“As big as a church and about as forgiving,” her grandmother said. “We were not supposed to. The girls were to be let in at noon and got out at four and that was the whole of the arrangement, and your great-aunt went in the back way with a ladder she left against the pear tree, and that is how the estate found out, and that is how we ended up with two keys and a rule.”

“What was the rule?”

“Both keys. Both turned. Together.”

“Your sister had the other one.”

Her grandmother put the crossword down. “Elspeth had the other one. And Elspeth is dead, and I have not seen the inside of that garden since the fourteenth of June, nineteen hundred and nineteen, and I have not been asked to since, and I am not going to start organising it at my age.”

Nadia went out through the hall and shut the door harder than she meant to.

* * *

Mr Iyer from the estate came on the Tuesday with a laptop and a copy of the schedule, and he had clearly been in a good mood when he arrived and less so by the middle of the afternoon.

“We have an offer on the whole holding,” he said. “Sixty-one thousand, and the buyer wants a completion date, and the one thing standing between me and a set of solicitor’s letters is item forty-one. Dual control, pending. Nobody can tell me who holds the keys.”

“Two sisters held them,” Nadia said. “In nineteen twenty-one. The settlement gave each of them one.”

“That is what the file says as well, and it is not good enough.” He turned the laptop round. “The keys were held by the family. The family is a house, and the house has been empty since nineteen sixty-one, and in sixty-one years nobody has produced a key or a certificate of loss. Until somebody does, that gate is a liability on a sale, and my client is going to take it off its hinges and put it in the skip. That is not a threat. That is item forty-one.”

The room went quiet. Nadia’s grandmother sat by the fire with her cardigan on and did not look up.

“Item forty-one also stops the sale if the keyholders are identified,” Mr Iyer said, more gently. “That is the same clause the other way round. Identify them, or lose them. Either is a page in my file and I can go home.”

Nadia’s grandmother now did look up. “I am the keyholder. Put that in it. Keyholder, identified, and available.”

“Madam, available is the part we have never been able to establish.”

Peter spoke from the doorway, where he had been standing for eleven minutes with wet socks.

“My mum’s got the other one,” he said. “It’s been in a tin since before I was born. My gran in Toronto is the sister’s daughter.”

The room went on being quiet in a different way.

Nadia’s grandmother looked at Peter for a long moment and then said, “You will go home and take that thing out of the tin and put it somewhere safe, and you will not put your hands in a tin again in my house.”

“I only put my hands in tins to help.”

“You only put your hands in tins to help,” her grandmother agreed, “and I have had four of your helps this week, and one of them involved the good teapot.”

* * *

They found the key that night on Nadia’s bedroom floor, because Peter had emptied his pockets the way he emptied his pockets, and it was a long flat iron thing with a square bit, black with tarnish, and about four inches long.

“That’s not a bottle opener,” Nadia said.

“I never said it was a bottle opener.” Peter sat on the floor against the wardrobe. “I said it looked like one. Those are different and you’re the one who isn’t allowed to complain.”

“It fits.” Nadia held it up against the light. “It’s the same key as hers. Same bit, same pattern. It’s half of a lock.”

“Nadia.” Peter was looking at her very seriously, which on Peter was faintly alarming. “I can’t do this on my own. If I hand it in they take it off you. I read the thing on the gate, pending, that means they take it off you.”

“My grandmother has said no.”

“Your grandmother has not said no,” Peter said. “She has said nothing at all, and that is not the same thing, and my gran would have said so.”

That was the part that stuck, and it stuck because it was true, and Nadia was awake until two thinking about it, with the key under her pillow like a bad tooth.

* * *

They took the key to Sol on the Thursday and he put both hands on the barrow and looked at it for a long time before he said anything.

“That the sister’s,” he said. “I saw her with it. Nineteen twenty-one, the last week they were both at home. She wore it on a chain round her neck because she lost things.”

“Sol.”

“There is nothing in there, Nadia.” He put the key down on the barrow and took his hands off it. “I have measured it from the outside for fifty-eight years. There is a yew walk two thirds dead. There is a pond with a metre of silt in it and a heron that does not nest. There is a wall that fell in nineteen thirty-one that I patched with cement because there was no money and nobody to pay. I know all of it, and I have not been in it since I was a boy, and the day I do go in I will find mud and a broken bench and forty minutes of work.”

“Peter’s got the key,” Nadia said. “And my grandmother’s got the other one.”

“Yes,” said Sol. “And your grandmother will not turn hers, and if she did not turn hers nobody in England could make her, and the estate will take the gate off its hinges in September, and there is nothing whatever in there worth losing.”

Nadia picked the key up off the barrow. “You’ve never looked.”

“No,” said Sol.

* * *

They did it on the last Saturday of the month, at eleven in the morning, in front of a solicitor from the estate who had been invited by Nadia’s grandmother by telephone and who stood on the gravel for the whole of it taking notes on his phone and saying almost nothing.

The grandmother would not turn her key. She stood with it in her hand for so long that Peter, for once in his life, said nothing, and then she put it back in her pocket and said she would not do it in front of a man with a phone.

So Nadia turned hers, and Peter turned his, and the bar came off its brackets, and the gate swung inward on a hinge that still had oil in it. The smell that came out was wet grass and old stone and something warm underneath it, like a cellar.

Nobody went in. Sol stood on the gravel with his hand on the bar and said, to nobody, that it was a shame for a door to stand open all day doing nothing.

Peter turned round. “Do you want to go in?”

She did not answer. She took the key out of her pocket, looked at it for a while, and then put it in the lower lock and turned it, and it went round, and that was the answer, and she did not give it any words at all.

There was no time for anybody to be brave. The garden went back in the way a room does when a door opens. Yew arches, a hundred feet of them, with the trunks grown together at the top into a roof. A lake with an island and a stone seat on the island. A rose walk that had become a bramble walk. A cold frame with a broken pane and, in it, a pear tree.

It had been planted in the nineteenth year of that century, by a young woman of twenty-three with her sleeves rolled up, on the last warm day of June, while her sister of nineteen sat on the low wall by the gate in her good coat with her boots off, because Elspeth had told her to keep her boots off.

It was six metres high and it was bearing, and it was, whatever else it was, a good tree.

Peter stood in front of it for a while with his mouth open, and then he went and got a tin from the barrow and filled it at the standpipe and set it in the fork of the tree, because Peter was a nuisance but he was not unkind.

Nadia’s grandmother went in first and went slowly, one hand on the yew, and did not go past the arch. She stood under the roof of it and looked up for a long time. Then Peter went in after her, because Peter was not going to be left standing anywhere, and Nadia went in last.

Sol did not come through. He stood on the gravel lane with his hand on the bar, and when Nadia came out and asked him why, he said he had said he would not, and that he was a man who meant it, and that he would come on the Sunday with a barrow and see what was worth shifting. He put the bar back on its bracket hooks and hung the length of chain through the loops so that it was ready to go across, and left it there.

Nobody closed the gate. It stood all day in the June with the two keys still in the locks, one above the other, turning a little every hour in the wind off the flats.
