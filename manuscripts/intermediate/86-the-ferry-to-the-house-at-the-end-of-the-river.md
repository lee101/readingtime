# The Ferry to the House at the End of the River

![Cover art: The Ferry to the House at the End of the River](../art/intermediate/86-the-ferry-to-the-house-at-the-end-of-the-river.webp)

Sasha’s grandmother had rules about the ferry, and the main one was the rule she broke most.

“You can ask the price,” she said, on the landing stage at half past seven, hooking her scarf over the rail. “Don’t ask the time. The time is printed.”

The ticket was a long strip of green card gone soft as cloth from nineteen years in a coat pocket. Sasha had been carrying it since the hospital car park, where her mother had said, take it, you can, without once looking at it.

Down the ramp four other people were waiting, and every one of them was carrying something that was not a bag. A woman had a roll of floorboards under her arm with sacking tied round the middle. A man had a door across his shoulders, the edge worn to the bone on one side. A boy of about ten carried a copper tap in a tea towel, unwrapping it to look and wrapping it again.

Odhiambo was coiling rope when they came aboard. He had forearms like a man who had hauled things since he was nine, and the rope he was coiling was the colour of the rope in the photograph on the ticket office wall. Sasha had looked at that photograph twice while her grandmother counted coins.

“You’re the girl,” he said. “Get on, before the light comes off the water.”

The boat was flat-bottomed and painted the green of the ticket. The engine started on the first pull. It sounded like a man clearing his throat, twice.

The river went east, and it kept going east for four hours, which struck Sasha as nonsense.

* * *

She counted. The phone in her pocket had no signal and nothing to do but count. At forty minutes the boat swung out into open water and came back along a curve half a mile over. Then again, wider. Then wider still, out past the point where the willows started, their branches lying flat on the surface like hands.

“You’re going round,” she said.

Odhiambo put his palm on the tiller and considered the middle distance.

“Slow water,” he said. “Three hours and fifty minutes of it, near enough. My grandfather called it the slow water, and so did his, and the one before that had a name for it I do not know. You go round the willows because the bottom comes up in the wrong places, and it comes up in the wrong places because the river has forgotten which way it means to run.”

“How much longer?”

“Ten minutes.” He spat over the side. “I have never once had a passenger ask me how long it takes. They want the time. The time is on the card.”

Her grandmother had said eleven miles at half past seven, and eleven miles had sounded like a walk.

Between the second turn and the third, the woman with the floorboards got a folding knife out of her coat and began trimming the end of one board, and the man with the door asked her not to, and she said the sacking had stretched.

Sasha sat with her grandmother at the stern. The engine dropped to a mutter and the willows went past without a sound, and out on the water it was eleven degrees colder than in them, and her grandmother had not brought a coat.

“You have done this a long time,” Sasha said.

“Thursdays.”

“Nineteen years.”

“Nineteen years,” her grandmother agreed, and did not add anything, which was a thing she did better than anybody Sasha knew.

* * *

The sun came up behind them and showed the whole shape of it, a wide flat ring of willow and grey water with the boat walking round the inside like something in a bowl. Sasha got the ticket out to read in the daylight, having carried it for six hours unread.

STAMP on the front, a ferryman’s thumb, ink the colour of a bruise.

On the back, printed: ADMIT ONE, THURSDAY 14 MAY. Under that, in the same small tidy type, DEPART 07:40, RETURN 12:40.

Her mother’s watch said eleven twenty. Sasha did the arithmetic twice, because the first time she did it wrong on purpose.

“You have been on this boat two hours and you have gone nowhere,” she said. “Where does it go?”

Her grandmother looked at the water for a while.

“You are not being told the destination, Sasha, there is a difference between not being told and there not being one, and the difference is that the ticket is not to a place, it is to a Thursday, the fourteenth of May, and the only requirement is that you turn up; there is one rule printed on that card and nobody alive has ever read it, and the rule is that it is good for one journey and one return, and the return is yours to arrange.”

Sasha waited for the rest of it. Her grandmother had the stillness she got when she had said the whole thing.

“The fourteenth of May,” Sasha said. “That is six weeks off. It is not six weeks off, it is a date somebody chose.”

“It is a Thursday,” her grandmother said, and that was all she said about it, then or afterwards.

* * *

The river narrowed. The willows stopped on one side and black rock came up on the other, and the engine laboured up the last two hundred yards into a channel so tight the boat had to be walked along with a pole.

Odhiambo put it in against stone steps cut into the bank. There were four steps, then a platform, then a level piece of ground the size of a two-car park, and on that ground were four stone footings in a rectangle, chalked, with a string line still stretched between two of them.

Everybody got off and started moving.

That was the part Sasha had not expected. The woman with the floorboards walked straight to the far footing, laid the boards flat and sighted along them with her thumb. The man with the door stood in the middle of the rectangle turning it on his heel, deciding, and then carried it to the north side and stood it up and leaned it against nothing, and it stayed. The boy with the tap unwrapped it one more time and put it down in the middle of the ground where anybody could step on it, and his mother told him to pick it up.

Her grandmother got off last. She had a canvas bag under the seat that Sasha had not noticed, and out of it came a tin of paint, a size nine, with the brush soldered to the lid.

She carried it to the edge of the platform, sat down on the cold stone, opened the tin on her knees, and began without hurrying to paint the string line white so that the footings would come out true.

“This is the last stop,” Odhiambo said to Sasha, while the others worked. “After this the water is too shallow and we do not pretend otherwise. My great-grandfather had a bigger boat and made more stops, and there has only ever been the one that mattered.”

“Nineteen years,” Sasha said.

“Longer than your grandmother. The rule of my family was that you do not ask.” He took the tiller again. “It used to be the rule of my family that you did.”

* * *

The wind came off the water and Sasha could not keep still on the stones. She walked the rectangle. The chalk lines had been gone over more than once, which meant somebody had kept coming back and laying them true. Under the north side, where the door was leaning, the ground had been dug and refilled and stamped hard.

“How long does it take,” she said, “to finish it?”

Odhiambo shrugged one shoulder, which took a moment, because of the door and the sacking.

“My father used to say the house is not built by the people who carry the most. He said it is built by the number of Thursdays. If the people stop, it stops. If the people keep coming, it is a house. There is a great deal of theory in that and very little arithmetic, but it has held a long while.”

Sasha turned and looked back down eleven miles of river at the town she had left at half past seven. The town was very small. The whole valley fitted into what she could see, and eleven miles did not look like enough to need four hours.

“Grandmother,” she called. “You are getting off here.”

Her grandmother finished the run of white on the string line, put the brush back in the tin, put the lid on, and looked up.

* * *

She came back on the Thursday, which was not unusual, and the ticket went into Sasha’s hand on the Sunday and stayed there four days, and nobody at the hospital would take it off her or look at it.

On the Thursday she got on alone, at twenty to seven, with the landing stage wet and the light coming off the water the same grey as in the photograph on the ticket office wall. Except that in the photograph the old man was third from the left in a boat with a girl of about ten in the bow, and Sasha saw that the girl in the photograph was a passenger too, and that this had been somebody’s first Thursday.

The engine started on the first pull. The river went east. The woman with the floorboards and the man with the door were not on it, and neither was the boy with the tap. It was only Sasha, and the ropes, and the diesel, and the slow water.

Four hours. Ten minutes of that was real.

The footings had a floor on them. Beyond the platform the frame had gone up: four rooms, a low wall at the front with two openings in it, studs like a row of bad teeth, and the string line gone and chalked and gone again so many times that the platform stone had a white edge. Somebody’s front door was fitted into the north wall, still in the old man’s colour. A sink stood on trestles inside, connected to nothing.

Sasha put her bag down in the middle of the floor and stood in the cold and looked at the four rooms.

Nobody could have said what had built it. It was not the river that had done it, and it was not any one person’s work. It was nineteen years of people turning up on a Thursday, and you could count the Thursdays in it as easily as you could count nails: a nail head every metre or so, all the way down, at slightly different heights, like the marks on a door frame that everybody’s father leaves.

On the platform, on a shelf under a bit of roofing felt, there was a passenger book in a tin.

Odhiambo waited while she opened it, holding his cap in both hands. “That is the whole business. Every sailing since my great-grandfather. Stamped, timed, signed.”

Two hundred years of Thursdays in a column so long Sasha did not want to count it, and the first pages not in English at all, only marks, weather symbols, and a drawing of a boat with the mast drawn twice.

She ran her finger down the last full page and stopped.

The last entry was not a Thursday.

14 MAY. RETURN, ARRIVED 16:40. One signature, her grandmother’s, and beside it in the firm hand of a man who had stamped things the same way for sixty years: OVERDUE, TUESDAY.

“Four hours late,” Sasha said.

“That was the first Tuesday I ever ran in two hundred years.” Odhiambo put his cap back on. “She was on the water when they telephoned me, and I was two hours out and there was nothing to do but go round, so I went round. I would have taken her on the Wednesday if she had let me. She said no, and she paid me, and being paid is worse.”

* * *

She got back on at ten to twelve and stamped her own card in the book, in the box under her grandmother’s name, because that was where the box was, and it took two tries to get a clean thumb.

Then the engine started on the first pull, and the four rooms went small behind her, and the river went east, and then, somewhere short of the willows, it did not.
