# The Winter Guard of the Glass Orchard

![Cover art: The Winter Guard of the Glass Orchard](../art/intermediate/26-the-winter-guard-of-the-glass-orchard.webp)

Ryn’s coat had six glass buttons, and she wrapped twine around each one before patrol. If a button fell, it would ring against the frozen ground and sound like a tiny warning bell. The orchard preferred quieter mornings.

Beyond the gate, three hundred glass trees stood in careful rows. Frost silvered every branch. Transparent pears hung among the leaves, each no larger than a plum. Inside them, pale lights drifted: a red mitten passed from hand to hand; a repaired waterwheel turned in summer rain; neighbors carried soup through snow; a child laughed until she hiccupped. The fruit held the memories of one season, offered by the people who lived it. A second season forced into the same fruit would burn it white and break it. A dropped fruit lost every memory inside.

Winter guard meant keeping the heat in the glasshouse, checking hairline cracks, and recording every loss. Ryn was fifteen and the last guard because the older ones had gone to the Crown’s river works. Every morning she walked the outer rows with a brass lantern, tapping each trunk and looking for a dark seam. Every evening she copied the day’s repairs into a narrow blue ledger. She wrote firmly, even when frost loosened her glove.

On the seventh morning, a horn sounded from the eastern ridge.

It was low and official. Ryn climbed the watch ladder and saw a black patrol craft sliding down the frozen canal on iron runners. Its hull was shaped like a folded beetle. Blue lamps flashed once, twice, then a red bar. Royal seal.

By the time the craft stopped beside the gate, Aunt Orla, sixty-three, was outside in her felt boots, carrying a ledger under one arm. Orla repaired orchard clocks and kept her sleeves pinned with copper wire. She stood with her shoulders squared and the ledger against her ribs like a shield.

Captain Maer, forty-one, climbed down first. She wore a black uniform trimmed in silver frost, and frost had already claimed one eyebrow. Four Crown wardens followed, carrying a brass machine shaped like a fruit press. Its many silver arms curled shut around nothing.

“Winter Guard Ryn,” Captain Maer said.

Ryn touched the gate but did not open it. “The orchard is closed.”

“The Crown has opened it.”

Ryn looked at the press. “By what law?”

“Emergency Requisition Nineteen. The drought engine has begun losing its instructions. Without stabilization, the water gates will disagree. Lower towns will receive flood while upper farms receive dust.”

Behind the captain, workers had begun securing the press to the sled.

Orla’s fingers tightened on the ledger. “You are taking the harvest.”

“We are securing it.” Maer’s voice stayed level. “The engine needs one full season of collective memory. Your orchard was built for this.”

Ryn knew the history. Before the drought, glass trees had stood in every district. Their fruit recorded shared work, so engineers could compare old repairs with new ones. The Crown’s engine had used those memories to coordinate water. After the drought began, the old harvests had dulled. Each season without rain weakened the network. The glass orchard’s last clear fruit shone like a small sun.

“If you force the fruit into that press, the memories burn out,” Ryn said.

“Then the engine gets them before they are lost.”

“And everyone in the valley loses the season we planted together.”

A message runner came from the west canal, sleeves white with frost. She stumbled from her horse and held up a red warning paddle.

“The West Tower lost its governor spring,” she called. “The main is opening.”

Maer’s eyes narrowed. “Then the engine is already failing.”

Ryn ran for the toolshed. Orla followed, faster than her stiff knees should have allowed. Behind them, the Crown press began to unfold.

The first loading arm punched through the orchard’s eastern gate. It ended in a claw shaped for gripping fruit trunks. A warden pulled a lever. The arm swept between the glass trees, and the fruit sang. Clear notes trembled from every pear as the machine’s silver field sounded each memory without taking it.

Ryn reached the pump house and turned its iron wheel. Heat poured into the buried pipes. Warm vapor hissed up through the soil, and frost crawled back from the nearest branches. But the loading arm kept moving. One pear trembled on its stem.

The arm struck again.

“No!” Ryn shouted. “The river remembers us; you do not own it.”

Captain Maer stepped into the row. She pulled a narrow sword from her belt but held it point-down, like a tool. “Guard, stand aside. You are obstructing emergency stabilization.”

“And you are burning history for a machine.”

“I am trying to prevent a water disaster.”

The west tower bell rang once, then again. Ice came loose along the canal. Water pushed up through a crack in the surface, glittering between the roots.

Orla stood beneath the watch ladder. She had opened the old gate ledger. “There is another way,” she called. “Not guaranteed.”

Maer looked up. “Explain.”

“The engine was built for witnesses, not cages. The first test was a whole district standing in the rain while strangers shared a drain. It remembered the promise because the people could withdraw it.” Orla lifted the ledger higher. “Your press turns witnesses into subjects.”

“That method failed during the Ash summer.”

“It failed because no one told people what they were promising. They felt watched.”

Ryn looked from the press to the orchard. The wards of three districts had gathered beyond the gate. Farmers in patched brown capes. Pump hands in blue coats. Carpenters with sawdust on their sleeves. A nurse holding a lantern for an old woman. They had come because the horn meant a Crown requisition, or perhaps because the West Tower bell shook the ice beneath their homes.

The loading arm hooked a branch. Ryn threw herself against it, but metal did not care how hard a fifteen-year-old pushed. The glass split behind her. Three pears struck the frozen ground, and three memories vanished with a sound too soft to hear.

Ryn shouted, “What promise do we make?”

Orla’s voice came from the ladder. “One promise the engine can hold. Not everyone must agree about the whole drought. They must agree to share this danger.”

Maer studied her. “A promise is not water.”

“No,” Ryn said. “But your machine doesn’t make water either. It only decides which hands move it.”

Ryn faced the people at the gate. “The engine needs honest memories. We can each say what work we will do before dusk. North gate, send two pump hands. West canal, open the low spillway only when the red paddle is raised. Mill district, clear the pump path. Others choose.”

Ryn pushed harder against the arm. “Then do not pretend we all want the same thing. Tell it how you will work when you want different things.”

Orla climbed onto the gate wall. “North district, are you willing to send pump hands?”

The north wardens hesitated. Then their captain stepped forward. “Two teams. Until the water is moving again.”

“West canal?”

Carpenter Nesa lifted her hand. “We will open the spillway at the third bell, and the mill district will clear it first.”

“Lower farms?”

A young woman named Iven spoke. Her red scarf hung over one shoulder. “We will share the first channel if the upper cisterns send half their stored rain.”

“That is not enough,” someone called.

“It is what we can promise,” Iven said.

One by one, districts chose work. Some promises were fair. Some were narrow. None covered every problem. The engine needed no magic words. It needed people to remain at the task after the choice stopped feeling noble.

Maer raised two fingers. The loading arm loosened.

Ryn dashed for the engine hall.

Maer brought the press to the slot. “Load one fruit. The fastest lawful response.”

Ryn shook her head. “Give us the engine.”

“We have promises.”

“If they fail, the flood takes Lower Stair Street first. Your orchard is uphill.”

Maer lowered her sword. “The press stays under Crown seal. But your engine may be tested. If it fails, I load the fruit.”

She placed both hands on the nearest engine ring. It was so cold the skin of her palms tightened. Orla opened the ledger to the first engine page, where the builders had recorded the Ash summer failure.

Ryn read the mistake aloud: “The district was told to share. It was not told what sharing required, or how to refuse. Trust became obedience. The engine shut down.”

Maer’s jaw tightened. “A useful lesson.”

“Not the whole one.” Ryn turned to the crowd. “We can refuse a promise. If someone cannot keep it, they must say so before the engine uses it. No punishment for an honest change.”

She pointed to the upper farms. “You promised half the cistern water. Keep it.”

Iven answered, “We can send the first siphon.”

Ryn placed her palm on the ledger. “The engine cannot use a promise made only to you. Each person must name what the whole town may ask of them.”

One by one, the workers spoke. Some offered two hours of labor. Some gave grain they could spare. Some promised access to tools, carts, or a roof. No one offered what they could not keep.

The engine rang.

The note began low, almost below sound. Copper circles climbed with amber light. Ryn felt it through the stone floor, through her boots, and into the bones of her hand. It did not choose one voice. It held many: the scrape of mill carts, the splash of a test siphon, Iven’s steady voice, the captain giving orders, Orla’s pen scratching corrections.

The engine had no verdict. It held a choice still in motion.

A gauge jumped. Then another. West Tower stopped rocking. The water gate began to turn at last.

But the flood had already gathered beyond the square. Ice pressed against the old mill wall, and the first channel opened crooked. Water struck the foundation hard enough to shake dust loose.

“Spillway blocked!” Nesa shouted.

Ryn saw a blue crate wedged between the stone teeth. A loading arm from the press had dropped it there before the machine stopped. One more obstruction, and the new water would have no path.

Maer saw it too. She ran for the gate, but ice slowed her boots. Ryn was closer.

Ryn crossed the engine platform, dropped to the mill race, and pushed through knee-deep water. The cold struck her legs like a wall. She grabbed the crate. It would not move.

Above her, the workers reached the lip. Orla held out a ledger strap. The north district’s pump hands pulled one end. Carpenter Nesa and Iven pulled the other. Maer cut through the crate’s frozen rope with her narrow sword.

The box broke free.

Water roared past Ryn, caught the open channel, and streamed west. Everyone hauled her up as the race swallowed the last of the crate. She lay on the stones, soaked and shaking, while the engine’s rings turned from amber to clear.

The flood did not vanish. It became water that people could use.

For the rest of the day, Ryn carried messages between the engine, the canal, and the orchard. The upper farms released half their cisterns. The lower farms gave grain. Pump hands stayed at the gates. The mill district repaired a wheel with salvaged boards. When the north team grew tired, another team arrived without being ordered.

“Your method is expensive,” Maer told Ryn. “It needs people to return when there is no fruit to show them.”

Ryn’s wet gloves squeaked as she closed the ledger. “So do water gates.”

The patrol craft departed before full dark, but Maer left two wardens to guard the sealed press, which remained in Ryn’s care.

Three bare sockets marked the broken limb where the press had struck. The lost fruit had held neighbors carrying one another through deep snow; now no one could say whose courage the lost memories had kept.

Ryn entered all three losses in the blue ledger. Then she added one more line: thirty-seven new guard apprentices to be trained.

The engine could hold for thirty days. The drought had not ended. Every morning, the district promises would need tending, and the Crown would ask what the valley remembered. Ryn would answer with work, disagreement, and the truth.

Behind her, the glass trees chimed in the cold. The sound was delicate and clear. It did not promise safety.

It promised that every safe return had to be made again.
