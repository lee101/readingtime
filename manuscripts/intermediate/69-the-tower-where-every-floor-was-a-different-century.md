# The Tower Where Every Floor Was a Different Century

![Cover art: The Tower Where Every Floor Was a Different Century](../art/intermediate/69-the-tower-where-every-floor-was-a-different-century.webp)

The moor had no road to the tower, which made the inspector’s van the most impressive thing Meriwether had seen all week.

“Listed structure,” said the man with the clipboard, before she had her bag off her shoulder. “Eighty feet of it, no stair, no foundations anybody can find, roofless, adverse survey in March. I’m closing it today.”

Eighty feet was wrong. The thing went up out of the heather and kept going, and the top twenty feet were the colour of a wet newspaper, and above that there was nothing at all, just the sky closing over it the way water closes over a stone.

She had walked out from the station at six, counting windows on the way in: four storeys’ worth, forty-one doors, and the last number she saw was 1911.

“Are you family?” he said.

“No.”

“Good.” He wrote something.

At the base, set into the door jamb, was a brass plate gone smooth with weather. IT IS PERMITTED TO GO UP ONE FLOOR FOR EVERY YEAR YOU CAN ACCOUNT FOR. NO STAIRS. THE TOWER KEEPS THE COUNT.

She read it three times and took it for a joke left for tourists. Then she thought about her own life, which was accounted for down to the week.

“I’ve been alive sixteen years,” she said out loud, to nobody. “I can account for all sixteen.”

The floor of the tower went down half an inch, and a door that had been a wall opened.

* * *

The first floor was a room and not a floor. Bare boards, a fire, a smell of peat and wet wool. A man on his knees mending a rope with a bone needle. A woman turning a hare on a spit. A boy of nine rolling a hoop along the wall and back again. Nobody looked at her.

A deal table with a plate, a bowl, a folded square of paper. A door-number cut deep into the frame: 1204.

“Twelve-oh-four,” she said, because it seemed to be the sort of thing the tower wanted.

There was another door, and she went through it.

A parlour with a harpsichord and a lady playing it with her eyes shut. Firelight, low, and a man asleep in a chair with a sword across his knees. Number 1318.

A kitchen with a copper pan the size of a wheel and two women arguing over it. Number 1402.

Each one was a household caught mid-sentence, and none of them so much as shifted a chair after she had looked.

By the sixth she had stopped feeling like a trespasser and started feeling like a woman in a museum, which was worse, because in a museum nobody expects you to be quiet.

“Tenth of August, sixteen fifty-one,” she said.

The wall refused.

Not locked. Nothing was locked anywhere in this building. The wall went on being a wall, with a door-number on the far side of it she could not reach, and in the middle of the floor there was a slab of grey stone with a slot cut in it, and the slot had the look of a place where something gets counted.

* * *

“That is not a structure,” said Oyelaran behind her. “That is a record.”

Meriwether turned round. He had come up, clipboard raised, writing, looking like a man in a church.

“Every floor is a household,” she said. “Every floor is somebody’s whole day. And every floor is stopped.”

“Stopped is a good word. Yes.” He came further in, carefully, the way you step onto ice you have not tested. “And there is a slot in your floor and a number in your floor and no stair in eighty feet of tower. Do you understand what a filing system looks like, Miss — ”

“Meriwether.”

“Of course.” He turned a page. “I have my own floor. I have had my own floor for twenty-two years, and I have never once been able to put it in a column, and I have concluded that I do not have one. Which is why I have a van and not a wife, and four years of letters to people about this building.”

“Is that a thing you are allowed to say to a stranger on a moor?”

“It is a thing I have never said to anybody,” said Oyelaran, “and you have been on the moor for two hours.”

In front of the slot was a low stone bench, worn pale in one place, and beside it a set-back in the wall the shape of a small ledger.

Meriwether sat down on the bench and opened it.

The first page said: HELD. Below it, a column of years, and against each year a mark. Beside 1911 the mark was a full stop. Beside most of the others it was a dash.

Four hundred years were listed. Thirty-nine had a full stop.

* * *

“So it files them,” she said. “One year at a time.”

“One year at a time, and it keeps the ones it is holding and files the rest down somewhere it will not let you go.” Oyelaran had gone the colour of the wallpaper. “Miss Meriwether, if that is what that is, then that thing is not a building. It is a cabinet with thirty-nine drawers in it, and I am required by the county to make the building safe.”

“It is safe. It has been safe for eight hundred years. Nobody has fallen out of it.”

“Nobody has gone up it,” said Oyelaran. “I have the register. Eleven people since the county took records. In 1908 a schoolmaster got to the nineteenth floor and came down in an afternoon in what he described as a temper. Nothing since.”

He turned the clipboard round. The last entry was three weeks old, in his neat hand: 1911 — OCCUPIED? — OCCUPIED? — OCCUPIED?

“Occupied,” said Meriwether.

“That is what I have written every three months for nine years, and I have no way of confirming it, and if I stop writing it then the answer stops existing.” He took the clipboard back. “I am not closing the tower because I want it closed. I am closing it because a hundred and forty-one people’s names are in that file and I cannot get to any of them.”

“Then we go up.”

“You cannot go up. That is rather the point of you, Miss Meriwether.” He nodded at the wall. “You have been able to say every year of your life and now you cannot say the next one. Why not.”

She stood in front of the slot with the ledger open in her hands, and she looked for the year, the way you look for a name in a crowd.

* * *

The forty-first floor is reached by not being stopped.

That was how she got there, and she was on her knees for the last of it. The wall kept declining to refuse her. She said the years out loud as she came up them, and every time it opened, and by the thirtieth she was crying with the effort and did not notice.

The forty-first floor was a kitchen.

Gaslight, though the gas had been off in this country since 1911. A range with a kettle on it, hot. A table with two chairs and one laid. A dresser of blue and white china. A window with a rail line across the fields, which stopped in the field, which was in 1911.

There was a man at the table with his hands round a cup, and he stood up when she came in, and he bowed, which no one in this tower had done.

“I heard you,” he said. “I hear everyone. It is very quiet and you were not quiet.”

He was somewhere past fifty and had been handsome, once, a long time ago, and he had a boy’s habit of standing very straight and a man’s hands.

“How long have you been here?” Meriwether said.

“A hundred and fourteen years,” said the man. “1911. I came up on a Tuesday with a ticket and a bag and I have not been able to go down since, and I am sorry about the state of the tin, I never got the paint out of the crevice, it was a tin of — ”

He stopped.

“You have a year in here,” he said, “and it is the only one you have never said.”

“Yes.”

“Say it, then. It is the only door in here.”

“It is not easy.”

“No,” he said. “That is how you know it is yours.”

Meriwether stood in a kitchen on top of a tower in a year that had ended, and found the place in herself where she had put the year: under the bed, in a shoebox, wrapped in a tea towel, the way you put a thing you mean to deal with.

“Twenty-two of April, nineteen sixty-one,” she said. “My mother went to sea. I was eleven. I have accounted for every year of my life since I was eleven to the week, including the weeks of her letters, and I came out here on purpose, and I have never once said what I came for.”

The man put down his cup.

“Nineteen sixty-one,” he said. “I have held this floor since nineteen eleven and there has been nobody on it to say. A hundred and fourteen years, and I have known somebody was in there the entire time, and I could not ask, because asking is a floor, and I had nothing to spend.”

* * *

She could go up. There was no forty-second floor. There was a ladder in the chimney, and above the ladder nothing.

“You’re not going up,” said the man. “Nobody has ever gone up. That is not the way out, that is only the top.”

“I know.”

“Then it is down.” He said it without any weight at all, the way you say a thing you have had a hundred and fourteen years to get right. “Go down, Miss Meriwether. Go down to the floor with your year in it. That is where the counting is done. You cannot be counted on the way up; you are counted where it happened. After that you may go up or down or out for the rest of your life, and it will not ask you anything again.”

“You are giving that away.”

“I have been sitting on it since Tuesday,” he said, “and it has never once earned its keep.”

Oyelaran met her on the twenty-second floor, on his knees in a doorway, with the register open on the floor beside him.

“Miss Meriwether — I have heard you. I hear everyone.” His hand was shaking. “Nine years of OCCUPIED, OCCUPIED, OCCUPIED, and I need to know what he is doing.”

“He’s boiling a kettle,” she said. “He’s been boiling a kettle since Tuesday.”

She went down.

* * *

The floor with 1961 in it was not what she expected, and she could not have described it afterwards, except that it was small, and there was a hall, and a man on a step with a case, and a child on the pavement with a book under her arm being told that her mother was not going to be there at four o’clock.

She stayed four days.

On the first day she did not leave the hall. On the second she followed the woman to the corner shop and carried the bag and nobody said anything about it. On the third she was let in, and there was a bad chair and a wireless, and a woman who was sorry, at length, in the way people are sorry when they are also defending themselves.

On the fourth day, at ten past four, her mother came up the path with her hat in her hand, twenty-one years younger, calling her name from the gate in a voice Meriwether had carried in a shoebox under a bed since she was eleven.

“Say it out loud,” said Oyelaran. He was on the step outside with the register, having come down the whole tower to stand in the yard and wait. “I am required to record the moment. It is the only entry I have ever been able to fill in.”

“Nineteen sixty-one,” said Meriwether. “The twenty-second of April. And I have accounted for it, and you can put the full stop in.”

Oyelaran wrote the full stop. Then he turned the register round, looked at the thirty-eight full stops above it, and took out a different pen.

“I’m reclassifying it,” he said. “Not a structure. A monument. Schedule two, public access, and a man to live in it.” He wrote it on the form, and signed it, and did not look up. “I have a flat and a cat and eleven years of letters. I would like to learn how it counts, if anybody is teaching.”

* * *

She came out of the tower on the fifth day, four days after she went down into it, and she was fifteen.

She checked it twice in the first week, in shops, in the corner of the mirror in the bathroom, in the wording of a postcard she wrote and did not send. Nineteen sixty-one had been a gap. Now it was a floor, and everything above it had somewhere to go.

Oyelaran did not leave. He sent for a chair, a mattress, and a kettle of his own, and put the first of his notes in the register under the full stop: HELD 1911. OCCUPIED. There is a man. He is all right. He has company Tuesdays.

He is up to nine floors by the spring, which is nine years and not forty-one, and the tower lets him go at that. The last page of the register, in his neat hand, says: count correctly, and it will open. She is the proof. She is the counter.
