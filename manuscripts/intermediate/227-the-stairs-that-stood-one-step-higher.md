# The Stairs That Stood One Step Higher

![Cover art: The Stairs That Stood One Step Higher](../art/intermediate/227-the-stairs-that-stood-one-step-higher.webp)

The toll-house at the Marrow Gate was older than the town it taxed.

It had one room, a fire that never quite drew, and a stair in the corner going up to the room where the books had been kept since before anybody alive was born. Nine steps, worn hollow down the middle. An oak step-block stood beside the bottom one for anybody who came up heavy.

The reeve of Marrowby climbed that stair on market day. He had done it every market day for nineteen years, and he was Oswin Grale, and the whole town had watched him do it so long that nobody mentioned it any more.

Sella Roke kept the toll-book. Her father Fenn wrote in it still, but his eyes had gone soft at the edges, so he put the sums to her and did the totals himself at night by candle.

Most of the book was tolls. A cart of oats. A barrow of salt. A load of wool. Beside each one was set down how high the load stood above the sill, because a high load wanted more room under the gate and paid for it. In the front of the book, in a hand two hundred years old, were the rules. Loads under the mark paid nothing. A widow, or a very old person, who could not raise her own load to the mark paid nothing, and was written down as often as she came.

* * *

The mark was a cut notch on the jamb of the toll-house door, and it had been re-cut so many times in so many centuries that the jamb was a staircase of old cuts, each one above the last, the wood raised and planed down and raised again until the grain of it ran in ledges.

“Who cuts it?” Sella asked her father.

“Ord cuts it. Sim Ord, carpenter. He comes before the market and he cuts the mark.”

“To what?”

“To the doorway.”

“That is not an answer.”

* * *

The mason who had built the stair came to look at it that spring, because the top tread had gone soft and nobody wanted to be the one to say so first. He was Doff Pike and he was eighty-one. He went up with a string and a plumb, and said the stair was sound and would outlast the road.

“Will it not need mending?” Sella said.

“No.”

“But it is said to do something.”

Doff Pike looked at her for a while. “I will tell you the whole of it once,” he said, “so write it down, because I will not tell you again. This stair gives one step more going up than it takes. Whoever climbs it comes off the stair one step higher than they went on, and everybody who sees him sees him higher. Coming down, it does the same. That is all of it.”

“Does it work on anybody?”

“It has never asked who.”

“Then it is in the stair.”

“No,” he said. “It is in the climber. It is not in the wood and it is not in the stone, and I cannot keep it out of one and put it in the other. When the town stops seeing it, it is only a stair.”

Then he said he would go and find his coat, and he went home, and he never said a word about it to anybody again.

* * *

Sella began with the tolls, because that was her work and because the sums had to be done by Friday.

A load paid nothing if it stood below the mark. She went back through the year and found eleven loads that had paid nothing in the last market week and would have paid a penny apiece the year before. She went back two years and found a hundred and six.

The notice on the gate had been repainted three times in nineteen years, each time a little higher up the post. Two pence the load, the reeve’s shoulder high. The last one was at the height of Grale’s shoulder, which was higher than the last, which was higher than the one before.

Then she went back to the widow.

Widow Hask brought one bushel of barley to Marrowby every market day for eleven years. In the book, in Fenn’s writing, every line said the same thing. Hask, one bushel, nothing, cannot lift to the mark.

Then, on the fourth Friday of the sixth month, the line said: Hask, one bushel, two pence.

Sella took the book to her father. “She can lift to the mark.”

“She can lift to the mark,” Fenn said.

“She could not lift to it in March.”

“No,” he said. “The mark is higher than it was in March. Somebody cut it.”

* * *

The boy was her brother and he was eight. His name was Tam and he could not reach the shelf.

Their mother had hung the shelf the week Tam was born, low down, where a child could get at it. Every year since, somebody in that house had put it back up. He will want it higher when he is a step taller, they said. So it went up a hand’s width every year, and Tam got a year older and no nearer to it, until at eight he had two blocks of ash under his feet to stand on and the shelf still above his head.

The blocks were the same fallen ash as the step-block by the stair.

“Do not stand on those in the dark,” their mother said. “You will have your toes off.”

* * *

The reeve’s daughter was Nell and she was six, and she came to the toll-house on market day to meet her father at the top. Her brother Roan was nine and came up as well. They stood on the boxes that Ord kept under the window, three for Roan and two for Nell, so that when he came down they were level with him.

That had gone on for some years. In the first year it had been one box each. Nobody in that house ever said the boxes were the point of the stairs.

* * *

Sella got the ash bar down in the last week of the month.

It was set into the wall of the toll-house at knee height, a bar of ash a foot wide with an iron foot and a brass nail in the top. The first reeve of Marrowby had put it there to measure anything that had to be measured true: a load, a cart, a doorway, a man. The bar did not care who was standing in the room.

She measured the reeve with it on a Friday. She measured him before he went up and after he came down, and she wrote both figures in the book, in the proper column, with the date beside them.

Up, five foot four and three quarter. Down, five foot seven and one eighth.

“That is two and three eighths of an inch,” she said.

“I do not doubt it,” said the reeve.

“You doubt what it is worth.”

He put his hand flat on the door frame. “I have been the reeve of Marrowby for nineteen years, and the market knows me by where I stand. You put a number in that book that says I am the same height as a man with a rod, and that is what I shall be. Think what you are doing.”

“I am writing down what Ord’s chisel does,” Sella said.

* * *

So she did it the way the town did things, which is slowly, in ink, with witnesses.

First she stopped the notch. She told Ord the mark was not to be cut on market day. Ord said the mark was his business and the reeve’s. She said the mark was a line in a book and the book was Fenn’s, and Fenn was in the room, and Fenn agreed with her. Ord looked at the door a long while, and then he went home and did not cut it.

The mark stayed where it was. It stayed where it was the next week, and the week after, and that was the whole of the first thing done.

Second she went through the year with a knife. Every entry that had been measured to the reeve was measured again to the bar. She wrote the old figure beside the new one and rubbed neither out. There were two hundred and forty-one such entries. It took her nine days.

Third she read it out.

She stood at the toll-house door on market day with the bar in her hand and she gave the figures, entry by entry, in the flat voice she used for sums. Anybody who liked could lay a thumb on the brass nail and look for himself. Two carters did. Widow Hask did.

Grale listened to the whole of it.

“Put it in the book,” he said.

“It is in the book. I read it out of the book.”

“Then take it out.”

“No,” Sella said. “You signed the rules in the front, and I write the mark in this book. If the book has something in it you do not like, then it is your book, and I am only the hand on the pen.”

* * *

The surplus went down the way water goes down a sink.

On the second Friday it was two and three eighths of an inch, and Nell could still stand level with her father on her two boxes.

On the ninth Friday it was one and five eighths, and Nell did not need the boxes and did not climb them any more. Roan said nothing about that.

On the sixteenth Friday it was three quarters of an inch, which was two fingers laid flat, and there was a small crowd at the nail.

On the twenty-third Friday Grale came down the stair and stood against the wall and the top of his head was level with his own chalk mark from the year his father died, which was thirty-one years back.

After that the book said the same as the bar. The reeve’s height was five foot four and three quarter on every market day, going up and coming down, and there was no more difference in it than there is in a bucket.

* * *

The tolls came down in the same month, which is the part that convinced the town when the stair had not.

Two hundred and forty-one loads were re-measured at the bar. Fifty-eight of them stood under the mark again and paid nothing. Widow Hask’s line went back to where it had been for eleven years, and stayed there.

“The stone and I,” she said at the door, “have been having this argument since April, and the stone has won.”

* * *

The reeve climbed the stair once more before the frost, to bring the old books down, because the clerk had said the top room wanted clearing and he had asked for it himself.

He went up, and he came down, and he stood at the foot of it with his hand on the newel post.

“Nineteen years,” he said. “I have never once been the same height twice.”

Hask laughed at him. “That is not what happened to you. Nobody ever was. We all just went on saying so, and then we built for it, and you are the only man here who never worked out what it cost.”

Grale did not answer her.

The boxes went in under the stair, and the step-block went with them, and Tam’s shelf came down in the spring to a height a boy of eight can reach, and he put his own cup on it without standing on anything.
