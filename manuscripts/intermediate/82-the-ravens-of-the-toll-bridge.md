# The Ravens of the Toll Bridge

![Cover art: The Ravens of the Toll Bridge](../art/intermediate/82-the-ravens-of-the-toll-bridge.webp)

The toll at Halstow bridge was fourpence, and the ravens took it.

Not one raven. Four, five, six of them, off the coping of the toll house and the ridge of the far arch, coming down onto the slab where you put your money and taking it and going back up, and the queue went forward, and the water went under four arches in the dark.

Tarek had been in the queue half an hour with his hands shut in his coat, and at the front of it he took out what he had, which was nothing.

Keeper Bray looked down at him. He had a broad flat face, a greatcoat three sizes too large, and a cashbox under his arm with a slot in the top.

“Fourpence.”

“I have got nothing.”

“That is a matter for you.”

“I had it on Thursday. It went on the back of the stall for salt and the cart took it.”

“Then that is a matter for you as well.” Bray tipped his head at the slab. “Go on, then. They take it out of the air, that lot. Half of them say the same, and you would be astonished what a coincidence it is after eleven years.”

He did not have a farthing. He did not have a button. He had fourpence worth of nothing, and the far arch had water in it, and the whole town was on the far side of the bridge watching a boy try to pay two hundred years of a fiction with his hands.

* * *

He tried a fish, a small roach his sister had given him, and the birds came down and looked at it and left it, and he put a stone, and the same, and he put his knife, which was the bad end of a knife.

“The charter says coin. This is a toll bridge. The toll is fourpence taken in coin of the realm, and I have had this post eleven years and never once taken anything else, and if I start taking fish this winter I shall be off the bridge by Christmas.”

“Who says it has to be coin?”

“The charter says it. Fourpence in coin, and here I am, and here you are not.” Bray shifted the cashbox. “And now you know what everybody knows. You have to have the fourpence, and there is no other way on, and I cannot help you.”

Then he said it, because it was the only thing he had, and he had been carrying it for two years.

“I am sorry about Tomax and the mill leat.”

Bray stopped.

“I am sorry about the boy in the mill leat. I am sorry to whoever the rookery belongs to. I was nine and I was in bed with a fever and I was not there, and I have been saying so for two years, and I would like it over.”

The rookery at Halstow is on the church tower, a hundred and forty steps up, with no key to it since 1809, and you can stand on the head of St Aldate and look straight into it.

Something came off the coping of the toll house and went up, and did not flap.

She went up the way a crow goes up when it is not in a hurry. She was the colour of a wet slate roof and the size of a lurcher, and when she came down onto the toll house she folded her wings in three stages, like somebody putting up a ladder.

She came down beside Tarek’s little roach, and put her head on one side, and looked at him for a long time with both eyes, which is the thing ravens cannot do.

Then she stepped over the fish and the knife, went to the middle of the slab, sat down on the fourpence, and did not move.

Bray made a noise. It was the noise of a man who has just been shown something in his own working area that he had arranged his working area so as not to look at.

“Madam,” he said. “I have never seen you take a coin.”

“No,” Tarek said. “Nor have I, and I have watched every one since I was six.”

* * *

Bray took the charter out of the tin box under his stool and unrolled it on the slab between them, and it was soft as a cloth and twice as long as the slab.

“Read it with me,” he said.

The rain had got into it. The seal of 1608 was the colour of a bottle.

“…and it is ordained and granted that a toll of fourpence the piece shall be taken and paid for every passage on foot or otherwise across the said bridge, in coin of the realm, by such toll-keeper as the said company shall appoint.”

Bray got to the end and turned the charter over. The back was blank except for a wet thumbprint from about 1611.

“Fourpence in coin. A keeper appointed by the company. Not a bird. Not one word about a bird, and it has been read out on this slab every Michaelmas by four men in a row of wigs, and no two of them ever said a bird.”

“Your turn,” Tarek said.

“The company’s, and the company is two chaps in Wessex with a book, and the book says the ravens are the attraction.” Bray re-rolled the charter. “Six hundred a year, near enough, and the freight is what the barges are charged. The ravens are not in the charter. The ravens are why people say they came over the Halstow bridge instead of saying they came over a bridge, like a person with a cart.”

“So the ravens are the business.”

“The ravens are the sign over the door,” Bray said. “And I am the door.”

He said it without any weight at all, and then got the broom and swept the slab, and Tarek crossed on his own two feet with nothing in his hand, and the ravens went up over the four arches, and none of them was a raven, and none of them had been for two hundred years.

* * *

Tarek went up the hundred and forty steps the next morning with a coil of rope and a candle, because the tower door had been off its hinge since before he was born and the council had never repaired it.

The matriarch was in the rookery on the head of St Aldate, and she did not move, and she made no sound the whole time he was there. Her feet were wrong for a bird: the joint goes the wrong way, and it goes the wrong way on purpose, because a bird that can hold a bridge coping cannot climb a spire. She was the size of a dog, and she weighed more than a dog, because the rookery floor has a hollow worn in the stone in the shape of her, and it was worn there before anybody living was born.

In the nest, under the twigs, there was a tin box, and in the tin box there were two hundred years of receipts, in copper, gone green, and the oldest of them was a farthing and the newest of them was for sixpence, and somebody had kept every single one.

Tarek stood in the rookery and looked at the receipts and then at the bird on the head of St Aldate, and worked out the rest of it, and it fitted together the way a bad lock does.

Below him the town woke up, and the bridge was there, and it was only a bridge, and the toll house was there, and the toll house was only a hut, and nobody else was going to work that out, because looking at the raven was what everybody had been paid to do for two hundred years.

* * *

The Halstow Bridge Trust met in the back room of the Bell on the Thursday, under a glass model of the bridge that had no ravens on it.

Swann of the company read out the resolution, and Mrs. Ivens, who was sixty-four and had crossed that bridge as a bride in a snowstorm, made them read it again.

“The toll is discontinued,” Swann read. “The bridge is free from the twelfth of May. The keeper’s post is discharged.”

Bray sat at the back with his hat on his knee, and when he spoke his voice had gone rough.

“Eleven years. I have stood on that slab in every wind there is. My roof came off in January and I have not put it back. You have taken my house and my post and my eleven years in one sentence, and which part of it have you costed.”

Nobody had costed it. Mrs. Ivens looked at the model under the glass for a while.

“Then we will find something for you. The bridge still has to be swept and the chain has to be kept, and it wants a man on it who is known. What will you take in place of the toll.”

“I have a wife who can gut a fish,” Bray said.

“Fish, then.”

“Fish,” said Bray, and put his hat back on.

The vote went eleven for and two against, and the two against were a man who sold ginger-beer on a Sunday and his brother-in-law, and somebody asked the brother-in-law what he thought the arch was for.

* * *

The toll house came down in June. They took the slab out first, and the fourpence line went with it, and the water under the arches went on doing what it had been doing since 1608.

The bell came out of the vestry of St Aldate, a ship’s bell, and it had been one since the Mary of Bristol went onto the Berryhead rocks in 1789, and the vicar said the bird on the roof had been in the tower since before the Mary went down.

They put the bell on the slab and the matriarch on the gatepost, and everybody stood about in the rain waiting to see what she would do with it, and she did nothing for a while, because she is not a raven and ravens do not perform.

Then a bargeman came up off the towpath and said he had lied about carrying timber, and she got a wing over the bell and rang it, and the sound went out flat across the water.

She has rung it, by the reckoning of the toll house chimney tally, two hundred and forty times since June. Once for the man who told the truth about the timber. Once for the girl who said the bell was hers. Once for a boy who stood on the slab and said he was sorry about the mill leat to a bird, and was not a man who was owed anything and had come to pay.

Bray stays on the bridge. There is no toll and no turnstile, but there is a broom, and a chain, and a bench under the toll house eaves with his name cut into the end of it, and his wages come in fish, and he is at the north end of the bridge most days, and he is happy.

And the matriarch rings for people being honest, which turns out to be a great deal more often than anybody expected. On the evening of the eleventh of May the town put a slate over the toll house door with the date on it, and she stood on the gatepost with the bell under her wing, and she said, in her own voice, which nobody on the ground had heard before, that she had been a very good bird for two hundred years, and would like it noted that she was a very good bird, and would like to be paid in something.
