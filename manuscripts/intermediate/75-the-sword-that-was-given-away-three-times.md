# The Sword That Was Given Away Three Times

![Cover art: The Sword That Was Given Away Three Times](../art/intermediate/75-the-sword-that-was-given-away-three-times.webp)

The museum was free on Saturdays, which gave a boy of twelve six hours in it, and the only part that changed was the light off the front windows.

He did the dinosaur first, because it was a fake, a plaster cast with a whale’s jaw hanging off it, and he had read the card saying so eleven times.

The sword was at the back, in the good room, in a case with a rope round the outside of it for reasons nobody could explain.

GIVEN, NOT AWARDED. This sword has been surrendered three times in its recorded history. Each surrender was voluntary. In every case the recipient had not asked for it and had not expected it. In every case the recipient was afterwards defeated. The Museum does not endorse the local account that the object is cursed. A curator’s note, filed 2015, is available on request. In eleven years nobody has requested it.

The card was in the curator’s writing, a small upright hand that used commas the way other men use full stops.

Rafe read it standing up, twice, and then found the curator in his office eating a sandwich out of the wrapper.

“Is the curse real?”

“No,” said Mr Ferrier.

“Then what is it?”

“That is in the note.”

“Then give me the note.”

Ferrier looked at the sandwich. “You are twelve.”

“I am nearly thirteen, and there is nothing in that note you cannot say out loud in front of my mother.”

* * *

The note was four typed pages, and the first three were about metallurgy.

The fourth said this: The blade is Netherlandish, about 1610. The hilt is a nineteenth-century replacement, and the replacement is the only part of the object anybody has ever cleaned. Of the three surrenders, 1911 is well documented, 1948 rests on one newspaper cutting, and 1979 on the donor’s own account, which is unreliable in the way that only true things are unreliable.

Then: The Museum’s view is that the object is not attached to whoever holds it. In one hundred and fifteen years of records, no holder has won anything. The Museum’s view is that the object rewards the surrender, and not the recipient.

The last line said: We are aware that this is unkind. We are also aware that nobody has put it to the test, and that we are not going to.

“That is on the wall,” said Rafe. “In the good room. Anybody can read it.”

“It is in a case that costs eleven hundred pounds a year to light, and nobody reads it, which is the whole difference between a museum and a museum that wins things.” Ferrier threw the wrapper in the bin.

* * *

Ambrose Ilderton was one hundred and twenty-one and had been mayor of Bell since 1974, thirty years longer than the charter allowed, because nobody wanted the job. He had lost eleven elections since 1911 and was standing for a twelfth in the spring.

He also bought things. The museum had a room called the Mayor’s Room, neither a gift nor a bequest, holding four hundred and six objects bought by Ambrose Ilderton for a town that had asked for none of them: a tractor, a fountain, a rowing boat, a chimney cowl, three urns, a refrigerated lorry.

Rafe found him in the middle of it with a catalogue binder under his arm.

“Mr Ilderton. You gave this sword away.”

The old man’s hand went flat on the binder. “In nineteen eleven.”

“To a farm hand. In the tap room of the Bell.”

“In the tap room of the Bell, with no ceremony and one dog for a witness. I was nineteen, it had been given to me by a man going to England, and I did not want it. I gave it to Tace Havers because he had asked me for a greatcoat that week and I had not got one to give him.”

“So you lost.”

“For the rest of my life. Nobody would stand against me until nineteen seventy-four, and then they all did, and I have been losing ever since.” He laughed, and it came out in two pieces. “I do not give things away, boy. Not in a hundred and fifteen years. I buy. Look at the room. I buy because it is the opposite.”

* * *

Mrs Ninian Boyd was ninety-five and had a fireguard she did not need.

“I gave it to Robert Sands, and he was the man I had spent four months telling the county was a fraud.”

“What did you expect?”

“I expected it to make him good at it. You give a man a thing and it makes him good at the thing he wanted.” She turned her cup round. “He won the by-election by eleven votes and I lost by nine hundred, and he was unseated in four years, and I have had forty years to think about eleven votes.”

On the twenty-eighth Rafe was in the reserve room with the 1911 ledger open on the settle.

The sword came in by gift in 1979, so the earliest record was the museum’s own: a typed sheet, a photograph of the case, a note in a third hand. It said the council had held the object since 1911, and that in 1912 Tace Havers had sold his herd and the top field to a buyer in Lupton.

Lupton was Ilderton’s cousin. Ilderton was twenty and had given a sword away in a pub.

The 1979 book was where Mr Sowerby had filled the form in himself. Asked what he had received in return, he had written: nothing. Asked why, he had written: I could not keep it in a shop. His bakery went under that autumn, and he came to every village meeting for twenty years afterward saying the same sentence, which was that he should have kept it. He was seventy-one now, and alive, and still said it at the harvest supper.

* * *

The part that mattered was the part nobody had put in a case.

Rafe stood in front of the sword and read the card for the fourth time in a month, and it came apart under him. Every holder lost. Every recipient lost. Three givers, three losses. Nine losers, no winners, and the object had sat in a glass box for forty-seven years doing nothing.

Unless it had never been in the box. Unless the box was where it went to be harmless.

A sword that gave victory to whoever held it would have made a spectacular set of winners out of three very unlucky people. A sword that gave victory to whoever handed it over explained every fact on the card, including the curse, which was the story the town told itself to avoid admitting that three people had passed a weapon round like a collection plate.

“Mr Ferrier,” he said, through the glass. “How long have you known?”

“Eleven years. Since the January I took this job, which I took for the desk and not the collection. I read the 1911 book in the first week, and I have read it every January since.”

“Then why did you not say it?”

“Because the day I say it, the sword’s next move is mine. I have signed every form in this building. The insurance is mine. The town is mine to hand a thing like that to, and I am not a brave man, and I would rather be a coward with a locked case than a brave man with a procession.” He looked at the blade. “I am also fifty-eight, with a mortgage and a housekeeper, and I liked it here.”

* * *

The sword came out of the case on the first Saturday of March for a bolt-clean, on a padded trolley under a grey blanket. The county man was due to take it away at four.

Rafe was in the reserve room at three with a folded coat over his arm.

Ferrier stood in the doorway with his keys in his hand and did not come any closer.

“You have decided something.”

“The card says nobody has put it to the test and that the museum is not going to. I am putting it to the test. Open the case, or I pull the blanket off and do it in front of the county man, and then it is a theft, and it is in the papers, and the paper will have it in a day.”

“That is blackmail, and you are twelve.”

“Yes,” said Rafe.

Ferrier came and unlocked it. He had had the key for eleven years and used it in about nine seconds, and then put it back in his pocket and said, “Not to the papers, and not to my house.”

“Who, then.”

“Whoever you have already chosen.”

“There is nobody,” said Rafe. “That is the other half of it. Pick somebody and it is a gift and you have asked, and if somebody asks, it is a swap, and it was never a swap. Put it down where anybody can have it, walk off, do not watch, and nobody has asked.”

Ferrier looked at him for a while. “That is a very good answer and I have never once thought of it.”

* * *

The market was on the square on Saturdays, the museum was up the square, and the county man’s van was parked at the wrong end of it.

Ferrier had taken the trolley out to photograph the sword against the new parish sign for the magazine, had been turned around by a man selling cheese, and the sword ended up at the market under a blanket on a trestle table between the bread and a raffle.

Rafe pulled the blanket off. The rain had got into the market and the townspeople stood looking at a piece of steel that had cost three lives and won none of them. Somebody said it was a bit rusty. Somebody’s father said it had been in that case when he was a lad.

Rafe put the coat on the table, and then the sword on top of the coat, and stepped back.

“It is given,” he said. “Not sold and not lent. It goes to whoever picks it up. I am not choosing, and none of you asked, and I would rather you did not say thank you, because thank you is the half that breaks it.”

Then he walked up the square with his hands in his pockets and did not look back, and behind him a baker called Hobbes said somebody had better not let it get rained on, and put it under his arm.

* * *

Four years later the town won the county cup final by one point, at ninety-one minutes, off a goal it had been awarded three in the first half and had not deserved one of.

Ferrier stood on the touchline and wept, in front of the parish, and the local paper photographed it, could not work out why, and printed it anyway.

The mayor resigned in the spring, in a letter of one sentence, having lost his twelfth election and held the office fifty-two years that the charter had not allowed. The new mayor put the Bell back the way it was, including the fountain, which came back at the council’s expense in October.

Rafe was sixteen and bored. He came into the museum on a Saturday and asked where the sword was, and was told it was in a bread bin in Bell Lane, under a coat, in a house where nobody would discuss it.

“It wins,” said the new curator. “That is what they say about it, and there is a woman in Bell Lane who has not won a thing in her life.”

“It wins the person who gives it.”

“Does it,” said the new curator.

And in Bell, in the Bell electorate, and afterwards over the counter of the Bell and for the better part of a decade, everybody insisted that it was the football.
