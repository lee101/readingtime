# The Hearth at the End of the World

![Cover art: The Hearth at the End of the World](../art/intermediate/76-the-hearth-at-the-end-of-the-world.webp)

The first thing was the knife, and the knife had to be sharp, because a blunt knife turns a billet into kindling and you can hear the difference in the voice of the fire.

Six o’clock, in the dark, in the fourth week of winter. Bridie knelt on the hearthstone in a wool skirt and a jersey three sizes too big for her, and the whole village of Ormsby was at four below and asleep above her, and she had nine minutes left of the round.

Open the draw. Rake the ash back to the back wall, where it would bank the fire all night. Split the billet, and split it the same way she had split it since she was six years old and could reach the handle, which was down the grain, because cross grain sends a rocket up the flue and the schoolmistress sleeps directly above the hearth-house.

The levy was a separate job and it came round on the first Monday of the month, when she went down the stepped street with a book and a basket. Nineteen households in kind: kindling, a sack of coal, a day’s labour if a man was idle, a hen if a woman was having a bad winter. Everyone else a penny, and the penny had not been raised since 1974. What actually went into the fire came out of the woodshed behind the hearth-house, out of her father’s own stack, because the kindling had stopped coming in 1974 as well. Bridie had worked that out at thirteen and had told nobody, and at fifteen she thought about it every morning at six.

That was the round. Twelve minutes, and the fire had been lit before the road, and before the houses, and by all accounts before the village, which Bridie had found funny at eleven and irritating at fifteen.

“Nine minutes,” said her father from the doorway, not looking at his watch, because he did not wear a watch and never had.

“There is a cat in the ash.”

“There is always a cat in the ash. Move her.”

Bridie moved the cat to the far corner with two fingers and finished the round at four minutes to six, and the fire took, and the village went on being warm behind her back.

* * *

The stranger came in on the Thursday of the fourth week, on foot, from the north, where there is nothing.

She was about forty and she had no coat, and her eyebrows were white with frost, and her boots had been bound round with fencing wire to keep the soles together. She stood at the top of the stepped street in the falling snow and said she wanted to sit by the fire, and Turl said the fire was a private hearth, and the woman said she had walked eleven days and would stand in the yard all night if she had to, and Turl, who was a decent man in his way, took the bucket off the door.

She said her name was Merrill, and then said it again, because he had not heard it the first time, which told Bridie something about the road. Her pack weighed nothing, and she had not eaten since Tuesday, and Turl put a plate of bread and beef in front of her and did not ask her name a third time.

She sat on the bench inside the hearth-house and did not speak for an hour and a quarter. She warmed one hand, then the other, and looked at the fire the way people look at a thing they have been told about.

Then she said, “What is it for?”

Bridie was fifteen, and she had done the round every morning for nine years, and she put the fire-rake down on the flagstones.

“What?”

“The fire.” The woman turned her head. “I have come eleven days to ask one question and it is that one, and I have got nothing else.”

* * *

Nobody in Ormsby could answer her.

The reeve, Hains, said it was the custom. The smith, Collier, said fire was for cooking, heating and the softening of metal, and that this fire did none of those things and had never done any of them, and that he had complained to the council in 1988. Miss Tranter said it was educational, because two hundred years of children had learned to be frightened of fire in that room, and there was nothing else in Ormsby to be frightened of. Old Jem, who was ninety-one, said it was for the dead.

“The dead?”

“There is always one,” said Jem. “Four hundred years, one a generation, and the firekeeper is meant to say a word over the basket. Your grandmother did. I only ever did it when the basket was full.”

Collier said that a village with a fire in it was a village with a hearth in it, and a village without one was a row of houses with a bus stop, and nobody disagreed with him either, which was the whole difficulty.

“That is not what it is for,” said the woman.

That was the problem with Ormsby, Bridie thought, watching twenty people find out they had been guarding a different thing all their lives. In a place with one industry, everybody was an expert. Nobody was ever wrong. You could not get a straight answer out of a village like that with a warrant.

The woman came to the hearth-house twice more and sat by the fire and asked, and the answers got worse, and on the second night she asked Bridie’s father whether anybody had ever looked the fire up.

“Looked it up where?”

“In writing.”

Turl stared at the fire for a long time. “There is a book,” he said.

* * *

The book was in the vestry of the small chapel, in a deal box under the bench, and it took two of them to get the lid up because the wood had swollen in four hundred winters.

Bridie had never looked at it. Her father opened it on the table by the lamp, and the first thing she noticed was that the paper was warm, which meant the box was against the wall, which meant the wall was the outside wall of the chapel, which was the same wall as the back of the hearth-house.

Then she noticed the hand.

The first entry was in a copperplate that had gone brown with age. The second was a different copperplate, a little worse. The third was a decent round hand, and then a bad one, and then a bad one with a spelling mistake in it, and so on down the book, four hundred years of it, every generation a new hand taking up the same pen.

And every entry said the same thing.

Wood: nine loads. Water: carried. Fire: kept. Nothing required.

Down the page, and down the next page, and the next, in nine hundred hands that had all been told what to write. Nothing required. Nothing required. Nothing required.

The levy book was in the same box and thinner, and Bridie had carried that one for nine years and knew every page of it. Four hundred and six names, going back to the first year, every household in Ormsby in it and in it since before anybody could remember. Ten pounds here, a day’s cutting there, a load of peat against the door, and along the bottom, against the firekeeper’s column, four hundred and six years of a man paying for the fire out of his own wood.

Bridie found the paymaster line on the second leaf of the first year, printed there in the same copperplate as the heading, because whoever made the book in 1613 had thought somebody might want to know.

Paymaster: and then a ruled line, and nothing on it, and nothing written on the line in any of the four hundred years below it.

The woman, who had read over Bridie’s shoulder, sat back on the bench.

“Eleven days,” she said. “That is the whole of it. Eleven days on a road that stops at the cairn, to find out whether it was for anything.”

“It is not,” said Bridie.

“No. Listen to me, because I have walked a long way and I am the only person in four hundred years who is going to say it out loud.” The woman put one finger on the open page. “Nine years of twelve minutes a day. That is six hundred and fifty-seven hours of your own hands, and the fire has been burning for nobody at all, and there are four hundred names in the levy book, and every one of them has paid for it without once asking what they were paying for.”

The fire popped. The cat came out of the ash and walked across the table and sat on the page, and nobody moved her.

* * *

Ormsby did not want to hear it.

That is not a thing you say out loud about a village, so Bridie will say it another way. On the Sunday, when she asked the vestry for the book, the reeve said that the book was a record of custom and not an instruction. The smith said that fire was fire. Miss Tranter said that if the fire stopped then two hundred children had nowhere to learn to be careful, and she was the one who would have to tell their parents, and by the time she had finished, the meeting was shouting.

Somebody said burn it. Somebody else said the book had kept the fire funded for four hundred years and that was worth a lot more than knowing why, and Reeve Hains agreed with him loudly, and then went outside and stood in the snow.

Only her father said nothing, and afterward, in the woodshed, with the axe in his hands.

“I have never asked either,” he said. “I was given the post at thirty-one and I was glad of it, and my father had it before me, and his father had it. A man can be a coward in a small way for forty years and still be a decent neighbour.”

“Does it matter to you what the fire was for?”

“No,” said Turl. “And that is the trouble with me, and you are the one who has to hold it.”

* * *

Bridie carried the book down to the schoolroom on the following Sunday and put it on the table, and the meeting was called for two o’clock, and the firekeeper was off duty for the first time in his life on a Sunday afternoon.

Forty-one people came, which was every soul in Ormsby except two who were in bed, and Miss Tranter gave up the chair that stood at the front by the wall so that the book could lie on the table where all of them could watch the hand change.

It took ninety minutes to read.

The room got cold the way a room gets cold when nobody is thinking about it. By the eightieth minute the windows had stopped being mirrors. Every time a voice reached the bottom of a page, the person nearest the door turned it over, did the count, and said the number out loud so that the room could carry on. Around the fortieth minute a man called Edwin Rowe fell asleep and was not woken, and his boots squeaked every time he dreamed. Around the sixtieth, somebody at the back laughed once at a spelling mistake in 1688 and was shamed into silence by a woman who did not know him. The hands kept changing. A woman near the window read four entries in one voice and then had to stop, and a schoolboy read the next two in a voice like somebody pulling a tooth.

At the end of it Bridie took the second leaf of the first year and read the paymaster line out, and then read the whole of the ruled space underneath it, which took a while, because there is a limit to how long a person can read nothing at a time.

Then somebody noticed, because the room had gone the colour of the inside of a bucket, and the window had stopped being a mirror.

The hearth-house is a hundred and forty paces from the schoolroom, and nobody heard anything at all, because a fire going out in an open hearth makes no noise at all.

* * *

The snow did not come in. The water did not rise. Nothing terrible happened, then or afterwards, and there was a boy of nine in the third row who had been looking at the door for some minutes, and who now got up, and pulled his coat on over his school bag, and said to nobody in particular that the fire was cold, and that he did not want it to be cold.

He went out into the snow and came back eleven minutes later with an armful of birch and a hatchet he had taken from the school porch, and set it down, and sat back down, and said, “We do the twelve minutes, then.”
