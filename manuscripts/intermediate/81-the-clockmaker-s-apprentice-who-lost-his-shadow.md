# The Clockmaker's Apprentice Who Lost His Shadow

![Cover art: The Clockmaker's Apprentice Who Lost His Shadow](../art/intermediate/81-the-clockmaker-s-apprentice-who-lost-his-shadow.webp)

At nine o’clock on a Tuesday in February, Ferro looked down and found his shadow in the wrong place.

It was not the wrong shape. It was the wrong where. It lay across the bench, and the bench was east of the door, and the light came in over the skylight, so his shadow belonged at his feet, pointing north with the rest of him.

“It’s moved,” he said.

Master Ilves did not look up from a train he was cleaning with a piece of boxwood. “Which.”

“The bench. It’s on the bench.”

“That is where the light is not.”

“The light is over me.”

Ilves set the train down, took out his glasses, and looked at the bench for a while. The frost had been on the skylight since dawn and had not gone. The workshop smelled of brass filings, oil and cold.

“No,” Ilves said. “It is not on the bench.”

At nine fifteen Ferro was holding a wheel in the vice and he had no shadow at all. Not a thin one. Not a grey one. He put the wheel down and turned all the way round, twice, and the floorboards were there and the vice was there, and there was nothing behind him making a shape of him.

By nine twenty he was out in Fennel Street in his shirtsleeves, and the whole street had shadows, long and blue and everybody’s, moving along the gutter with them, and his own place in the middle of the pavement was empty, like a chair.

* * *

The Assay Office was two rooms above a candle shop on Fennel Street, and Ferro had been in it twice in his life, both times with Master Ilves, both times for a licence.

The stairs went up crooked. The first room had a stove, two desks, a bench, and nine people on the bench. The second room had a counter with a grille in it and a woman behind the counter who was maybe thirty and had ink on the side of her right hand.

“I want to register a shadow,” Ferro said.

“That is not a service we offer, I am afraid. Registration is what we do. It is the same word.”

“The same word?”

“You bring us a shadow and a bearer and a trade, and we put it in the book, and it is yours until you are twenty-one or you are apprenticed elsewhere, whichever is earlier.” She turned the ledger to face her. “Name.”

He gave his name and the row and the trade, and her finger went down the column and stopped. Ferro, apprentice, clockmaker, entered on the eleventh of March. Fee of sixpence. There was no receipt, but the fee was in the margin in the clerk’s hand, and so the apprentice’s shadow was registered.

“It was registered three years ago.”

“It was registered three years ago and it has not been in your possession since, according to this.” She turned the page round and pushed it through the grille. “Loose, from the eleventh of March. Returned to the street. No bearer, no trade. Loose shadows are held at the office’s expense, and when one has been loose two years running we attach it to a trade that has asked for hands.”

“Attached to who?”

“That is Form Nine. I can give you a copy.”

Nobody had ever asked for a copy of Form Nine. Ferro had a word in his ear and a hole in his day and the whole of it was printed on a piece of buff paper, and there was a date in the margin three weeks old and a trade against it.

Chandler. He read it twice.

* * *

Halda Rook’s shop was on Chandler’s Row and it was the warmest place Ferro had ever stood in.

Tallow and hot wax and the coal fire under the copper, and the whole back wall given over to sticks laid in rows like organ pipes, and a girl of nine named Pim eating wax on the counting board, and a bell on the door that had been broken since before Ferro was born and so did not ring.

The shadow was at the far end, in the corner by the dipping frame, working.

That was the word for it. It was drawing wax off a rod with a knife, at exactly the right thickness, and laying the lengths in a row without a single one crooked, and it moved the way he moved, except that it had somewhere to be.

Ferro stood in the doorway with the bell not ringing.

The shadow had not turned round. It said Ferro had taken six weeks, and that it had been three weeks a probationer and three signed, and that there was a certificate on the wall with a number and a date on it, framed, because somebody in the house thought it was a good thing.

“My bearer does not own you,” said Halda Rook from behind a candle with a wick in it. “Master Rook and I found you in the street at six weeks old and nobody came. The office sent me a letter. It said you were an unattached shadow of no trade and asked whether I had a vacancy. It did not say there was a boy.”

Ferro’s ears had gone hot. He could hear the whole room, the fire, the girl chewing, the wax settling in the copper, and under all of it the flat patient tick of a clock on the far wall, a good regulator, keeping time to a quarter of a second.

“Come home,” he said.

The shadow stopped with the knife up.

“I am better at this than you are at clockmaking,” it said. “You cannot true a verge without taking the pivot out twice. I have made two hundred and forty candles since January and not one of them is a fool. I have a roof over me, a name in a book, and a girl who talks to me all day and wants nothing.”

Ferro had no answer, and the room went on making noise around him, and he walked out into Chandler’s Row with the buff paper in his fist and the cold coming off the roofs.

* * *

“The examination is in April. You have to be registered. They put you in a cell with a clock and a candle, and the marker will not sign the certificate without a registered shadow, because the mark has to be a shadow.”

Master Ilves put down a file. He was sixty-one and had been in that workshop since he was nine, and he turned a thing over in his hands twice before he said anything about it.

“I know.”

“You knew.”

“I assumed the street had it. In forty years I have had two apprentices and one of them died in the army, and neither ever said a word to me about what the light was doing.”

“Three years.”

“Three years,” Ilves agreed, “of you not looking down.”

The regulator on the wall was eleven seconds fast. Ferro could hear it from the doorway, a thin wrongness under the fire, and he had been hearing it wrong for a fortnight, and it was not the clock that was wrong.

“I will go and get the certificate off Halda,” Ilves said. “And then we will take it to the office and pay the fee and put it back where it was.”

“No,” said Ferro.

* * *

The queue on the stair was nine deep on the Thursday and Ferro made it eleven by pushing in at the back of it.

“You should sit down,” said Ivet Sallow, when he got there. “Nobody has ever been angry in here and there is no need to start.”

“I want to see the book.”

“You may look at the book. Everyone may look at the book. That is the whole point of a book.”

The loose ledger was on a lectern by the stove. Ferro turned back through her neat columns, past the twenties, past a page corner marked in ink where a flood had got at it, into the fifties.

He read four pages of it and then he sat down.

Fifty-one years of loose shadows, unattached, held on the street, and every one of them attached to a trade that had asked for hands. A wheelwright, a dyer, two to a chandler on this very row, a cooper, a woman who took in washing. The hand did not change and it did not hurry, the fees were all marked paid, and against every fifth entry in the margin was the same small note in the same hand: held, not claimed.

“They are not corrupt in there,” Ferro said, half to himself.

“No,” said Ivet Sallow. “We are two of us, we are on a stair, and in the winter the stove goes out, and before your time we had four clerks and a registrar. We hold the loose ones on the street because the alternative is a third of this city walking about in the dark, and we have never had a single case brought to us by anybody, because everybody who lost a shadow assumed they were the only one.”

Nobody said anything for a little while. The stove ticked. The girl with ink on her hand turned a page over and squared it against the desk.

“Was there a fee,” Ferro said. “When I was entered.”

“Sixpence. It was paid. The margin is in the clerk’s hand, and the clerk died in the September of your first year, and we do not go looking for a certificate that has been paid for and not collected.” She looked at him steadily. “You are not angry at the office.”

“No.”

“You are angry at a man who died.”

“No,” said Ferro, and then, because it was true and because it was worse, “I am angry that it was sixpence.”

* * *

Form Fourteen had not been filled in in the whole of the printed history of the office, and Ivet Sallow had to send a boy down to the printer for a clean sheet.

Voluntary surrender of a registered shadow. Bearer. Registered number. Statement of consent. Witness.

Ferro filled it in on the counter with the pen held badly, because his right hand wanted to be holding a pair of pliers.

The last line of the form, in a box an inch high, asked what the bearer proposed to do without one.

He read it three times. It was not a form of words. It was a real question, and somebody had thought to print it, and that meant somebody had sat at that counter without an answer.

“I don’t know,” Ferro said. “That is why I am signing it.”

Ivet Sallow read the box, and read it again, and did not write anything in the margin, which was the first time in his life that he had seen her leave one empty.

“Witness,” she said.

Master Ilves came up the crooked stair as far as the top step, and would not come further, and put his hand on the rail.

“That’ll do,” he said.

She stamped it. The stamp made a noise like a plate of ice going down a step, the ink was the colour of a bruise, and the certificate came out pale green, and the office gave it to the chandler and not to the man who had signed for it.

* * *

By the following Tuesday there were forty-one people on the stair.

They were not all there to surrender. Two had come to reclaim, and one of those had a shadow reattached to a greengrocer, who said it was now insolent. Most had come to look at the book, because in eleven days it had gone round the workshops that a boy in Chandler’s Row had given his shadow away on purpose and got nothing for it.

The second room had been cleared of ledgers. There was a second stove in the passage, and a boy from the printer bringing up more Form Fourteens, and Ivet Sallow had tied her sleeves back and was asking, for the first one, the second one and the third one in turn: what does the bearer propose to do without one.

Ferro sat on the bench with his coat on and answered for nobody and listened to thirty people say I don’t know.

Across the water, on Chandler’s Row, the light came through the shop window at a low angle and fell across the dipping frame and the rows of sticks on the boards, and the shadow at the far end of the shop drew the wax off a rod with a knife at exactly the right thickness, and laid each length in a row without a single one crooked.

There was a certificate on the wall in a wooden frame, with a number and a date on it, and underneath it in Halda Rook’s square capitals somebody had painted ROOK’S, in gold, with the two children’s names either side of it, and the shop bell on the door was still broken, and the shadow was quite happy.
