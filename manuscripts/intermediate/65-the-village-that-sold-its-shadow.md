# The Village That Sold Its Shadow

![Cover art: The Village That Sold Its Shadow](../art/intermediate/65-the-village-that-sold-its-shadow.webp)

The letter came on the fourteenth of February, walked four miles by a boy from Brackmoor who lost the last mile and found the dyke by the sound of a dog.

Maren Roeg was twelve, she was clerk of Ninebanks, and she had written out the deed herself in November, in the second column, where the seller puts her name.

In a fen, winter is not a cold season. It is a grey one. The sun cleared the eastern bank at twenty past eight and went down behind the alder beds at ten past four, and in between it did very little but be visible. The sedge stood in its own water and the water would not freeze, because moving water does not. The reeds made a noise all day like a hand going slowly through a coat. People slept four hours a night and had been doing it since November.

Eleven winters ago the village had sold its shadow.

They had done it quickly, with a lawyer out of Brackmoor, a chest of money, and the money gone into the dyke and the drainage pumps inside a year, because a fen village with no dyke is a marsh with opinions. What the village got in exchange was light. For eleven years, Ninebanks had had all of its day.

Maren knew what the shadow looked like. She had seen it at nine, with her father’s hand flat between her shoulder blades. It lay on the cobbles by the Brackmoor fountain, about the size of a man on his side, and it was the coldest thing she had ever stood near.

The letter was four paragraphs long and it said regard four times.

It said that under the old law, which had never been repealed, a shadow was a person. It said that if the village’s shadow had been living in Brackmoor’s square for eleven years, then the village had been keeping a person in a dark patch. It said the town would compensate generously, and that a cart was already on the road with two hundredweight of lamp oil in it, so that Ninebanks would not have to sit in the dark while the lawyers talked.

Maren read the last line twice. We are sure you will understand, it said, that the cart comes on Tuesday.

* * *

The hall smelled of wet wool and the goodman’s pipe, and eleven people crowded in to look at a piece of paper.

Cobb read the letter aloud twice, because the second reading is where people start agreeing. Then he said a hundred and forty pounds of compensation would roof the whole east side of the lane, open to the weather since the week of the sale.

Merrit the smith stood at the back with his hat in his hands and said the east side could wait, and that his daughter was fourteen and had not slept properly since Christmas.

Father Anselm said it was not a question of money, and Cobb said it was always a question of money, and that the church had said a great many things on the day and the dyke had still needed mending.

Then Aurel stood up.

He did it the way he did everything: suddenly, and without making anybody look. He was the size and roughly the shape of a man lying on his side, and where he went the cobbles stayed wet. He had a face nobody agreed on. Some said it was young. Some said it was Merrit’s second daughter, who went into the canal in the year of the sale, and that the village had known.

“I have been very comfortable,” he said. “I have a bench. I know the fishwives. Mrs Ivery gives me a piece of the day’s bread and she is not obliged to.”

Cobb said that if he wanted to come home, here was the cart.

“It is not my home,” Aurel said. “It is a field with water on it. I am content. Ask anybody.”

Maren had the deed out of the box by then. When a room fills with arguing she can go quiet and get on with it. In the second column was her own writing, small and even, four months old and never once wrong.

A shadow may be conveyed, it said, and the term of a conveyance is eleven years. The conveying is of the shadow and not of the person. The person consents. Consent is given by a mark in the third column.

Under that there was a ruled line for the name of the person, and nothing else. She had the conveyance form under her hand, the great printed sheet with every kind of transfer upon it, to a buyer, to a lender, to a hospital. A hundred and forty boxes. One said yes. None of the rest said anything.

“You wrote it,” said Father Anselm.

“I copied what was put in front of me,” Maren said, and went red.

* * *

On Tuesday the cart came, and Dunn with it, and he was disappointed. He wanted the oil unloaded, the shadow loaded, and his horses home by dark.

“They come off themselves if you draw a line round them,” Dunn said. “I draw the line, your clerk signs for it, and he walks on if he feels like it. If he does not, I go home empty and you have paid for the oil.”

Dunn had a stub of chalk, and he drew the line on the cobbles of the market square all the way round, the way a man draws round a puddle, and it took him the better part of the afternoon.

“Off you come, then,” Dunn said, when the line was shut.

Aurel did not move.

“He has consented to be fetched,” Dunn said. “That is the law.”

Maren looked at the deed on the fountain, in her own writing. The person consents. There was no line under that for anything else. She turned the sheet over. Blank.

“Right,” she said. “Then he does not come, and you take the oil back to Brackmoor, and Cobb writes a letter about it.”

Dunn put the chalk in his pocket and said that in his trade nobody had ever sent oil back.

Maren signed the second column a second time, which she had never done in her life, in a taller hand than the November one.

* * *

They went at first light, with Aurel: Cobb, Anselm, Dunn and the cart, and Maren on the tailboard with her feet in the straw.

The road ran two miles along the embankment and two across Brackmoor heath. Aurel walked, keeping to the verge, and where the verge was muddy the mud stayed dark.

At the top of the embankment Aurel said, “We had no night either. That is what nobody tells you. There was nothing to be tired from.”

Maren had the deed across her knees and a pencil she was not using. “Aurel. When you said yes, did anybody tell you that you were allowed to say no?”

“No,” he said. “Nobody ever told me that not was a thing I could say.”

She did not ask him again. She put the deed in her satchel and watched the hedges go by, and thought how much easier it is to fill in a thing that has only one place for the answer.

* * *

The market square at Brackmoor had two shadows in it, and the noon bell had not helped.

The old one lay by the fountain where it had lain for eleven years, black as a hole in a coat. The new one lay over the top of it and was wider. Where the two met, the cold came up through the cobbles like water through a boot. The pigeons would not cross. The pair of them leaned a little in the wind, and the leaning was not the same lean.

Mr Pryse, of Pryse and Chattis, Solicitors, waited under the clock with his hat off and a paper umbrella he did not need.

He gave the law in a business voice. No conveyance of a person is good without free consent, given after the person has been told that consent may be withheld. The term is eleven years. The term is up, and his client does not wish to argue the point twice.

Mr Pryse walked to the dry edge of the wet place by the fountain and stood there the way you stand at a gate.

“Eleven years in the open,” he said. “Are you content?”

“I am content,” said Aurel.

“Say it to me, and not to the clerk.”

“I am content,” said Aurel.

“He has been perfectly content,” Cobb said. “He has said so.”

“He has said what he was asked,” said Mr Pryse. “That is not the same instrument, Mr Cobb.”

“You asked him twice,” Maren said. “Both times you asked whether he was content.”

“I did,” said Mr Pryse. “I shall have to write to my client that I did.”

And there was the thing in the middle of the square with eleven years of grey in it, sitting in the cold, listening to two men discuss it in the third person.

* * *

The bargain took until dark and a third of a bottle of Brackmoor’s best.

The town would drop its claim. The village would pay Brackmoor’s lighting, oil and tallow and candle, carted up the embankment road every month of every year for a hundred years, the first load at Candlemas. In exchange, no person in either place would be owned, sold, conveyed, held or claimed, and the instrument that said so would be destroyed at dusk in this square, and neither party would keep a copy.

Cobb said that a village cannot keep a promise for a hundred years, and Anselm said that it can, and that this one would have to.

Mr Pryse said he was not a notary and the market square was not a court, and Maren Roeg, clerk of Ninebanks, said it was the seat of her parish, and got out her pen.

The deed went into the fountain basin page by page and burned black at the edges, and Cobb’s hand shook on the last one, and nothing was said about it. Anselm’s copy went in after. Pryse had Maren write him a receipt, and then held it back, because a receipt for a burnt thing has no name at the top of it, and put that in his pocket instead.

Aurel had asked for the bench to be carried over from the wall.

Afterwards both villages walked out to look at the wet patch by the fountain, because there was nothing else to look at, and it was only a wet patch. Mrs Ivery carried him a piece of bread and stood over him while he ate it, because that was the arrangement.

“You are the wrong shape for this square now,” she said. “You never fitted it.”

“No,” Aurel said. “But I know the fishwives.”

Dunn got his horses home at ten, four hours behind his plan, and did not grumble.

Maren has the old conveyance form in her satchel. She has ruled a new line into it, under the third column, in the same hand as the rest, and it says no, with room to write over the answer if somebody changes their mind. Cobb says it is not proper. She says it is proper now.

The first cartload of oil came up the embankment road in March. It came every month after that, for a hundred years. The lane was never dark, and the sundial on the churchyard wall stayed a plain round stone, because a sundial wants a shadow, and Ninebanks had arranged to have none.
