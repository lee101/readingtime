# The Starship Lighthouse of the Long Winter

![Cover art: The Starship Lighthouse of the Long Winter](../art/intermediate/91-the-starship-lighthouse-of-the-long-winter.webp)

The tender came out on the last Tuesday of September with a crate of lamp oil, two gas bottles, a sack of coal, a fortnight of mail, and a woman called Adisa Lark who had been on the boats before but had never been put ashore at Cape Vessel.

The cliff road went in eleven hundred metres from the slipway to the tower, and Adisa walked all of it twice before she went in, because the first time was for the view and the second time was to count the steps.

Twenty-two years old. Last keeper of Cape Vessel, if the Board kept its word about the closure, which was the sort of thing a Board said in a letter and repeated in a newsletter.

The tower was black basalt and white lime, eleven days’ walking from anywhere, and the whole of the third floor was the lamp room, with a drum of glass three metres round in the middle of it that you could not touch and could not see out of at any angle at all.

The rotation weight came down the inside wall on a chain, and when the chain was all the way out the light stopped turning, and you wound it up with a crank every two hours, all night, all winter, forever.

“That is the whole job,” said the previous keeper, waiting by the door with her bag already packed. “Two hours, and then again. The lens is heavier than the both of us and it does not care.”

“Stormy?”

“Stormy, gales, and the dark comes in from three directions,” said Mrs Ivell. “I have done eleven winters. You will not be here for the twelfth, and I am sorry about that.”

She left at four. Adisa watched the boat go and then went up and put the log on the desk and opened it at the last page and read backwards.

11 Nov — gales wly, sea breaking over the west gantry, all hands to the stores, visibility nil, lamp burning.

3 Nov — first ice on the gallery, cleared.

21 Oct — stores landed, coal 4 tons, oil 2 drums, visitor, Mrs Lark of ...

Adisa turned back further, and then further, and then stopped.

The Board's log for Cape Vessel had been kept in the same hand for two hundred years, and every eighty-one years, in the middle of a winter, somebody had written the same four lines.

7 Dec. F/81. Lamp extinction, cause not established. Spectrum normal, mantle sound, no obstruction, no damage. Dark not empty. No fault found. Light re-established 4 Feb.

7 Dec. F/81. Lamp extinction, cause not established.

6 Dec. F/81. Lamp extinction, cause not established.

The dates moved. The entry never moved. Two hundred years of it, back to the first keeper, and not one word of it in any hand but the clerk's.

* * *

The drawer of the desk had a false bottom, and under the false bottom there was a note, and the note said:

I have been keeping the interval. I am not going to pretend to you that I do not know what it is. It comes at eighty-one years, it comes in the first week of December, and it takes the light for the whole winter and puts it back in the spring, and it has never given me a reason I could write down.

There is another book. It is not this book. It is nine generations of the family who kept this light before me, and the family called the thing out there THE ANSWER, and the family has said the same words at the lamp every winter for two hundred years, and the words are: SOMEBODY IS OUT THERE.

I never said them until my second winter. Mrs Ivell said them with me, and her predecessor said them with her, and I have said them into that lamp every night for nine years, and every woman who did it before me said the same, and I want you to understand that I have stopped.

It is not a duty. It is a conversation, and it has been one-sided for nine generations, and the last entry in the family book is from a woman who did not survive the winter she is describing.

Do not wind the clock for the men who wrote F/81 and go to bed. I am sorry to write this to you. Wind it, and be in the lamp room, and be there all night.

— E. Ivell, keeper, Cape Vessel.

Adisa read it twice. Then she put it back under the false bottom and shut the drawer, and sat in a lighthouse on the first evening of her last posting with the chain already needing a wind.

* * *

The old woman came ashore on the last boat of the year, in a coat with fur on the collar, in a January that had not been forecast and did not explain itself.

She was eighty-one years old and she walked the eleven hundred metres of cliff road in the wind without being helped, and when she got to the door she stood in the doorway looking at the stairs for a while.

“Vera Lark,” she said. “My mother was Nesta Lark. She was the keeper here in the winter of the last extinction, and I was born in this tower in the February, and the Board has had eighty-one years to ask me something and has never asked me one question.”

“Come in. It is minus four on the gallery.”

“I know what it is on the gallery. I was born in that number.” She came in and took off her gloves one finger at a time. “You have read the note. I can see it on you. Mrs Ivell wrote to my mother and my mother never wrote back, and I have the letter, and the letter is the reason I know about you.”

“What does it say?”

“That I was to be told if the winter came round again,” said Vera Lark, “and that I should bring the book, and that I should not bring the book unless somebody asked me for it.”

“There is a book,” Adisa said.

“There is a book. There have been nine names in it and there is a tenth line ruled and empty, and it has been in a bread tin in a house in Mullion for eighty-one years, and it is not in my car.”

* * *

It came in on the seventh of December, in the middle of the afternoon, which was the part Adisa would keep.

The day had been flat and grey and cold with a long swell from the south-west and no wind at all, and at twenty past two the burner went out without a noise, and the lens carried on turning for four minutes on the weight, and then the chain was all the way out and the light stopped, and the glass drum above Cape Vessel went from grey to black in under a second, the way a light goes out in a room.

The relief boat was three weeks away and the nearest rescue station was four hours down the cliff road and the road was under a metre of ice.

Adisa wound the clock. That was the job, and she did it, and the lens turned in the dark and threw nothing anywhere.

The dark was not empty.

That is the phrase in the Board’s own report, in the clerk’s hand, two hundred years old and repeated, and by midnight Adisa understood that the clerk had not meant it as a figure of speech. Something was there, and it was not a shape, because shapes move and this did not. It was a weight in the air off the north side of the gallery, at the height of the rail, out at the distance the light used to reach.

It did not come in. It did not go out. It was simply there, the way a held note is there, and the sea went on doing what the sea does, and the lens went on turning, and at about two in the morning she said the words.

Somebody is out there.

She said them because the previous keeper’s note said they were the words, and because she was twenty-two and alone and there was nothing else to say into a lamp room with a dead lamp in it. She said them four or five times, and then sat down on the floor with her back against the cold drum.

Vera Lark was asleep on the camp bed in the oil store, and came up at four, and found Adisa on the floor.

“You said them,” she said.

“Yes.”

“I heard you through the floor. It carries in this building. It has carried for two hundred years, that is what a lighthouse is, a thing built so that a voice carries out of it.”

“I know.”

“My mother said them for one winter only, the winter you are having now, and she wrote down what it felt like.” Vera Lark sat down on the step with her back to the drum. “Do you know why this happens, or have you decided not to ask?”

“No.”

“The answer is not coming to take your light,” said Vera Lark. “The answer is coming because nine generations of your family have stood at that lamp and promised it, out loud, into a dark, that there is somebody out there, and the dark believed them, and the belief has weight, and the weight is what the light is made of. Every year the promise gets heavier. Every eighty-one years the promise is heavier than the lamp, and the lamp goes out.”

Adisa sat with that for a while. The chain needed winding and she did not wind it.

“Then what do I do?” she said.

“That is your end of it,” said Vera Lark, “and I have carried the other end of it for eighty-one years.”

* * *

The Board came in on the first real weather, in February, with three days’ warning and a broken gantry, and put two mechanics and Doss the boatmaster onto the rocks in a barge, and Adisa sent up her report of no fault found, and the Board wrote F/81 again because that is what the form said.

The barge went out at first light with a letter in Vera Lark’s hand, and the light was still out, and the tower was still black against a black sea, and Adisa watched it from the slipway until it was a dot.

“Two hundred years,” Doss said, pulling the throttle. “Every eighty-one. And now it ends with a girl of twenty-two and a postmistress from Mullion.”

“It ends with a girl of twenty-two,” Adisa said. “My name is Adisa.”

* * *

Vera Lark put the book on the desk on the last morning in March, when the ice had gone off the cliff road and the boat was two hours out and the light was still out.

It was a school exercise book with a marbled cover and nine lines written in it in nine hands, one a generation, and each line said the same thing in a different spelling.

Somebody is out there. Somebody is out there. Somebody is out there.

The ninth line was in a small square hand.

Fifteenth of January. Sixty-one winters of it, and the words get heavier every year and tonight they will not lift. I am going out onto the gallery to see what has been listening. If the entry stops here, then the entry stops here. N. Lark, keeper, Cape Vessel.

And a tenth line, ruled, and empty, and dated that morning in Vera Lark's writing.

Adisa read it twice. Then she took the book and the lamp-room key and went up the stairs.

* * *

She wound the clock first, out of pure habit, and then she stopped, because a dead lamp does not need turning, and a hundred and nineteen days of the winter were still to go.

She had said the words on the first night and then she had stopped, and the dark had gone on sitting out there exactly where it had always sat, which told her something about the words that she had not wanted to know.

Adisa Lark stood in the middle of the lamp room and opened her mouth and said nothing at all, on purpose, for the first time in her life in front of that lamp.

Then she said: “I am Adisa Lark, and my name is in the tenth line, and I am not promising you anything. Who is out there?”

The dark out past the north gallery took a long moment to come up with it, and it came up with a voice that was not a voice, and what it said was a name.

“Nesta Lark.”

* * *

The light did not come back that winter, and the Board wrote it down as a fault, because the form said fault, and there was nothing else on the form.

The Cape was dark from the seventh of December to the fourth of April. The road was closed. Doss left food at the slipway in a chained locker. Adisa wound the clock every two hours anyway, out of the shape of the work, and read by the lamp she had carried up, and slept four hours at a time.

And when the boiler was lit and the foghorn was back on the mainland, there were still a hundred and nineteen ruled days in Adisa’s hand and not one word entered on any of them, because there is no column on the form for a name.

Vera Lark did not stay for the boat. She left the bread tin on the desk in the tower and went down the eleven hundred metres of cliff road on her own feet, and at the slipway she turned round and looked up at the tower for a long time.

Adisa carried the tin out to the slipway and stood on the stones in the wind with the light still off above her.

Out past the north gallery, where the beam had ended for a hundred and nineteen nights, the shape was still sitting exactly where it had been, and it had not come in, and it was not going to.

It was enormous. It was gentle. It was sitting at the edge of the light with its attention turned toward the tower, the way you sit when someone has finally answered you, and you are not going to spoil it by moving first.
