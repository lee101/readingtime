# The Monster Under the School Got a Landlord

![Cover art: The Monster Under the School Got a Landlord](../art/intermediate/85-the-monster-under-the-school-got-a-landlord.webp)

The card on corridor three was screwed to the door at Rafi’s height, which meant somebody had once thought about a boy his age.

CORRIDOR 3 IS CLOSED TO ALL PUPILS AT ALL TIMES. DO NOT ENTER. DO NOT KNOCK. DO NOT SPEAK TO ANYTHING YOU CANNOT SEE.

Rafi could see it, usually. Late on a wet Thursday he could see a shape at the far end with its back to the wall and a head like a rolled carpet, and it did not turn round, and he had worked out over four months that this was a decision rather than a rudeness.

“The school is the landlord,” he told the Fair Future Society, which was himself, Bilal, and his cousin Nora, and which met on Tuesdays under the stairs by the bins because the corridor rule is not the only rule. “The monster is a tenant. A tenant who has never once been asked for rent is a tenant being exploited, and the correct action is organised.”

“The correct action is organised what?” said Bilal.

“The correct action is a meeting.”

* * *

He wrote the notice out on Wednesday and posted it on eleven doors, including the caretaker’s.

TENANTS’ MEETING. TUESDAY, 6.40 P.M. UNDER THE SCHOOL. ALL RESIDENTS WELCOME. CHAIR: R. ABDULLAH (AGE 10).

Mrs Ncube caught him at the top of corridor three at lunchtime on Thursday with the notice still folded in his hand.

“Corridor three is closed to all pupils at all times.”

“Yes, Miss.”

“All times. There is no hour of the day that is not all times.”

“And underneath the school after four o’clock is prohibited, too, and the prohibition is older than the corridor, which is interesting.”

He was braced for it to be confiscated, and she did not confiscate it, which unsettled him more.

“Mrs Ncube, you are the landlord.”

“I am the headteacher of this school, which is a public building, which is a corporation sole, which means there is nobody at all at the top of it holding the deeds and everybody in this village thinks I do.” She took the notice and read it the way you read something in a language you have. “I am not coming to a meeting at twenty to seven under a building I am trying to sell.”

“Why are you trying to sell it?”

“Because the roof cost forty-one thousand pounds in March and my hallway has a bucket under the stain in the corner,” said Mrs Ncube, “and the second letter from the building society is in my bag, and I have read it the way you read a diagnosis.”

* * *

He put the chairs out at twenty to seven, twelve of them from the hall, and a trestle table, and a jug of water, and he had written an agenda in a hard hand that went crooked on the third line.

Mrs Ncube came down at seven past seven with her coat on and a folder under her arm, and Gerald came up the stairs at eight past, because he would not use the stairs while anyone was on them.

He was about the size of a piano and a little the shape of one, pale grey, with a collar he did not need and a fountain pen in a coat pocket. He sat down, adjusted the collar, and said, “I have brought my lease.”

Then he handed Rafi four sheets of paper, stapled, in order, with a contents page.

“Basement one, sub-lease of the land granted by the Board of Education in 1904, renewed 1978, unrenewed 1991, held continuously, no rent paid, no rent demanded, thirty-one complaints lodged with the governors between 1906 and 1989, all answered.” He looked up. “I am the oldest tenancy in the borough. You are eleven minutes late, Mr Abdullah.”

“My name is Rafi.”

“I have it. You spelled it wrong on the notice, and I have not corrected it, because minutes record what was said.”

Mrs Ncube sat down, opened her folder, and said, “You have kept a ledger.”

“I have kept a ledger since 1911, when the school put in a form,” said Gerald. “Nobody has ever asked me for it. I have offered it eleven times.”

Within ten minutes they were arguing about the corridor rule, and they got on so completely that Rafi had to sit on his own agenda for a minute while they worked out that the rule had no date on it, no minute number, and no signature at the bottom, and that in 1979 it had been typed by a caretaker who had also typed a fire notice with a mistake in the address, and that both had been screwed up together on the same card.

“He is right,” Mrs Ncube said, to Rafi. “It is unenforceable. I have never once enforced it and I have never been asked to.”

“I have been asked to,” said Gerald. “By a child, weekly, for four months, and it is the only enforcement I have ever had, and I found it restful.”

* * *

The agenda had eleven items and they got to item one.

“Rafi,” said Mrs Ncube. “What is this meeting for?”

“The hall ceiling.”

The two of them looked at each other, and Rafi discovered he had lost the thread, and that both of them were far more interested than he was.

The hall ceiling had eleven damp patches. Rafi had counted them in September. They made a shape like a hand, splayed, the biggest one the size of a dinner plate, over the part of the floor where the choir stood.

“They are not structural,” said Mrs Ncube. “I have had the roof surveyed twice. There is nothing wrong with the roof.”

“There is a slab above the hall,” said Gerald, “which fell in 1961, and the fall is in a report I have read, and the report was sent to the governors and not to the caretaker, and the caretaker retired in 1984, and the next caretaker came from the plumber’s firm and did not open the box.”

Mrs Ncube put her folder on the table and squared it. “Then who is responsible?”

“Thirteen years ago you wrote to my landlord saying the damp was not mine,” she said. “It is in here. Dated the fourth of March. You said, and I am quoting, the source of the moisture is external to your tenancy.”

Gerald took out his ledger, found a page, and read from it without any expression at all. “On the ninth of March you replied that the source of the moisture was an internal defect, and you attached a survey which says the waterproofing to the stack is sound.”

“That is you. That is your letter.”

“It is my letter, but it is also a letter from 1972,” said Gerald, “which I have here, in which you accuse me of the same thing, which is why the damp is still there.”

The room went quiet in the way rooms do.

“Thirty years,” said Mrs Ncube.

“Thirty years,” said Gerald. “I have measured it. One of the eleven has not changed size since 1981. Two of them have not moved since 1974.”

“It is the girls’ changing room,” said Rafi.

* * *

It was the girls’ changing room. The downpipe from the roof of the west range had cracked in 1991 and had been mended with tape, and the tape went, and the water came into a ceiling void above the hall and sat there because the slab had fallen in 1961 and made a low place, and a low place with water in it is a low place that keeps it.

Nobody had ever asked the monster.

They did the joint inspection on the Monday, all three, with the caretaker and a torch, and Rafi had never felt so much like a man filling out a form in his life. The report went in on the Friday. The invoice came back on the fourteenth: a dehumidifier, three hundred and forty pounds, lead time six weeks, recommended in the first instance. Rafi had wanted a revolution. What he got instead was a joint inspection, an invoice for a dehumidifier that his mother had to phone the building society about on a Sunday, and a monster who was better at paperwork than he was by a distance of about nine hundred years, and who was also the only tenant in the borough who knew the address of every drain within a mile of the school.

The dehumidifier arrived in April and the first patch on the ceiling stopped growing. That was the visible victory and Rafi was disappointed by it.

Gerald started going up at night.

Mrs Ncube found out from the caretaker, and Rafi found out from Mrs Ncube, and neither of them told the school. Twice a week, after the last club, Gerald went up into the void above the hall with a lamp and a length of hose and a bucket, and he moved the water out of the low place and down the pipe, and he did it without asking anybody for anything, which was the thing Rafi could not get past.

“Nobody had thought to ask you,” he said, on a Thursday, in the plant room, when Gerald came down wet to the knee.

“No,” said Gerald. “Nobody has ever asked me for anything, and there was a time when I took that as rudeness, and I want to say to you, young chairman, that it was not rudeness. It was that nobody had ever asked a tenant a question. They had only ever issued a rule at him.”

* * *

Gerald remains.

The rent is four pence a week, and Rafi is treasurer, and the collection is by crumb, one crumb being the minimum, and the ledger says so, and Gerald signs for every week in a hand that is not a monster’s hand, and has never once missed a week, and has never once asked what the crumb was for.

The eleven damp patches went from eleven to four to none over the summer, and the hall ceiling is a ceiling now rather than a document.

The corridor rule was reprinted in September, and the office supplied the same card, and Mrs Ncube put it up herself with a screwdriver she kept in her desk for a purpose she has never explained. It is screwed to the door at Rafi’s height, which means somebody still thinks about a boy his age.

The new card is three lines shorter by one. The wording is the same, the typeface is the same, the screws are in the same four holes, and the third line has been taken off it with a blade, so that what it now says is this.

CORRIDOR 3 IS CLOSED TO ALL PUPILS AT ALL TIMES. DO NOT ENTER. DO NOT KNOCK.
